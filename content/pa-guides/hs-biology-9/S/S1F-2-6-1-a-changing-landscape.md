**The Human** **Effect**

- Humans alter ecosystems through three primary, interconnected activities: **Agriculture, Development (Urban/Suburban Sprawl), and Industrial** **Growth.**
- **Agriculture:** Enables large, settled populations but can degrade soil and water. **Monoculture** is practice of clearing large areas of land to plant a single highly productive crop.
- **Development:** Divides habitats into fragments, consumes farmland, and concentrates waste.
- **Industry:** Provides modern conveniences but consumes vast energy (mostly from fossil fuels) and historically released pollution directly into air, water, and soil.

**Sustainable Development**

- **Core Idea:** **Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.**
- **Relies on understanding resources:**
  - **Renewable Resource:** Can be replenished by healthy ecosystems within a human timeframe (e.g., timber, fresh water, wind energy). Can become nonrenewable if used faster than replenished.
  - **Nonrenewable Resource:** Cannot be replenished in a practical timeframe (e.g., **fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas)**, minerals).
- **Principles of Sustainable Development:**
  - **Must not cause long-term harm** to soil, water, air, or climate.
  - Must **conserve energy and materials**.
  - Must be **resilient** to environmental stresses (drought, floods).
  - Must balance **human economic systems** with **ecosystem goods and services**.

**Ecosystem Goods and Services**

- The **benefits healthy ecosystems provide to humanity, often for free.** Examples include:
  - **Goods:** Food, timber, pharmaceuticals, fresh water.
  - **Services:** Purification of air and water, decomposition of waste, pollination of crops, climate regulation, flood control, nutrient cycling, aesthetic/cultural value.
- **When ecosystems are degraded, society must pay to replace these services** (e.g., building water filtration plants, manually pollinating crops).

## 6.2 Using Resources Wisely

**1. Soil Resources**

- **Importance:** The foundation of terrestrial life. Healthy **topsoil** is a living, complex mix of minerals, organic matter, water, and air. It takes **centuries to form** but can be lost in a season.
- **Threats:**
  - **Erosion:** The removal of soil by wind or water. Worsened by plowing, overgrazing, and deforestation.
  - **Desertification:** The combination of farming, overgrazing, drought, and climate change turning fertile land into desert (e.g., U.S. Dust Bowl of the 1930s).
  - **Deforestation:** Loss of forests, especially old-growth forests (nonrenewable on human timescales). Leads to severe erosion, loss of habitat, and disruption of the water cycle.
- **Sustainable Solutions:**
  - **Contour Plowing:** Plowing across a slope to slow water runoff.
  - **Terracing:** Creating level "steps" on steep slopes.
  - **No-Till/Cover Crops:** Leaving old stems/roots or planting cover crops to hold soil.
  - **Crop Rotation:** Planting different crops to prevent nutrient depletion.
  - **Sustainable Forestry:** Selective cutting, tree farms, and protecting old-growth forests.

**2. Freshwater Resources**

- **A Limited Resource:** Only 3% of Earth's water is fresh, and most is locked in ice. Many sources (like aquifers) are being used faster than they are recharged.
- **Pollutant**: Harmful material that enters the biosphere.
- **Water Pollution:**
  - **Point Source:** Pollution from a single, identifiable location (factory pipe, oil spill).
  - **Nonpoint** **Source:** Pollution from many diffuse sources (agricultural runoff, street grease, lawn chemicals). **Much harder to control.**
- **Types of Pollutants:**
  - **Industrial** **Chemicals:** Heavy metals (mercury, lead), **PCBs** (persistent organic pollutants).
  - **Agricultural** **Chemicals:** Pesticides and fertilizers that run off into waterways.
  - **Residential Sewage:** Contains nitrogen/phosphorus (causes algal blooms & **dead zones**) and disease-causing microorganisms.
- **Biological Magnification:**
  - **Definition:** **The increasing concentration of a persistent, non-biodegradable toxin in the tissues of organisms at successively higher trophic levels.**
  - **Mechanism:** The pollutant is absorbed but not broken down or excreted. Each consumer accumulates all the toxin from the many organisms it eats.
  - **Result:** **Top predators (like eagles, osprey, humans) can have toxin concentrations millions of times higher than the water or producers.** Classic example: **DDT** causing thin eggshells in birds of prey.
- **Sustainable Solutions:**
  - **Protect Natural Filters:** Preserve **wetlands** and forests, which purify water.
  - **Watershed-Based Management:** Address pollution across an entire drainage area.
  - **Integrated Pest Management (IPM):** Uses biological controls, crop rotation, and targeted spraying to reduce pesticide reliance.
  - **Water** **Conservation:** e.g., **Drip** **irrigation** delivers water directly to plant roots.

**3.** **Atmospheric** **Resources**

- **Vital Services:** Provides oxygen, contains the **ozone layer** (shields from UV radiation), and maintains climate via the **greenhouse effect**.
- **Air Pollution:**
  - **Smog:** A gray-brown haze of **ground-level ozone** and other chemicals formed by reactions between vehicle/industrial emissions and sunlight. Harms respiratory health.
  - **Acid** **Rain:** Caused by sulfur and nitrogen oxides from burning fossil fuels reacting with water vapor. **Lowers the pH of soil and water,** harming plants, aquatic life, and buildings.
  - **Greenhouse Gases:** Excess **carbon dioxide (CO₂)** and **methane (CH₄)** from burning fuels and agriculture trap more heat, causing **global warming**.
  - **Particulates:** Microscopic solid particles that can cause lung damage.
- **Sustainable Solutions:** Clean Air Act regulations, vehicle emission standards, transitioning to renewable energy, international agreements (e.g., Montreal Protocol for ozone, Paris Agreement for climate).

## 6.3 Biodiversity

**What is Biodiversity?**

- **The total of all the genetically based variation in all organisms in biosphere.**
  - **Ecosystem Diversity:** Variety of habitats, communities, ecological processes.
  - **Species Diversity:** Number of different species in an area or on Earth.
  - **Genetic Diversity:** Sum of all genetic information contained within all individuals of a species. **The raw material for evolution and adaptation.**

**Why is Biodiversity Valuable?**

- **Direct** **Economic** **Value:**
  - **Medicine:** Over 50% of modern drugs are derived from natural compounds (e.g., aspirin from willow bark, digitalis from foxglove).
  - **Agriculture:** Wild relatives of crops provide genes for pest/disease resistance, drought tolerance, and improved yield.
- **Indirect Ecosystem Services:** Biodiversity underpins the ecosystem services we rely on: clean air/water, pollination, fertile soil, climate stability, flood control.
- **Ecological** **Resilience:** Diverse ecosystems are generally more stable and productive, and better able to recover from disturbance (e.g., drought, fire).

**Threats to Biodiversity (The HIPPCO Acronym)**

- **H**abitat Loss & **Fragmentation:** The #1 threat. Turning large, continuous habitats into small, isolated **"habitat islands."** Small fragments support fewer species and smaller, more vulnerable populations.
- **I**nvasive Species: Non-native organisms that outcompete, prey upon, or bring disease to native species (e.g., zebra mussels, leafy spurge).
- **P**ollution: Chemicals (DDT, PCBs, mercury), nutrients (sewage), and plastics harm species directly or via food webs (biological magnification).
- **P**opulation Growth (Human): More people = greater demand for resources, accelerating all other threats.
- **C**limate Change: Forces species to shift their ranges; many cannot adapt or migrate fast enough, especially in fragmented habitats.
- **O**verharvesting/Overexploitation: Hunting, fishing, or collecting species faster than they can reproduce (e.g., passenger pigeon, Atlantic cod, illegal wildlife trade for pets/medicines).

**Conserving Biodiversity**

- **Protecting Individual Species:**
  - **Laws:** Endangered Species Act (U.S.), CITES treaty (bans international trade in endangered species).
  - **Captive Breeding & Reintroduction:** **Species Survival Plans (SSPs)** in zoos/aquariums aim to maintain genetic diversity and rebuild wild populations (e.g., golden lion tamarin, black-footed ferret).
- **Preserving Habitats & Ecosystems:**
  - The most effective long-term strategy. Involves creating national parks, wildlife refuges, and marine protected areas.
  - **Ecological** **Hot** **Spots:** Relatively small areas with exceptionally high species diversity and high threat levels. Concentrating conservation efforts here protects a large number of species per dollar spent (e.g., Madagascar, tropical Andes).
- **Considering Local Interests:** For conservation to succeed long-term, it must benefit local people.
  - **Ecotourism:** Provides income.
  - **Debt-for-Nature Swaps & Payments for Ecosystem Services:** Compensate communities for preserving ecosystems.
  - **Sustainable Resource Use:** Promoting practices like shade-grown coffee or certified sustainable timber.

## 6.4 Meeting Ecological Challenges

**The Ecological Footprint**

- **Definition:** A measure of the human demand on Earth's ecosystems. It calculates the total **area of productive land and water required to produce all the resources an individual/population consumes and to absorb all the waste they generate.**
- **Key Insight:** It shows that if everyone lived like the average American, we would need **4-5 Earths.** The U.S. footprint is over twice the world average.
- **Limitations:** It's a **model and simplification**, but excellent for **comparing lifestyles and nations** and visualizing unsustainable consumption.

**A Framework for Solutions**

Ecology guides us toward sustainability through a three-step process:

- **Recognize** **a** **Problem** through observation and monitoring.
- **Research the Cause** through scientific investigation.
- **Change Behavior** by using that scientific understanding to inform policy, technology, and personal choices.

**Case Studies in Applying the Framework**

- **Atmospheric Ozone Depletion: A SUCCESS STORY.**
  - **Problem Recognized (1970s):** Satellite data showed a thinning **"ozone hole"** over Antarctica.
  - **Cause Researched:** Work by Molina, Rowland, and Crutzen proved **CFCs** (chlorofluorocarbons) were destroying ozone.
  - **Behavior Changed:** Global **Montreal** **Protocol** **(1987)** phased out CFCs. The ozone layer is now slowly recovering. **Proof that global cooperation on environmental issues is possible.**
- **North Atlantic Fisheries Collapse: A WORK IN PROGRESS.**
  - **Problem Recognized:** Catches of cod and other fish plummeted despite more intense fishing effort.
  - **Cause Researched:** **Overfishing**—fish were being harvested faster than they could reproduce.
  - **Behavior Changed (Partial):** Quotas, closed seasons/areas, and gear restrictions were implemented. **Aquaculture** (fish farming) provides an alternative but must be managed carefully to avoid pollution and disease. **Recovery is slow and complicated by economic pressures and international competition.**
- **Global Climate Change: THE DEFINITIVE CHALLENGE.**
  - **Problem Recognized:** **Global warming** documented by rising temperatures, melting ice, rising sea levels, and biological changes (e.g., earlier spring phenology).
  - **Cause Researched:** Overwhelming scientific consensus points to increased **greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄)** from human activities (**burning fossil fuels & deforestation**) as the primary driver.
  - **Future Projections:** IPCC models predict significant warming (1.5-6.4°C by 2100), leading to more extreme weather, sea-level rise, shifts in agriculture, and massive biodiversity loss.
  - **Behavior Change Needed:** A global transition from fossil fuels to **renewable energy (solar, wind, geothermal)**, massive increases in energy efficiency, protection of forests, and technological innovation (carbon capture). This requires unprecedented international political and economic cooperation.
