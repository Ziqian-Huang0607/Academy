**Primary Producers (Autotrophs)**

- Autotrophs use solar or chemical energy to produce their own "food" by converting inorganic compounds into complex organic molecules (like carbohydrates).
- They are called **primary producers** because they store energy in forms that become available to other organisms. They are the foundation of all ecosystems.
- **Photosynthesis**: Captures light energy to power reactions that convert CO₂ and water into oxygen and energy-rich carbohydrates. (Plants, algae, some bacteria).
- **Chemosynthesis**: Uses chemical energy (from hydrogen sulfide) to produce carbohydrates. Found in deep-sea vents, hot springs, and other extreme environments.

**Consumers (Heterotrophs)**

- Heterotrophs must **acquire energy** from other organisms by ingesting them.
- Types of Consumers:
  - **Carnivores**: Kill and eat other animals (e.g., snakes, lions).
  - **Scavengers**: Consume dead carcasses of animals killed by predators or that died naturally (e.g., vultures).
  - **Herbivores**: Eat plant leaves, roots, seeds, or fruits (e.g., cows, caterpillars).
  - **Omnivores**: Have diets that naturally include both plants and animals (e.g., humans, bears).
  - **Decomposers**: "Feed" by chemically breaking down organic matter, returning nutrients to the soil (e.g., bacteria, fungi).
  - **Detritivores**: Feed on detritus (small pieces of decaying plant and animal remains) by chewing or grinding it into smaller pieces (e.g., earthworms, snails).

## 3.3 Energy Flow in Ecosystems

**Food Chains and Food Webs**

  - Energy flows through an ecosystem in a **one-way stream**, from primary producers to various consumers.
  - A **food chain** is a series of steps in which organisms transfer energy by eating and being eaten (e.g., algae → small fish → large fish → bird).
  - A **food web** is a network of complex, interconnected feeding relationships formed from all the food chains in an ecosystem. It shows that most organisms eat, and are eaten by, more than one species.
  - Key Concept: **Decomposers and detritivores** are essential as they recycle nutrients back into the ecosystem for use by primary producers.

**Trophic Levels and Ecological Pyramids**

  - Each step in a food chain or web is called a **trophic level**.
    - **First trophic level:** Primary Producers
    - **Second trophic level:** Primary Consumers (Herbivores)
    - **Third trophic level:** Secondary Consumers (Carnivores that eat herbivores)
    - **Fourth trophic level:** Tertiary Consumers (Carnivores that eat other carnivores)
  - **Ecological pyramids** show the relative amount of energy or matter contained within each trophic level.

**Pyramids of Energy:**

    - Show that the amount of energy available decreases at each succeeding trophic level.
    - On average, only about **10%** of the energy from one trophic level is transferred to the next. The rest is used for life processes (respiration, movement) or lost as heat.
    - This 10% rule explains why there are typically fewer organisms at higher trophic levels and why food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels.
  - **Pyramids of Biomass:** Illustrate the relative amount of living organic matter (biomass) at each trophic level.
  - **Pyramids of Numbers:** Show the relative number of individual organisms at each trophic level.

## 3.4 Cycles of Matter

**Biogeochemical Cycles**

  - Unlike energy, matter is recycled within and between ecosystems.
  - Elements pass from one organism to another and through the environment through closed loops called **biogeochemical cycles**.
  - These cycles involve biological, geological, and chemical processes.

**The Water Cycle**

  - Water moves between the oceans, atmosphere, and land.
  - Key Processes:
    - **Evaporation:** Water turning from liquid to vapor (from oceans, lakes).
    - **Transpiration:** Water vapor released by plants.
    - **Condensation:** Water vapor forming clouds.
    - **Precipitation:** Water falling as rain, snow, etc.
    - **Runoff:** Water flowing over land into rivers and oceans.
    - **Groundwater:** Water seeping into the soil.

**The Carbon Cycle**

  - Carbon is a key component of all organic compounds.
  - Key Processes:
    - **Biological:** Photosynthesis (takes in CO₂), Cellular Respiration (releases CO₂), Decomposition.
    - **Geochemical:** Release of CO₂ by volcanoes, burial and conversion of dead organisms into fossil fuels (coal, oil) over millions of years.
    - **Human Activity:** Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests releases large amounts of CO₂ into the atmosphere.

**The Nitrogen Cycle**

  - All organisms need nitrogen to make proteins and nucleic acids.
  - Although nitrogen gas (N₂) makes up 78% of the atmosphere, most organisms cannot use it in this form.
  - Key Processes:
    - **Nitrogen Fixation:** Certain types of bacteria convert N₂ gas into ammonia (NH₃). This occurs in soil and on roots of legumes (e.g., peanuts, peas).
    - **Assimilation:** Plants absorb nitrogen compounds (ammonia, nitrates) from the soil and incorporate them into proteins. Consumers get nitrogen by eating producers.
    - **Decomposition:** Decomposers return nitrogen to the soil as ammonia.
    - **Denitrification:** Other bacteria convert nitrates in the soil back into N₂ gas, which returns to the atmosphere.

**The Phosphorus Cycle**

  - Phosphorus is essential for DNA and RNA.
  - Unlike other cycles, it does **not** include the atmosphere. It is found mainly in rock, soil minerals, and ocean sediments.
  - It is released slowly as rocks wear down and is cycled locally between organisms and the soil.

**Nutrient Limitation**

  - **Primary Productivity** is the rate at which primary producers create organic material.
  - A **limiting nutrient** is a single nutrient that is scarce and therefore limits growth in an ecosystem.
  - In aquatic ecosystems, **nitrogen** and **phosphorus** are often limiting nutrients.
  - **Algal Bloom:** A sudden, rapid growth of algae caused by an influx of a limiting nutrient (e.g., from fertilizer runoff).
