**The Water Molecule**

  - **Polarity:** A water molecule (H₂O) is **polar** because the oxygen atom is more electronegative, pulling the shared electrons closer. This creates a slightly negative oxygen end and slightly positive hydrogen ends.

**Hydrogen Bonding**

  - The attraction between the positive hydrogen end of one water molecule and the negative oxygen end of another.
  - Though individually weak, the multitude of hydrogen bonds gives water its unique properties.

**Key Properties of Water:**

  - **Cohesion:** The attraction between molecules of the same substance (water to water). Explains **surface tension** (e.g., insects walking on water).
  - **Adhesion:** The attraction between molecules of different substances (e.g., water to glass). Explains **capillary action** (e.g., water moving up a plant's roots and stems) and the **meniscus** in a graduated cylinder.
  - **Heat Capacity:** Water can absorb or release a large amount of heat with only a small temperature change. This helps regulate cell temperatures and stabilizes environmental temperatures (e.g., in oceans).

**The pH Scale**

  - Measures the concentration of Hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution.
  - Ranges from 0 to 14.
  - **pH 7 = Neutral** (equal H⁺ and OH⁻ ions). Pure water is neutral.
  - **pH below 7 = Acidic** (more H⁺ ions). The lower the pH, the stronger the acid.
  - **pH above 7 = Basic (Alkaline)** (more OH⁻ ions). The higher the pH, the stronger the base.
  - Each step on the scale represents a tenfold change in H⁺ ion concentration.

**Acids and Bases**

  - **Acid:** A compound that forms H⁺ ions in solution (e.g., HCl, lemon juice).
  - **Base:** A compound that forms OH⁻ ions in solution (e.g., NaOH, soap).

**Buffers**

  - Weak acids or bases that can react with strong acids or bases to prevent sharp, sudden changes in pH.
  - **Crucial for maintaining homeostasis** in organisms. For example, blood has buffers (bicarbonate) to keep its pH stable at around 7.4.

**3.1 Introduction to Ecology.**

**What is Ecology?**

  - **Ecology** is the scientific study of interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical environment.
  - The **biosphere** is the combined portions of the planet where all life exists, including land, water, and air.

**Levels of Organization**

  - Ecologists study nature at different levels, from an individual to the entire planet:
  - **Species:** A group of similar organisms that can breed and produce fertile offspring.
  - **Population:** A group of individuals of the same species that live in the same area.
  - **Community:** An assemblage of different populations that live together in a defined area.
  - **Ecosystem:** All the organisms that live in a place, together with their physical environment.
  - **Biome:** A group of ecosystems that share similar climates and typical organisms.
  - **Biosphere:** Our entire planet, with all its organisms and physical environments.

**Biotic and Abiotic Factors**

- An **ecosystem** is shaped by the combination of its **biotic** (living) and **abiotic** (non-living) factors.
- **Biotic factors** include any living part of the environment with which an organism might interact (animals, plants, fungi, bacteria).
- **Abiotic factors** include any nonliving part of the environment (sunlight, heat, precipitation, humidity, wind, water currents, soil type).
- Key Concept: These factors are **interdependent**. For example, a forest (biotic) affects the humidity and soil (abiotic) of an area.

**Ecological Methods**

- Ecologists use **three** main scientific approaches:
- **Observation**: The first step; asking questions, often leading to experiments or models.
- **Experimentation**: Testing hypotheses in a lab or field setting.
- **Modeling**: Using mathematical formulas based on data to understand complex, long-term phenomena.
