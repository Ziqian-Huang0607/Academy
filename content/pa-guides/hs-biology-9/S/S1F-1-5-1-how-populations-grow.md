**Describing Populations**

- **Geographic** **Range:** The actual area inhabited by a population. Ranges can be tiny (bacteria in a pumpkin) or vast (cod in the Atlantic Ocean). Human activities (like transporting species) can dramatically alter a species' range.
- **Population Density:** The number of individuals per unit area (e.g., 100 deer per square kilometer). Different species in the same environment can have very different densities.
- **Distribution (Dispersion Patterns):** How individuals are spaced relative to each other.
  - **Random:** Unpredictable spacing; resources are uniformly distributed (e.g., wildflowers in a field).
  - **Uniform:** Evenly spaced; often due to territorial behavior or competition (e.g., penguins nesting).
  - **Clumped:** Grouped together; most common pattern, due to patchy resources, social behavior, or reproductive patterns (e.g., elephant herds).

**Growth Rate:** Determines if a population increases, decreases, or remains stable. A **positive growth rate** means births + Immigration exceed deaths + emigration. A **negative growth rate** means the opposite.

- **Age Structure:** The number or percentage of males and females in each age group of a population (e.g., pre-reproductive, reproductive, post-reproductive). **This is the most critical factor for predicting future population growth.** A population with many young individuals will likely grow rapidly.

**Factors That Change Population Size**

- **Birthrate (Natality):** The number of births in a given time period.
- **Death Rate (Mortality):** The number of deaths in a given time period.
- **Immigration:** The influx of new individuals from other areas.
- **Emigration:** The exit of individuals from a population.

**Exponential Growth (J-Curve)**

- **Definition:** Growth pattern where a population's **growth rate accelerates as size increases**, because a constant percentage multiplies an ever-larger base number.
- **Conditions:** Occurs only under **ideal, unlimited conditions**—unlimited resources, no predators, no disease.
- **Graph:** Produces a characteristic **J-shaped curve**. The slope gets steeper over time.
- **Examples in Nature:**
  - **Rapid Reproducers:** Bacteria dividing every 20 minutes.
  - **New/Invasive Species:** Hydrilla in Florida, gypsy moths in the U.S., rabbits in Australia. These species experience a "lag phase" followed by explosive growth in a new environment lacking natural limiting factors.

**Logistic Growth (S-Curve)**

- **Definition:** A more realistic growth model where population growth slows and eventually stops due to environmental resistance.
- **Phases:**
  - **Exponential Phase:** Initial rapid growth when resources are abundant.
  - **Transitional Phase:** Growth rate begins to slow as resources become limited and competition increases.
  - **Plateau Phase:** Population growth stops; birth rate ≈ death rate and/or immigration ≈ emigration.
- **Graph:** Produces a characteristic **S-shaped (sigmoid) curve**.
- **Carrying Capacity (K):** The **maximum population size that a particular environment can sustain indefinitely**. Represented by the flat line at the top of the S-curve. Populations fluctuate around K due to environmental variations.

## 5.2 Limits to Growth

**Limiting** **Factors**

- **Definition:** Any factor that restrains the growth of a population. **Collectively, limiting factors determine the carrying capacity of an environment.**
- **Categories:**
  - **Density-Dependent Factors:** Their effect **increases as population density increases**. They regulate population size around the carrying capacity.
  - **Density-Independent Factors:** Affect populations **regardless of their size or density**. They can cause sudden, drastic population crashes.

**Density-Dependent** **Limiting** **Factors**

- **Competition:** Individuals of the same (**intraspecific**) or different (**interspecific**) species compete for limited resources like food, water, space, mates, or sunlight. **Increased density intensifies competition**, leading to lower birth rates, higher death rates, or increased emigration.
- **Predation:** The predator-prey relationship creates cyclical population dynamics, as seen in the classic **Isle** **Royale wolf-moose** study. More prey supports more predators, which then reduce the prey population, leading to a decline in predators, and so on.
- **Herbivory:** Similar to predation; herbivore populations cycle with the availability of their plant food sources.
- **Parasitism and Disease:** **Spread more rapidly in dense populations** where hosts are in close contact. Example: Canine parvovirus in the Isle Royale wolf population.
- **Stress from Overcrowding:** High density can cause psychological stress (in animals), leading to hormonal changes, suppressed immune systems, decreased parental care, aggression, and lower reproduction rates.

**Density-Independent** **Limiting** **Factors**

- **Abiotic, physical factors** that strike affects all population.
- **Examples:** Severe weather (hurricanes, droughts, blizzards), wildfires, volcanic eruptions, pesticide application, and major habitat destruction (e.g., a landslide).
- **The Key Difference:** These factors can **cause a population crash, but they do not** **regulate** **a population around a carrying capacity.** The population may recover quickly or stay low.

**Controlling Invasive Species: A Key Application**

- Invasive species like **zebra** **mussels** or **hydrilla** grow exponentially because the **density-dependent limiting factors from their native habitat (specific predators, parasites, diseases) are absent.**
- Control efforts often try to re-establish these checks (e.g., introducing a natural predator like grass carp for hydrilla) or use artificial density-independent methods (herbicides, manual removal), which are often expensive and temporary.

## 5.3 Human Population Growth

**Historical Overview**

- **For most of history (< 1800s):** Very slow growth. **High birth rates** were offset by **very high death rates** (famine, disease, lack of medicine).
- **Industrial Revolution (~1800s) onward:** **Exponential growth** began. Advances in **agriculture, medicine, sanitation, and technology** caused a dramatic **drop in death rates**, while birth rates remained high. This is the fundamental cause of the modern population explosion.

**Patterns of Growth: Demography**

- **Demography:** The scientific study of human population statistics (births, deaths, age structure, etc.).
- **The Demographic Transition:** A model explaining the shift from high to low birth and death rates. **It is not a law, but an observed pattern in many developed nations.**
  - **Stage I (Pre-Industrial): High birth rate, High death rate.** Slow, unpredictable growth. (Most of human history).
  - **Stage II (Transitional): High birth rate, FALLING death rate.** **Rapid exponential growth.** This is where many developing countries were/still are.
  - **Stage III (Industrial): FALLING birth rate, Low death rate.** Population growth slows. Birth rates fall due to increased education, urbanization, lower child mortality, and greater status for women.
  - **Stage IV (Post-Industrial): Low birth rate, Low death rate.** Population stabilizes or even declines slightly.
- **Age-Structure Diagrams (Population Pyramids):**
  - **Wide base (many young):** Predicts rapid future growth (e.g., **Guatemala**).
  - **Nearly vertical sides:** Predicts slow, steady growth or stability (e.g., **United States**).
  - **Inverted pyramid (narrow base):** Predicts future population decline (e.g., Japan, some European nations).
- **Future Projections:** Global growth is slowing, but the population is still increasing. Nearly all future growth will occur in **developing countries**. The challenge of **Stage II** growth has profound implications for global resource use and environmental impact.
