Biosphere: consists of all life on earth and all parts of earth in which life exists, including atmosphere, lithosphere, hydrosphere.

Ecology: the scientific study of interactions among and between organisms and their physical environment in the biosphere.

Levels of Organization

- a species is a group of similar organisms that can breed and produce fertile offspring. Individual organisms

Population: a group if individuals that belong to the same species and live in the same area at the same time.

Community: an assemblage (集合）of different populations that live together in a same area. Ecosystem: all the organisms that live in a community together with their

physical environment.

Biome: a group of ecosystems that share similar climates and typical organisms. Biosphere: our entire planet with all its organisms and all its physical environments. Biotic factors- the biological influences on organisms, any living part of the environment with which an organism might interact. (Eg. Plants, animals, fungi, bacteria)

Abiotic factors- physical components of an ecosystem, any non-living part of the environment. (Eg. Sunlight, soil, temperature)

Abiotic factors can be influenced by the activities of organisms and vice versa.

Ecological Methods

Experimentation- It can be used to test the hypothesis.

Modeling- used to hep scientists understand events that occurred over such long periods of time or over such large distances that are difficult to study directly.

## 3.2 Energy, Producers, and Consumers

Autotroph: organisms that use solar or chemical energy to assemble inorganic compounds into complex organic compounds.

Inorganic compounds: water (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), oxygen (O2)

Organic compounds: carbohydrate (sugar, starch)

Photosynthesis: use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into oxygen and carbohydrates such as sugar and starch. (CO2+H2O+light energy—>carbohydrate+O2) Chemosynthesis: use chemical energy from inorganic molecules such as hydrogen sulfide to produce food. (CO2+hydrogen sulfide+O2—>carbohydrate+sulfur compound)

Heterotroph: Organisms that acquire energy from other organisms by ingesting.

Types of Consumers:

Carnivores: kill and eat other animals (tiger, shark)

Herbivores: eat plants, leaves, roots, seeds, or fruits (cow, rabbit)

Omnivores: diets include both plants and animals (human, pig, bear)

Scavengers: animals that consume the carcasses of other animals that have been killed or have died. (Eg. King vulture)

Decomposers (Saprotrophs): release digestive enzymes to break down dead organisms. (Eg. Bacteria, fungi)

Detritivores: ingest detritus particles, chewing or grinding them into even smaller pieces. (Eg. Snail, shrimp)

## 3.3 Energy Flow in Ecosystems

Energy flows through an ecosystem in a one-way stream, from primary producers to consumers.

Food chain: A series of steps in which organisms transfer energy by eating and being eaten. Food web: A model representing the many interconnected food chains in which energy flows. much more complicated than a single simple chain because many animals eat more than one kind of food.

Food chain shows how energy moves through the ecosystem. A food web shows how food chains interact.

Photoplankton: primary producers that are a mixture of floating algae; autotroph Zooplankton: small, swimming animals, feed on marine algae; heterotroph

Trophic level: each step in a food chain or food webs

Ecological pyramid: A diagram that show the relative amounts of energy / biomass / numbers of organisms at each trophic level in an ecosystem.

Pyramid of Energy: the relative amount of energy available at each trophic level, measured in units of energy, per unit area, per unit time.

Transfer of energy is never 100% efficient, only 10% is transferred to the next trophic level. 90% of energy is lost between trophic levels (not ingested, not digested, excreted, lost as heat from respiration)

Pyramid of numbers: the relative number of individual organisms at each trophic level in an ecosystem.

Pyramid of Biomass: the relative amount of living matter at each trophic level. Biomass is the total amount of living tissue within a given trophic level.

## 3.4 Cycles of Matter

-Unlike the one way flow of energy, matter is recycled within and between ecosystems. Biogeochemical cycles:

-biological process: eating, breathing

-geological process: volcanic eruption

-chemical process: decomposition

-human activity

Matter: anything that takes up space and has

mass never created or destroyed.

Nutrient: a chemical substance that an organism must obtain from its environment to sustain life are cycled.

The Water Cycle

Water continuously moves between the oceans, the atmosphere, the land, and living organisms.

Evaporation: water becomes water vapor

Condensation: cooling water vapor condenses into water droplets around dust particles.

Precipitation: water falls from clouds in the form of rain, sleet, snow, or hail.

Transpiration: water loss from plant leaves

Runoff: process of water flows over the land surface down into streams. Rivers, lakes and oceans.

Percolation: downward movement of water through soil or rock layers, underground phenomenon.

The Carbon Cycle

Carbon on the earth:

Living organisms: carbon compounds in producers, consumers, decomposers

Atmospherere: CO2

Lithosphere: carbonate rocks (CaCO3), fossil fuels (oil, coal), natural gas (CH4) Hydrosphere: dissolved CO2

Events of the cycle: Photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition, fossilization, combustion.

The Nitrogen Cycle

-All organisms require nitrogen to make amino acids and nucleic acids, which are used to build proteins and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA).

Nitrogen fixation:

-The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonia NH3

- A small amount of nitrogen gas is converted to usable forms by lightning called atmospheric nitrogen fixation.

Nitrification:

- Other soil bacteria convert NH3/NH4+ into nitrates and nitrites that primary producers Nitrogen-fixing bacteria can use to make proteins and nucleic acids

Ammonification

- Decomposers breakdown organic nitrogen compounds in waste and dead organisms to release ammonium NH4+.

Denitrification

-Other soil bacteria convert nitrates, nitrites into nitrogen gas.

The Phosphorus Cycle

-Phosphorus forms a part of vital molecules such as DNA and RNA.

-Although phosphorus is of great biological importance, it is not abundant in the biosphere.

Phosphorus cycles between geological and biological systems.

-Geological: Weathering and erosion release phosphorus from rocks.

-Biological: Phosphorus is taken up by producers, then passed to consumers.

-Long-term: Uplift returns phosphorus to rocks.

Nutrient Limitation: If ample sunlight and water are available, the primary productivity of an ecosystem may be limited by the availability of nutrients.

In soil: Fertilizers mainly supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium for plant growth. Micronutrients like iron and manganese are needed in tiny quantities.

In water: in saltwater(ocean), nitrogen is the limiting nutrient. In freshwater(lakes, streams), phosphorus is the limiting nutrient.
