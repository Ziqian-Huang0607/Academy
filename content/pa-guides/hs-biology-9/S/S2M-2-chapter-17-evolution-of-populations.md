### 17.1 Genes and Variation

**Genetics:** the scientific study of heredity-Delivery of traits from parents to the offspring

**Terms**

- **Genes**
- Basic unit of heredity, determined traits

- **Alleles**
- Different form of gene

- **Gene pool**

**Sources of Genetic Variation** Genetics and evolutionary theory identify three main sources of genetic variation:

- **Mutations:** Any change in the genetic material of a cell.
  - Mutations can be neutral, harmful, or beneficial depending on how they affect an organism's fitness.
  - Crucial factor: For a mutation to be passed on to the next generation and affect evolution, it must occur in the germ line cells that produce either eggs or sperm.
- **Genetic Recombination in Sexual Reproduction:** Most heritable differences are due to genetic recombination during sexual reproduction, not just mutations.
  - **Crossing-over:** Occurs during meiosis when paired chromosomes swap lengths of DNA, creating highly diverse, new combinations of alleles.
- **Lateral Gene Transfer:** The passing of genes from one organism to another organism that is not its offspring.
  - This is common in single-celled organisms (like bacteria swapping genes on plasmids) and increases genetic variation in any species that picks up the "new" genes.

**Single-Gene and Polygenic Traits** The number of phenotypes produced for a given trait depends on how many genes control the trait.

- **Single-gene trait**: A trait controlled by only one gene.
  - May have just two or three distinct phenotypes.
  - Example: Snail shell banding (some snails have dark bands, some do not).
- **Polygenic trait**: A trait controlled by two or more genes.
  - Each gene of a polygenic trait often has two or more alleles, resulting in many possible genotypes and an even wider range of phenotypes.
  - When the frequency of phenotypes for a polygenic trait is graphed, it typically forms a bell curve (a normal distribution).
  - Example: Human height varies greatly and is not just "short" or "tall."

### 17.2 Evolution as Genetic Change in Populations

**How Natural Selection Works**

- **Evolution=**Change of allele frequency in a population
- **Fitness=**Survive and reproduce=successfully pass genes to the next generation
- **Natural selection on single gene traits**
- Selection on a particular phenotype->change in allele frequency->evolution
- **Natural Selection on Polygenic Traits:** Can affect the relative fitness of phenotypes and thereby produce one of three types of selection:
  - **Directional selection**: Occurs when individuals at one end of the bell curve have higher fitness than individuals in the middle or at the other end. The range of phenotypes shifts.
  - **Stabilizing selection**: Occurs when individuals near the center of the curve have higher fitness than individuals at either end. The center of the curve stays at its current position, but the curve narrows.
  - **Disruptive selection**: Occurs when individuals at the outer ends of the curve have higher fitness than individuals near the middle. This acts against intermediate types and can create two distinct phenotypes (splitting the curve).

**Genetic Drift**

- **Genetic drift**: A random change in allele frequency that occurs in small populations. Individuals that carry a particular allele may leave more descendants than other individuals leave, just by chance.
  - **Bottleneck effect**: A change in allele frequency following a dramatic reduction in the size of a population (usually due to a disaster or disease). This can severely reduce a population's genetic diversity.
  - **Founder effect**: A change in allele frequencies as a result of the migration of a small subgroup of a population to a new habitat.

**Evolution Versus Genetic Equilibrium**

- **Genetic equilibrium**: A situation in which allele frequencies in a population's gene pool do not change. If a population is in genetic equilibrium, it is not evolving.
- **Hardy-Weinberg principle**: States that allele frequencies in a population should remain constant unless one or more factors cause those frequencies to change.

- **No mutations:**No new alleles be introduced into the gene pool.
- **No sexual selection:**All members of the population must have an equal opportunity to produce offspring (Random Mating)
- **No mutation:**Population does not gain or lose allele by any movement of individuals into or out of a population (no gene flow)
- **No natural selection:**No phenotype can have a selective advantage over another.

### 17.3 The Process of Speciation

**Isolating Mechanisms**

- **Species**: A population or group of populations whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
- **Speciation**: The formation of a new species.
- Interbreeding links members of a species genetically. Any genetic change that occurs in one individual can spread through the population as that individual and its offspring mate.
- **Reproductive isolation**: Occurs when a population splits into two groups and the two populations no longer interbreed. When populations become reproductively isolated, they can evolve into two separate species.

**Types of Reproductive Isolation** Reproductive isolation can develop in a variety of ways, including:

- **Behavioral isolation**: Occurs when two populations that are capable of interbreeding develop differences in courtship rituals or other behaviors.
  - Example: Eastern and Western meadowlarks share identical habitats but will not mate with each other because they use different songs to attract mates.
- **Geographic isolation**: Occurs when two populations are separated by geographic barriers such as rivers, mountains, or bodies of water.
  - Example: The Kaibab squirrel and the Abert's squirrel were separated by the formation of the Grand Canyon, leading to two distinct populations.
  - Note: Geographic barriers do not always guarantee isolation. A flooded river might isolate rodents, but it would not isolate populations of birds that can simply fly across.
- **Temporal isolation**: Occurs when two or more species reproduce at different times.
  - Example: Three similar species of orchids live in the same rain forest, but each species blooms on a different day, meaning they cannot pollinate one another.

**Speciation in Darwin's Finches** Speciation in the Galápagos finches occurred by founding of a new population, geographic isolation, changes in the new population's gene pool, behavioral isolation, and ecological competition.

- **1. Founders Arrive:** A few finches from South America (species M) arrived on one of the Galápagos islands. Because of the **founder effect**, the allele frequencies of this founding finch population could have differed from those in the original South American population.
- **2. Geographic Isolation:** The island's environment was different from the mainland. Some birds then crossed to another island. The two populations were now geographically isolated and no longer shared a common gene pool.
- **3. Changes in Gene Pools:** Over time, populations on each island adapted to local environments. On one island, directional selection favored birds with larger, heavier beaks to crack large seeds, leading to a new phenotype (species A) and different allele frequencies.
- **4. Behavioral Isolation:** If birds from the second island cross back to the first island, will they mate? Finches carefully choose mates with beak sizes similar to their own. Because the two populations now have different beak sizes, they will not mate with each other. Thus, reproductive isolation has occurred, and they are distinct species.
- **5. Competition and Continued Evolution:** The two distinct species now live together on the first island and compete for seeds. During dry seasons, birds with the most distinct beak types (e.g., the largest and the smallest) have the highest fitness because they don't have to compete as much for the same food. This can lead to further evolution and the emergence of even more species.
