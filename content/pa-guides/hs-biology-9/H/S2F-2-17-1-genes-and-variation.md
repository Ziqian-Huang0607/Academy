**Genetics Joins Evolutionary Theory**

**Gregor Mendel:** scientist that discovered the basic laws of heredity through experiments with pea plants

- “Father of Genetics”

**Genetics:** the scientific study of heredity

- **Heredity:** delivery of traits from parent to offspring

**Alleles:** specific forms of a gene that may vary individual to individual

- **dominant allele**: allele that is expressed in an organism's appearance even when only one copy is present.
- **recessive allele**: allele that is expressed only when two copies are present and no dominant allele is present.

**Genotype:** specific combination of alleles an organism carries

- **Homozygous:** same alleles on the homologous chromosomes
- **Heterozygous:** different alleles on the homologous chromosomes

**Phenotype:** all physical, physiological, and behavioral characteristics of an organism

- natural selection acts directly on phenotype (not genotype!)

**Trait**: characteristic or feature of an organism

(To differ between phenotype, genotype, allele, and trait: eg. flower color is a trait, purple flowers is a phenotype, Pp is a genotype, and P is a dominant allele)

**Natural Selection:**

- Mutations in genes generate various forms of alleles (variation)
- Natural selection acts directly on phenotypes, not genotype
- some individuals have phenotypes that are better suited to their environment than others
- these individuals are more likely to survive and reproduce (more likely to pass on more copies of their genes to others)

**Population**: group of individuals of same species that can interbreed

**Gene pool**: all the genes and alleles present in a population

**Allele frequency**: number of times an allele occurs in a gene pool

- Has nothing to do with whether an allele is dominant or recessive

**Evolution:** (in genetic terms) the change of allele in a population over time.

- unlike natural selection, populations evolve, not individuals

**Sources of Genetic Variation**

**Mutation:** any change in genetic material of a cell

- neutral mutations: do not change organism’s phenotype
- May increase, decrease, or not affect fitness
- Only mutations in gametes would be passed to the next generation

**Genetic Recombination During Sexual Reproduction:**

- during independent assortment, chromosomes move independently, producing unique gene combinations that make the next generation different from its parents
- During crossing over, paired chromosomes swap DNA randomly
- Sexual reproduction increases genetic variation without changing the allele frequency

**Lateral Gene Transfer:** passing of genes from one organism to another organisms that’s not its offspring

- genes can be passed to same or other species
- increases genetic variation in species that receives the new genes
- eg. Bacteria swap genes on plasmids in the evolution of antibiotic resistance

**Single-Gene and Polygenic Traits**

- number of phenotypes produced for a trait depends on how many genes control the trait

**Single-gene trait**: trait controlled by one gene

- usually, there’s only 2-4 distinct phenotypes
- eg. human ABO blood type

**Polygenic trait**: trait controlled by two or more genes

- many possible genotypes and phenotypes
- eg. human height
- **normal distribution**: bell-shaped curve that appears when many genes contribute small effects to the same trait

## 17.2 Evolution as Genetic Change in Populations

**How Natural Selection Works**

**Evolutionary fitness**: success in passing genes to the next generation

**Evolutionary adaptation**: any genetically controlled trait that increases individual’s ability to pass along it alleles

**Natural Selection on single-gene traits**: selection on particular phenotype -> changes in allele frequency -> changes in phenotype frequencies -> evolution

**Natural selection on polygenic traits**: selection on a range of phenotypes -> changes in allele frequency -> changes in phenotype frequencies -> 3 types of selection: directional, stabilizing, disruptive

**Directional Selection:** individuals at one end of the curve have higher fitness than individuals in the middle/at the other end

- eg. Beak sizes of Galapagos finches

**Stabilizing Selection**: individuals near the center of the curve have higher fitness than individuals at either end

- intermediate organisms have higher fitness
- Eg. Birth weights of infants

**Disruptive selection**: individuals at outer ends of the curve have higher fitness than individuals near the middle

- acts against intermediate organisms
- Eg. In particular bird population with disruptive selection, the population might split into two groups: one with smaller beaks and one with larger beaks

**Genetic Drift**: random change in allele frequency

- individuals that carry a particular allele may leave more descendants than other individuals by chance
- **Bottleneck** **Effect**: change in allele frequency following a dramatic reduction in the size of a population
- Eg. A disease/disaster that kills many individuals in the population, and by random chance, the remaining population’s gene pool may contain different allele frequencies
- **Founder Effect**: allele frequencies change as a result of the migration of a small subgroup of population, new population’s allele frequency doesn’t reflect old group’s
- Eg. Fruit flies on Hawaiian islands descended from same mainland fruit fly population, but they have different allele frequencies from the original

**Evolution VS Genetic Equilibrium**

**Genetic Equilibrium**: population is not evolving, allele frequencies in its gene pool do not change

- Meiosis and fertilization (during sexual reproduction) do not change allele frequencies! (But increase genetic diversity, as mentioned in 17.1). Hypothetically, population of sexually reproducing organisms could remain in genetic equilibrium.

-

**Hardy-Weinberg Principle:** allele frequencies should remain constant unless one or more of factors cause those frequencies to change (five factors listed below)

- **Nonrandom mating**: organisms in the population do not have equal opportunity to produce offspring
- **A small population:** genetic drift have deadly effect on small populations
- **Immigration/emigration:** causes **gene flow**, which introduces/takes away alleles to a population
- **Mutations:** introduce new alleles in gene pool
- **Natural Selection**: different genotypes have different fitness, driving evolution

**Hardy Weinberg Equation:**

- p+q=1, (p+q)2=1
- p = frequency of dominant allele (A) , q = frequency of recessive allele (a)
- p2=frequency of homozygous dominant (AA), 2pq =frequency of heterozygous (Aa), q2=frequency of homozygous recessive (aa)

## 17.3 The Process of Speciation

**Isolating Mechanisms**

**Species**: a population or group of populations whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring

**Speciation**: formation of a new species

**Reproductive** **isolation**: when members of a population stop breeding with other members, over time, isolated gene pools diverge into separate species.

**Behavioral isolation:** two populations develop differences in courtship rituals/other behaviors

    - Eg. Eastern and western meadowlarks, which won’t mate each other since they use different songs to attract males
  - **Geographic isolation**: two populations separated by geographic barriers such as rivers/mountains
    - Eg. Abert’s squirrel, where a small population became isolated on the north rim of the Grand Canyon, forming separate gene pools than the major population, and over time, evolving differently into two separate species
    - Geographic barriers do not always guarantee isolation.
      - Eg. large river can separate animals on land, but cannot separate bird populations
- **Temporal Isolation:** two or more species reproduce at different times
    - Pre-mating/post-mating mechanism
    - Eg. Similar species of orchids bloom on different days, so they can’t pollinate each other
