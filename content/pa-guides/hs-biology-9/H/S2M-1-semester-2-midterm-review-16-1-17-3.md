#### Chapter 16: Evolution

#### 16.1 Darwin’s Voyage of Discovery

Evolution

- The process of change over time
- Modern organisms evolved from common ancestors through descent over long periods of time
- Species: a group of similar organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring

Charles Darwin

- 1st person to publish convincing evidences that species evolve
- Proposed reasonable mechanism explaining evolution (natural selection)
- Began 5 year voyage on HMS Beagle in 1831
  - To map coast of South America
  - Collected specimen of plants and animals, found evidences of evolution

3 Patterns of Biodiversity

- Species vary globally
  - Different species inhabit separate but ecologically similar habitats around the globe
  - i.e. similar flightless birds (ostrich, rhea, emu) inhabit separate but similar grasslands
- Species vary locally
  - Different species occupy different habitats within a local area
  - i.e. Isabela Island Tortoise vs Hood Island Tortoise
- Species vary overtime
  - Some fossils of extinct animals are similar to living species
    - fossils: preserved remains and traces of ancient organisms

Darwin’s thoughts

- Inspired by discoveries, especially in Galapagos Islands
- Wondered if species originated from common ancestor

#### 16.2 Ideas That Shaped Darwin’s Thinking

James Hulton

- Theory of Geological Change, 1785
  - Changes in Earth’s surface can be caused by slow and continuous changes
  - The Earth is much older than we once thought (thousands of years old)

Charles Lyell

- Principles of Geology
  - Geological processes in action today must be the same ones that shaped Earth millions of years ago
- Uniformitarianism: changes happening very gradually

Jean Baptiste Lamarck

- Hypothesis of the Inheritance of Acquired Characteristics, 1809
  - Acquired characteristics would be passed on to the offspring
    - Acquired characteristics: traits altered by an individual organism during its life by selectively using/disusing a body part
  - All organisms have an inborn urge to be more complex and perfect
- Evaluation of this hypothesis
  - Incorrect:
    - Evolution does not mean that over time a species becomes “better”
    - Acquired characteristics cannot be passed to offspring (only changes in reproduction cells can)
  - Correct:
    - One of the first naturalists to suggest that species changed
    - Among first to try to explain evolution scientifically using natural processes

Thomas Malthus

- Principle of populations, 1798
  - Wars, famine, and diseases limit population growth (population grows exponentially and these factors grow linearly)
  - If human population grows unchecked, not enough food/space

Artificial selection

- Nature provides variations, humans selectively breed those that are found useful
- Darwin realized some variations could be inherited
  - Natural variation – provides raw material for evolution

#### 16.3 Darwin Presents His Case

1859, Darwin, book “On the Origin of Species”

- Outlined ideas of evolution: natural selection with 3 principles

1. Struggle for existence

- If more individuals are produced than can survive, individuals must compete to abtain food, living space, and other limited necessities of life

2. Variation and adaptation

- Individuals in a population vary in heritable traits
- Adaptation: any heritable characteristic that increases an organism’s ability to survive and reproduce in its environment
  - i.e. camouflage, mimicry, certain behaviors

3. Survival of the fittest

- Fitness: how well an organism can survive and reproduce in its environment
  - Survive and reproduce always go together!!!
- Difference in the rates of survival and reproduction (passing hereditary adaptations on to the next generation)

Natural selection

- Process by which organisms with variations most suited to their local environment survive and leave more offsprings
  - Environment influences fitness
  - Well adapted individuals survive and reproduce

Common descent

- Over many generations, adaptations could cause successful species to evolve into new species
- Common descent: all species (living and extinct) are descended from ancient common ancestors
- Descent with modification: living species are descended with modification from common ancestors
  - Fossil records provide evidences of such descent

#### 16.4 Evidences of Evolution

Biogeography

- Study of where organisms live now and where they and their ancestors lived in the past
- Patterns in distribution of living and fossil species tell us how modern organisms evolved from their ancestors

Evolution Patterns

- Adaptive radiation: One species evolved into several different forms that live in different ways (closely related but different)
  - Adapted to different environments
  - Share common ancestors
  - i.e. Human and blue whale
- Convergent evolution: Unrelated organisms living in similar environments come to resemble each other (distantly related but similar)
  - Adapted to similar environments
  - Do not share common ancestors
  - i.e. Bat and bird (wings), shark and dolphin (fins)

Evidences of Evolution

- Fossils
  - Earth is 4.6 billion years old, enough timeframe for natural selection to happen
  - Intermediate fossil shows evolution of modern species from ancestors
    - Due to geological processes, marine fossils will be found on mountain tops
- Comparing anatomy
  - Homologous structures: similar structures in different species adapted for different functions
    - Evidence of recent common ancestor
    - i.e. forelimb in tetrapods (amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals)
  - Vestigial structure: structures inherited from ancestors which have lost much of their original functions due to different selection pressures on the descendant
    - A type of homologous structure
    - Evidence of common ancestor
    - i.e. hind limbs of whales and dolphins
  - Analogous structures: body parts that share a common function but not a common structure
    - Not evidence of common ancestor
    - i.e. wings of birds and bees
- Comparing embryology
  - Identifying similar patterns of embryo development
  - Evidence of common ancestor
  - i.e. all vertebrates exhibit similar structures in embryo development
    - Tail
    - Notochord (early form of a spine)
    - Pharyngeal pouches
- Genetic and molecular biology (most useful evidence)
  - Compare the genetic information of different species and determine whether they are closely related or not
  - All living things use the same genetic code
    - Comparing DNA/RNA/protein sequence is the most powerful evidence of common ancestry
  - Homologous genes
    - i.e. Hox genes
      - Determine the head to tail axis in embryonic development
      - In almost all multicellular organisms
      - Gene Hoxc8 most commonly looked at
  - Homologous proteins
    - i.e. Cytochrome C
      - Functions in cellular respiration
      - In almost all living cells

Testing natural selection

- Darwin’s hypothesis: natural selection shaped the beaks of different bird populations as they become adapted to eating different foods
- The Grants observed the Galápagos finches living in their natural environment to evaluate Darwin’s hypothesis
  - 2 assumptions
    - For beak size and shape to evolve, there must be enough heritable variations in those traits
    - Differences in beak size and shape must produce difference in fitness
  - Conclusion: variation within a species increases the likelihood of the species’ adapting to and surviving environment change

#### Chapter 17: Evolution of Populations

#### 17.1 Genes and variation

Gregor Mendel

- Born in 1822, Germany; Botanist
- He tested 34 varieties for constancy of their traits. He chose seven traits, such as plant height (short or tall) and seed color (green or yellow)

Genetics

- The scientific study of heredity
  - Heredity: delivery of traits from parent to offspring
- Terms
  - Traits
    - A specific feature inherited from the parents
    - Physical / physiological / behavioral characteristics
    - Determined by genes
    - A trait many have different forms
  - Genes
    - Basic unit of heredity, determine traits
    - Specific fragment of DNA on chromosomes
  - Alleles
    - Different forms of a gene
    - Determine different forms of one trait
    - Differ from each other by one or a few DNA bases only
  - Gene pool
    - All the genes, including all the different alleles for each gene present in a population
  - Allele frequency
    - The number of times a particular allele occurs in a gene pool
  - Homologous chromosomes
    - Pair of chromosomes that one comes from the male parent and one from the female parent
      - Same genes in same order, same length, same shape
      - May not the same alleles for one gene
  - Genotype
    - Particular combination of alleles an organism carries
    - Together with environmental conditions, determine its traits
    - Homozygous: same alleles on the homologous chromosomes
    - Heterozygous: different alleles on the homologous chromosomes
  - Phenotype (same thing as traits)
    - All physical, physiological, and behavioral characteristics of an organism

Genetics Joins Evolutionary Theory

- Heritable traits are controlled by genes that are carried on chromosomes
- Mutation in genes generate various forms of alleles, hence generate variation
- Natural selection acts directly on phenotype, not genotype
- Evolution is any change in the relative frequency of alleles in the gene pool of a population overtime
  - Natural selection operates on individuals, but ultimately changes allele frequencies in the gene pool, so populations evolve

Sources of Genetic Variation

- Mutation
  - Any change in the genetic material of a cell
    - Gene mutation, chromosome mutation
  - Beneficial, neutral, harmful (most are neutral)
  - Only mutations in germ line cells that produce gametes can be passed on to offsprings
- Sexual Reproduction
  - Meiosis (crossing over and independent assortment of homologous chromosomes)
    - Crossing over: paired chromosomes swap lengths of DNA at random
    - Independent assortment: the random arrangement and separation of homologous chromosome pairs during meiosis
  - Sexual reproduction increases genetic variation without changing allele frequency!!
  - Fertilization of sperm and egg
- Lateral Gene transfer
  - Organisms pass genes from one individual to another that is not its offspring
    - Can occur between same or different species
    - Increase genetic variation in a species
    - i.e. Bacterial conjugation

Single-Gene and Polygenic Traits

- The number of phenotypes produced for a trait depends on how many genes control the trait
- Single-Gene Traits
  - A trait controlled by only one gene
  - May have just 2-4 distinct phenotypes
  - i.e. pea plant flower color is controlled by only gene, with two alleles
  - i.e. Human ABO blood type is controlled by one gene, with three alleles
- Polygenic traits
  - Traits controlled by two or more genes
  - The trait shows wide range (usually continuous) phenotypes
  - Also influenced by environmental factors
  - Distribution of phenotype tend to show normal distribution
  - i.e. Human skin color

#### 17.2 Evolution as Genetic Change in Populations

1. Natural selection on single-gene traits

  - Selection on particular phenotype Change in allele frequency Evolution

2. Natural selection on polygenetic traits

  - Selection on a range of phenotypes Change in allele frequency Evolution
- Directional selection
  - Selection on one extreme end of phenotypes
    - When individuals at one end of the curve have higher fitness than individuals in the middle or the other end
    - i.e. Beak size of finches
- Stabilizing selection
  - Selection on common or intermediate forms of phenotypes, eliminating extreme forms
    - When individuals near the center of the curve have higher fitness than the individuals at two ends
    - i.e. Selection on birth weights of infants
- Disruptive selection
  - Selection on both extreme ends of phenotypes, eliminating common groups
    - One population divides into two distinct groups
    - When individuals at the two ends of the curve have higher fitness that the individuals near the center
    - i.e. Decrease of medium sized seeds leads to
      - Birds with usually small or large beaks would have higher fitness
      - Two groups: One with smaller beaks and one with larger beaks

3. Genetic Drift

- Alleles can become more or less common by chance
- Random change in allele frequency
- Usually in small populations
- Individuals carrying a particular allele may leave more descendants than others
- BY CHANCE
  - Bottleneck effect
    - A change in allele frequency following a dramatic reduction in the size of the population
      - Due to change in the environment (disaster, disease)
      - Only a small portion of the population survived
      - Leading to a change in allele frequency
  - Founder effect
    - A change in allele frequency that may occur when a few individuals from a population migrate to and colonize a new habitat
      - Due to migration of a small subgroup of a population
      - Few individuals become isolated from a large population and establish a new population
      - The new population's allele frequency is different from the original population’s

Evolution vs. Genetic Equilibrium

- A population is in genetic equilibrium or not evolving if allele frequencies don’t change
  - Only for a short period of time
- Hardy Weinberg principle, five conditions are required for genetic equilibrium
  - A large population: genetic drift has less effect on large populations than on small populations.
  - No mutations: no new alleles be introduced into the gene pool.
  - No sexual selection (random mating): All members of the population must have an equal opportunity to produce offspring.
  - No migration (no gene flow): Population does not gain or lose alleles by any movement of individuals into or out of a population.
  - No natural selection: No phenotype can have a selective advantage over another.
- The Hardy-Weinberg Equation
  - p=frequency of allele A (dominant allele)
  - q=frequency of allele a (recessive allele)
  - p+q=1, (p+q)2=1 so p2+2pq+ q2=1
  - p2=frequency of AA (homozygous dominant)
  - 2pq =frequency of Aa (heterozygous)
  - q2=frequency of aa (homozygous recessive)

## 17.3 The Process of Speciation

Isolating Mechanisms

- Speciation: the formation of a new species.
- Reproductive isolation: separation of population so that they do not recognize each other for mating purposes
  - Three types:
    - Behavioral isolation
      - Two populations develop different in courtship (seeking affections) rituals, or other behaviors.
      - i.e. eastern and western meadow larks use different songs to attract mates.
    - Geographic isolation
      - Two populations are separated by geographic barriers such as rivers, mountains, or bodies of water.
      - i.e. Abert's and kaibab squirrels
      - Geographic barriers do not always guarantee isolation. (think about birds when writing the exam!)
    - Temporal isolation
      - Two or more species reproduce at different times.
      - i.e. orchids bloom on different days.
      - Premating mechanisms vs postmating mechanism

Case Study: Speciation in Darwin’s Finches

- Speciation in Galapagos finches occurred by founding of a new population, geographic isolation, changes in the new population’s gene pool, behavioral isolation, and ecological competition.
