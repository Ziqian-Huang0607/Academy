Ecology:

Scientific study of interactions among organisms and with their physical environment in the biosphere.

- Interdependence: Organisms rely on each other through various interactions:
- E.g. tobacco hornworm system:
- Parasitism: Wasp lays its young on the hornworm.
- Feeding: Hornworm consumes tobacco leaf.
- Biotic Factors: living parts of ecosystem; organisms
- Abiotic Factors: physical, non living parts of the ecosystem
- the difference between two is not always clear because they influence each other

Levels of Organisms

1. Individual Organisms.

A single, separate individual that can breed and reproduce (basic life functions).

2.    Population

Group of individuals that live in same area at the same time

3. Community

Assemblage of different populations that live in the same area (*Only living organisms!!)

4. Ecosystem

All organisms and physical environment in a community (*Community + physical environment)

5. Biome

A group of ecosystems that share similar climates+ typical organisms

6. Biosphere

All organisms + biomes in the planet (atmosphere, lithosphere (Earth), hydrosphere)

Ecological Methods

- Observe: noticing and describing events in a careful+orderly way
- Experimentation: used to test hypothesis
- Modeling: creating simplified representations (with numerical equations, graphs,etc) to understand long-term/distance events

## 3.2 Energy, Producers, Consumers

### Primary producers (autotrophs)

- Make their own food.
- Use solar or chemical energy to assemble inorganic molecules into complex organic compounds (carbohydrates).

### Two types of primary production

1. **Photosynthesis:** using light energy from the sun to convert $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ into oxygen and carbohydrates.
   - Formula: $6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$
   - Occurs in the chloroplast.
   - E.g. plants, algae, some bacteria.
2. **Chemosynthesis:** using chemical energy from inorganic molecules to produce food.
   - Organisms that cannot access sunlight.
   - E.g. bacteria in the deep sea.

### Consumers (heterotrophs)

- **Carnivores**
- **Herbivores**
- **Omnivores**
- **Scavengers:** consume carcasses (dead bodies).
- **Decomposers (saprotrophs):** break down food chemically and produce detritus.
  - **Detritus:** small pieces of dead and decaying plant and animal remains; recycles nutrients.
  - **Digest, then ingest** (external digestion), e.g. bacteria, fungi.
- **Detritivores:** ingest detritus, chewing and grinding it into smaller pieces.
  - **Ingest, then digest** (internal digestion), e.g. earthworms.
- **Others:**
  - The Venus flytrap is an autotroph.
  - **Not** all plants are autotrophs (holoparasites).
  - Euglenoids are mixotrophic.
  - Parasites.

## 3.3 Energy Flow in Ecosystems

- Energy: flows through ecosystem in one way stream; NOT recycled

- Food Chain: series of steps in which organisms transfer energy by eating/being eaten

- 1st is always producers
- Arrows (->) indicate direction of energy flow
- Food chains vary in length

Trophic Level: each step in food chain/web

Food Web: network of food chains/ feeding relationships

- Species may play roles at more than one trophic level + feed on more than 1 food Change in population size of one organism -> causes disturbtions on others

Ecological Pyramids

- graphical models that display the relative biomass, energy, or biomass of each trophic level in an ecosystem.

1. Pyramids of Energy

- shows the amount of energy available at each trophic level
- Unit: kcal/ m2 / year (per sqm per year) - 10% rule: only about 10% of the energy of one level is successfully converted into biomass at the next level; most energy is lost as heat or excreted

2. Pyramids of Biomass

- relative amount of living tissue in each level
- Unit: kg/m2 or g/m2
- amount of biomass a trophic level can support is

determined by amount of energy

3. Pyramids of Number

- relative number of individual organisms

## 3.4 Cycles of Matter

- **Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space; it cannot be created or destroyed.
- **Nutrient:** a chemical substance an organism must obtain from its environment to sustain life.
- Unlike energy, matter **recycles** within and between ecosystems.

### 1. The water cycle

Water continuously moves between the oceans, organisms and the environment (ocean/lakes → water vapour → clouds → land/rivers → groundwater, with plants returning water to the air).

1. The sun's heat **evaporates** water from the sea.
2. The water vapour **condenses** in cold air into clouds.
3. Water **precipitates** as the clouds become dense and heavy.
4. Water in the soil flows underground, called **percolation**, replenishing underground aquifers.
5. Water on land flows to the sea, called **runoff**.
6. Water inside plants is given off through the leaves, called **transpiration**.

### 2. The carbon cycle

- Carbon is found in living organisms, and also in the atmosphere, the lithosphere ($\text{CaCO}_3$) and the hydrosphere.
- Learn the carbon cycle by understanding each way carbon travels, then connect them into the whole cycle (atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ ⇄ plants → animals → dead matter → fossil fuels → back to the atmosphere).
- **Biological transfer:** **photosynthesis** and **feeding** take carbon (sugar) into organisms; **respiration** releases carbon (carbon dioxide) from organisms; **decomposition** returns carbon to the soil.
- **Geological transfer:** **fossilisation** stores carbon in the lithosphere.
- **Human transfer:** **combustion** burns fossil fuels, releasing carbon (carbon dioxide).

> Plants are a hotel for carbon: photosynthesis checks carbon in and respiration checks it out. When plants are eaten or decomposed, the carbon changes rooms. Fossilisation traps the carbon inside the hotel, and it eventually escapes through combustion, ready for its next check-in.

### 3. The nitrogen cycle

- Nitrogen helps form amino acids and nucleic acids, and makes up 78% of the atmosphere's gases.
- **Nitrogen fixation:**
  - Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (such as *Rhizobium*) produce an enzyme that converts atmospheric $\text{N}_2$ into **ammonia** ($\text{NH}_3$), which can form **ammonium** ($\text{NH}_4^+$) in soil.
  - The high energy of lightning breaks the triple bond of $\text{N}_2$, causing it to react to form $\text{NO}_x$, which dissolves in rain and falls to the soil as **nitrate** ($\text{NO}_3^-$).
  - Humans convert $\text{N}_2$ and hydrogen into **ammonia** ($\text{NH}_3$) under high pressure and temperature to make chemical fertilisers, creating more greenhouse gases at the same time.
- **Nitrification:** soil bacteria convert $\text{NH}_3/\text{NH}_4^+$ to **nitrite** ($\text{NO}_2^-$), then to **nitrate** ($\text{NO}_3^-$).
  - Plants absorb nitrate and use it to build organic molecules; animals obtain nitrogen by feeding (a type of **assimilation**).
- **Ammonification:** decomposers convert the nitrogen in dead organisms and waste into ammonium ($\text{NH}_4^+$).
- **Denitrification:** bacteria convert nitrate ($\text{NO}_3^-$), through intermediate forms, back to **nitrogen gas** $\text{N}_2$, returning it to the atmosphere.

> The nitrogen cycle works like building a Lego set. From the original scattered nitrogen

gas, its base forms as ammonia and ammonium. Nitrite and Nitrate are details built upon the base, but mistakes present frequently within the details, forcing to start again from the base. Eventually it finishes, but a pet dog breaks it, turning back to Nitrogen gas and rebuilt again and again.

3. The Phosphorus Cycle

- Phosphorus is commonly found as

phosphate in rocks and minerals;

organisms contain organic

phosphorus compounds.

- Weathering and erosion release

phosphate from rocks into soil and

water.

- Plants absorb phosphate; consumers obtain phosphorus by feeding on plants or other animals.
- Waste and decomposition return phosphorus to soil and water. Some phosphate eventually settles into sediments and may form rock over geological time.
- Unlike the water, carbon and nitrogen cycles, the phosphorus cycle has no major atmospheric gas phase, which means that this cycle is hard to move back to land in short- term.

Nutrient Limitation

- Primary productivity is limited by specific nutrients: the limited amount of one nutrient control the rate producers create organic material and their growth.
- Oceans have a limited amount of nitrogen, while freshwater ecosystems have a limited amount of phosphorus.
- Fertilizers, used to prosper crop growth, contain large amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, etc.
- These substances are often the limited nutrients of an ecosystem, and lead to excessive growth of producers such as algae, disrupting the ecosystem.
- Excess phosphorus from fertilizers can cause algal blooms and eutrophication, reducing dissolved oxygen and harming aquatic life.
