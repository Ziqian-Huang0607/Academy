## 4.2 Niches & Community Interactions

Habitat: where an organism lives.

- Determined by range of tolerance

Tolerance: ability of an organism to survive+reproduce within a range of conditions

- Different species have different ranges of tolerance.

Ex: If temperature becomes too high, an organism must expend more energy maintaining homeostasis. This leaves less energy available for growth and reproduction.

Range of tolerance

- the range of environmental conditions under which an organism can survive.
- Optimum range: best conditions for performance and reproduction
- Zones of physiological stress: near the upper/lower limits of an organism's tolerance range, can survive but performance declines

- Zone of intolerance: conditions the organism cannot survive in.

Niche

- ecological role occupied by the organism; includes habitat, biotic interactions, interaction with environment (abiotic factors), resources used, physical tolerances, etc. Generalist: Organisms with a broad niche
- can tolerate a broad range of environmental conditions + habitats + resources
- Ex: Rat

Specialist: Organisms with a narrow niche

- narrow range of tolerance
- Ex: Panda

Resources: Necessities organisms need to survive, grow, and reproduce. Competition: occurs when organisms require the same limited resources at same place+time

- Intraspecific competition: Competition between organisms of the same species.
- Interspecific competition; Competition between organisms of different species.
- Shapes community by:

1. Determinethe number and kinds of species inside a community

2. Determine the niche each specie occupies

3. Cause species to divide resources

Competitive Exclusion Principle (Gauses Principle)

- two species CANNOT occupy identical ecological niches in the same habitat indefinitely.
- When organisms compete, one may have greater access to resources or a better ability to compete has advantage in obtaining resources and surviving.
- One will eventually outcompete and eliminate other, or cause shift in resource use

Overlapping Niches: shared resource requirements → possible competition. overlap does not always prevent coexistence.

Resource partitioning: species divide resources by place/ time/ method to reduce competition and coexist.

Ex: two insect-eating bird species feed at different heights of the same tree.

## 4.3 Successions

Communities and Successions

- Ecosystems start to change after a disturbance: and event that dramatically changes order and patterns within an ecosystem.

- Ecological Succession: series of predictable changes that occur in a community over time.

- Primary succession: establishment of a community in an area of exposed rock that has no top soil.

- Pioneer species: grow on rock and fixes nitrogen gas, break down rocks and add organic matter to the soil.

- Secondary succession happen with existing communities not completely destroyed.

- Climax community: ecological community in final stage of succession (dynamic equilibrium)

- Communities change overtime because of natural or human disturbances. Organisms would alter their environment and pave the way for other species.

- Whale’s death nurture the ecosystem dramatically, performing as a succession.

Factors preventing Succession

- repeated disturbances, changes in microclimate and soil, chance events such as an introduced invasive species prevent successions.

## 4.5 Aquatic Ecosystems

-Aquatic ecosystems are influenced by factors such as water depth, water temperature, water movement (currents), and nutrient availability.

Water depth and light zones

- Sunlight penetrates only a limited distance through water. The amount of light available changes with depth. Photic Zone
- Photic zone: the sunlit region near the water surface. The name means ‘able to process photosynthesis zone’. In tropical seas it may extend to about 200 m, while in rivers Aphotic Zone and swamps it may be only a few meters deep.

- Phytoplankton are microscopic, photosynthetic organisms, including unicellular algae and cyanobacteria. They are important producers in aquatic food webs.

- Zooplankton are planktonic animals. They feed on other plankton and are part of aquatic food webs.

- Aphotic zone: the region below the photic zone where sunlight is insufficient for photosynthesis. Chemosynthetic producers may be present. Not every aquatic ecosystem has an aphotic zone.

- Benthic zone: the bottom of a lake, stream, or ocean. Benthic organisms live attached to or resting on the bottom. The benthic zone may be within a photic or aphotic region.

Freshwater ecosystems

- The main freshwater ecosystems are rivers and streams, lakes and ponds, and freshwater wetlands.

Rivers and streams

- Headwaters are the source of a river or stream; they may be underground springs or melting snow.

- The mouth is the place where flowing water empties into another body of water.

- Rivers and streams contain running water and generally have high oxygen levels.

Lakes and ponds

- Lakes and ponds are inland bodies of standing water.

- The base of their food webs commonly includes phytoplankton, attached algae, and aquatic plants.

Freshwater wetlands

- Wetlands are areas where water covers the soil or is present at or near the surface for at least part of the year.

- Wetlands are often nutrient-rich and highly productive. They can provide breeding grounds for many organisms and may be used for agriculture.

- Wetlands help purify water by filtering pollutants. They can also reduce flooding by absorbing large amounts of water and releasing it slowly.

- Bogs: wetlands where decaying plant material accumulates as peat; peat moss is characteristic.

- Marshes: wetlands with a variety of reeds and grasses.

- Swamps: wetlands dominated by woody plants and trees.

Estuaries

- An estuary is a transition area between freshwater and saltwater ecosystems, where fresh water mixes with seawater.

- Estuaries provide spawning and nursery grounds for many ecologically and commercially important fish and shellfish, including bluefish, striped bass, shrimp, and crabs.

- Salt marshes are associated with temperate estuaries; mangrove swamps are associated with tropical estuaries.

- Mangroves are trees with roots that extend above the ground. They grow along coasts and the banks of large rivers in hot regions.

Saltwater/Marine ecosystems

- Zones by distance from shore:

- Intertidal zone: the narrow area where the ocean meets the land. It is submerged at high tide and exposed to air and sunlight at low tide. Organisms experience frequent changes and disturbance.

- Some intertidal organisms, such as barnacles and seaweeds, attach themselves permanently to rocks.

- Coastal zone: extends from the low-tide mark to the

outer edge of the continental shelf. It is relatively shallow

and borders the continents. Kelp forests and coral reefs

are important coastal communities.

- Open ocean: begins at the edge of the continental shelf

and extends outward. It makes up about 90% of the

world’s ocean and is divided into photic and aphotic

zones.

- Zones by light and depth in the open ocean:

- Open-ocean photic zone: receives sunlight but

generally has low nutrient levels and supports small phytoplankton. Most photosynthesis in the open ocean occurs in roughly the upper 100 m.

- Open-ocean aphotic zone: permanently dark and includes the deepest parts of the ocean. Deep-sea vents occur here, where superheated water emerges from cracks in the ocean floor.

- Pelagic zone: the water column of the ocean, described in the notes as extending from the surface to about 4,000 m.

- Abyssal zone: the deepest region described in the notes, where the water is cold.

- Benthic zone: the area along the ocean floor.
