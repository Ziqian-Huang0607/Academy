Folk culture

Origin: typically anonymous, with unknown dates and originators; may have multiple independent hearths in isolated locations.

Diffusion: diffuses slowly and on a small scale, usually through relocation diffusion; changes over space

Distribution: clustered in specific, often rural or isolated areas, strongly influenced by local physical and cultural factors. EXAMPLE: distinct folk painting traditions are distributed within the Himalayas as a result of isolation of cultural groups. (Buddhists—divine figures, Hindus —domestic everyday scenes, Muslims—plants and flowers, animists—symbols)

Popular culture

Origin: originates at the hearth, a node of innovation. Usually traceable to a specific person or corporation, often in developed countries.

Diffusion: diffuses rapidly, typically through hierarchical diffusion, aided by modern technology, changes over time

Distribution: widespread across many countries with little regard for environments; main obstacle is income

Key issue 4: Sustainability

Globalization creates sustainability problems for folk culture (struggles to keep unique local traditions) and popular culture (struggles with uniform, unsustainable practices).

Folk Culture Challenges

- Globalization + spread of popular culture make it hard to preserve old, unique local customs.

- Rising incomes make people want popular culture goods; they may abandon traditional folk values.

- Diffusion of popular culture from wealthy countries can push Western ideas over local folk culture.

EXAMPLE: Amish folk culture

Origin: 1600s Swiss Mennonite group (Jakob Ammann) fled Europe for religious freedom.

Migration to North America: two main waves (early 1700s to Pennsylvania; early 1800s to Ohio, Illinois, Iowa, Canada) looking for cheap land.

Cultural traits: Reject most modern electrical/mechanical technology; use horse & buggy, hand farming tools, distinctive clothing, unique religious/farming customs.

Distribution: Spread across rural US (mostly Northeast/Midwest), slow diffusion by migration; isolated communities keep their folk identity separate from mainstream American culture.

Land pressure problem: Traditional Amish custom gives each adult son a farm. Farmland in Lancaster County, PA is expensive and scarce near cities.

Solution: Many Amish sell their Pennsylvania land and move to Kentucky, where farmland is cheaper.

Conflict with popular culture: Tourism brings outsiders to view their folk culture; this forces the Amish into contact with popular culture.

Marriage in India:

Dowry definition: Originally a gift from the bride’s family to the groom’s family as a sign of respect. Historically, it was small, to support the new bride.

Cultural shift: Over time, the custom reversed. Families now expect a large, expensive dowry (cash, cars, electronics, appliances). Dowries became a major income source for the groom’s family.

Government action: India banned dowries in 1961, but the law is widely ignored.

Harm/violence: If the bride’s family cannot pay the promised dowry, the bride may be abused, abandoned, or killed. Statistics show thousands of dowry-related deaths and abuse cases each year.

Cultural trait: A single, distinguishing feature of regular occurrence within a culture, such as using chopsticks.

Cultural complex: A related set of cultural traits describing one aspect of a society's behavior or activity. For example: Americans love cars.

Cultural system: Multiple cultural complexes share common traits (such as ethnicity, language, religion), forming a larger system. For example, the Amish.

Acculturation: a group adopts part of the host society's culture but does not completely lose its own traits.

Assimilation: A group's cultural traits are altered to resemble those of another group, and the immigrant group blends into the host culture, losing many distinctive traits.

Cultural syncretism: cultural blending, e.g., Day of the dead

Cultural relativism: An approach to understanding other cultures that seeks to understand individuals and cultures from a broader perspective of their own cultural logic.

Cultural ethnocentrism: Judging another culture solely by the values and standards of one's own culture.

Ethnic enclave: A place with a high concentration of an ethnic group that is distinct from those in the surrounding area. It can serve as an example of "spatial clustering" in urban settlement. Eg: Little India
