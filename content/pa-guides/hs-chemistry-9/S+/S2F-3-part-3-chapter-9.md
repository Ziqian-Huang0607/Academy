### 3.1 Why Molecular Shape Matters

The shape of a molecule governs:

- Physical properties: boiling point, melting point, density, solubility (polar vs nonpolar solvents)
- Polarity: whether the molecule is polar or nonpolar depends on BOTH bond polarities AND geometry
- Biological function: enzyme active sites, drug-receptor binding, and protein folding all depend on molecular shape
- Reactivity: spatial arrangement of atoms determines how two molecules can approach each other to react

### 3.2 VSEPR Theory — Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion

VSEPR theory is the primary model for predicting molecular geometry. The core principle is simple but powerful:

> **VSEPR Core Principle**
> **Electron pairs (and electron domains) around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves to be as far apart as possible, minimizing repulsion.**
> An ELECTRON DOMAIN = any region of electron density: a single bond, double bond, triple bond, or lone pair each count as ONE domain, regardless of how many electrons are actually there.
> The DOMAIN GEOMETRY (electron geometry) = arrangement of ALL electron domains. The MOLECULAR SHAPE = arrangement of ATOMS only (ignoring lone pairs for naming).

**Repulsion strength (from strongest to weakest):**

- **Lone pair – Lone pair repulsion > Lone pair – Bonding pair > Bonding pair – Bonding pair**

This explains why lone pairs compress bond angles below the ideal geometry value.

> **Example: Why H₂O bond angle is 104.5° not 109.5°**
> H₂O has 4 electron domains (2 bonds + 2 lone pairs) → tetrahedral electron domain geometry.
> Ideal tetrahedral bond angle = 109.5°
> But 2 lone pairs exert extra repulsion on the 2 O-H bonds, pushing them closer together.
> **Result: bond angle compresses to 104.5°. Compare NH₃ (1 lone pair): 107°. More lone pairs = smaller bond angles.**

#### 3.2.1 The 5 Basic Electron Domain Geometries

| **# Electron Domains** | **Domain Geometry** | **Ideal Bond Angles** |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 180° |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120°, 180° |
| 6 | Octahedral | 90°, 180° |

#### 3.2.2 Complete VSEPR Shape Table

The molecular shape is derived from the electron domain geometry by noting how many of those domains are lone pairs vs bonding pairs.

| **Molecular Shape** | **Bonding Domains** | **Lone Pairs** | **Bond Angles** | **Examples** | **Notes** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | 2 | 0 | 180° | CO₂, CS₂, HCN | BeF₂ also (but lone pairs on Be is exception) |
| Trigonal planar | 3 | 0 | 120° | BF₃, BCl₃, CH₂O | B has only 6 e⁻ (exception to octet) |
| Bent | 2 | 1 | ~117.5° | SO₂, NO₂⁻, O₃ | 1 lone pair compresses bond angle slightly below 120° |
| Tetrahedral | 4 | 0 | 109.5° | CH₄, CCl₄, NH₄⁺ | Perfect symmetry, nonpolar if same surrounding atoms |
| Trigonal pyramidal | 3 | 1 | ~107° | NH₃, PCl₃, NF₃ | 1 lone pair compresses from 109.5° |
| Bent | 2 | 2 | ~104.5° | H₂O, H₂S, OF₂ | 2 lone pairs compress bond angle the most of tetrahedral-based shapes |
| Trigonal bipyramidal | 5 | 0 | 90°,120°,180° | PCl₅, PF₅ | Axial bonds (90°) and equatorial bonds (120°) are NOT equivalent |
| Seesaw | 4 | 1 | ~90°,~120° | SF₄, XeO₂F₂ | Lone pair in equatorial position (largest 'seat') |
| T-shaped | 3 | 2 | ~90°,180° | ClF₃, ICl₃ | 2 lone pairs in equatorial positions |
| Linear | 2 | 3 | 180° | XeF₂, I₃⁻ | 3 lone pairs in equatorial positions |
| Octahedral | 6 | 0 | 90°,180° | SF₆, MoF₆ | All bonds equivalent; highly symmetric |
| Square pyramidal | 5 | 1 | ~90° | IF₅, XeOF₄ | 1 lone pair on top or bottom |
| Square planar | 4 | 2 | 90° | XeF₄, ICl₄⁻ | 2 lone pairs opposite each other (180° apart) |

#### 3.2.3 VSEPR Procedure

> **How to Determine Molecular Shape — 4 Steps**
> Step 1: Draw the Lewis structure. Count total valence electrons carefully.
> Step 2: Count electron domains on the central atom. (lone pairs + bonds = total domains)
> Step 3: Identify the electron domain geometry from the 5 basic geometries.
> Step 4: Name the molecular shape based on atom positions only (ignore lone pairs). Estimate bond angles (compress by ~2.5° for each lone pair in tetrahedral family).

> **Example 3.2: Determine the shape of XeF₄**
> Step 1: Lewis structure: Xe is central. Total VE = 8 + 4(7) = 36. Place 4 Xe-F bonds (8 e⁻). Complete F octets (24 e⁻). Remaining: 36-8-24=4 e⁻ = 2 lone pairs on Xe.
> Step 2: Electron domains on Xe = 4 bonds + 2 lone pairs = 6 total domains
> Step 3: 6 electron domains → OCTAHEDRAL domain geometry
> **Step 4: 2 lone pairs arrange opposite each other (180°) to minimize lone pair-lone pair repulsion. The 4 F atoms form a square. Molecular shape = SQUARE PLANAR. Bond angles = 90°.**

#### 3.2.4 Shapes of Larger Molecules

For molecules with more than one central atom, determine the geometry around EACH central atom separately using VSEPR. The overall molecular shape is described by listing each atom's geometry.

> **Example 3.4: Larger molecule with two central atoms**
> **Consider CH₃CHO** **:**
> First carbon (CH₃): 4 single bonds, 0 lone pairs → TETRAHEDRAL (109.5°)
> Second carbon (CHO): 2 single bonds + 1 double bond (=O) = 3 domains → TRIGONAL PLANAR (120°)
> The overall molecule is NOT flat, because the CH₃ end is tetrahedral. Only the CHO end is planar.

### 3.3 Molecular Polarity

A polar molecule (also called a dipole) has a positive end and a negative end due to an uneven distribution of electron density. Whether a molecule is polar depends on TWO factors: bond polarity AND molecular geometry.

> **Key Rule for Molecular Polarity**
> **A molecule is POLAR if:**
> 1. It has polar bonds (ΔEN > 0), AND
> 2. The bond dipoles do NOT cancel each other out due to molecular geometry (asymmetric molecule).
> **A molecule is NONPOLAR if:**
> 1. All bonds are nonpolar (same elements), OR
> 2. It has polar bonds but they CANCEL perfectly due to symmetric geometry.

**Symmetric shapes where bond dipoles cancel (nonpolar IF all surrounding atoms are the same):**

- Linear (2 identical bonds): CO₂ , two C=O dipoles point opposite directions and cancel → NONPOLAR
- Trigonal planar (3 identical bonds): BF₃ , three B-F dipoles arranged 120° apart, vector sum = 0 → NONPOLAR
- Tetrahedral (4 identical bonds): CH₄ , four C-H dipoles cancel perfectly → NONPOLAR
- Trigonal bipyramidal (5 identical bonds): PCl₅ → NONPOLAR
- Octahedral (6 identical bonds): SF₆ → NONPOLAR
- Square planar (4 identical bonds): XeF₄ → NONPOLAR

**Asymmetric shapes where bond dipoles do NOT cancel (polar if bonds are polar):**

- Bent: H₂O , 2 lone pairs push the molecule into a V-shape; O-H dipoles add up, not cancel → POLAR
- Trigonal pyramidal: NH₃ , the lone pair on N means the N-H dipoles don't cancel → POLAR
- Seesaw, T-shaped, Square pyramidal: all have lone pairs creating asymmetry → typically POLAR

> **Example: Determine if CH₂Cl₂ is polar or nonpolar**
> Step 1: Lewis structure → C is central, bonded to 2 H and 2 Cl. 4 bonding domains, 0 lone pairs.
> Step 2: Molecular shape = TETRAHEDRAL (4 bonds, no lone pairs)
> Step 3: Bond polarity? C-H: ΔEN = 0.4 (barely polar). C-Cl: ΔEN = 0.5 (polar, Cl is more electronegative).
> Step 4: Even though the geometry is tetrahedral, the surrounding atoms are NOT all identical (2 H + 2 Cl). The C-Cl dipoles point toward Cl, and the C-H dipoles are weaker. The net dipole ≠ 0.
> **Conclusion: CH₂Cl₂ is POLAR. (Compare with CCl₄** **,** **4 identical C-Cl bonds in tetrahedral geometry → nonpolar)**

> **Example: XeF₄ (Square Planar)**
> XeF₄: octahedral domain geometry, 4 bonds, 2 lone pairs → SQUARE PLANAR molecular shape
> The 2 lone pairs are on OPPOSITE sides (top and bottom), canceling each other's effect.
> The 4 Xe-F bonds are in the same plane, 90° apart (opposite bonds cancel, adjacent bonds also cancel.)
> **Conclusion: XeF₄ is NONPOLAR despite having polar Xe-F bonds! The square planar geometry makes all bond dipoles cancel perfectly.**

### 3.4 Hybrid Orbitals (Section 9.5)

> **What is Hybridization?**
> Hybridization = the mixing of atomic orbitals on an atom to form a new set of 'hybrid' orbitals that are all equivalent in energy and shape.
> **Key rule: The NUMBER of hybrid orbitals formed = the number of atomic orbitals mixed.**
> **The hybridization of a central atom ALWAYS matches its electron domain geometry.**

#### 3.4.1 sp Hybridization (2 electron domains → LINEAR)

When 1 s orbital and 1 p orbital mix, they form 2 sp hybrid orbitals arranged at 180° (linear).

The 2 remaining unhybridized p orbitals are perpendicular to each other and to the bond axis.

> **sp Hybridization in BeF₂**
> Be ground state: [He] 2s² (no unpaired electrons — can't form bonds!)
> After sp hybridization: mix 2s + one 2p → 2 sp hybrids at 180° apart + 2 unhybridized p orbitals
> Each sp hybrid has 1 unpaired electron → forms σ bond with F
> **Result: BeF₂ is LINEAR with 180° bond angle ✓ (matches VSEPR prediction for 2 electron domains)**

#### 3.4.2 sp² Hybridization (3 electron domains → TRIGONAL PLANAR)

When 1 s orbital and 2 p orbitals mix, they form 3 sp² hybrid orbitals arranged at 120° (trigonal planar).

The 1 remaining unhybridized p orbital is perpendicular to the plane of the molecule and participates in π bonding.

> **sp² Hybridization in BF₃**
> B ground state: [He] 2s² 2p¹ (only 1 unpaired electron normally)
> After sp² hybridization: mix 2s + two 2p → 3 sp² hybrids at 120° apart + 1 unhybridized p orbital
> Each sp² hybrid forms a σ bond with F. B has only 6 electrons (exception to octet).
> **Result: BF₃ is TRIGONAL PLANAR with 120° bond angle ✓**

#### 3.4.3 sp³ Hybridization (4 electron domains → TETRAHEDRAL)

When 1 s orbital and 3 p orbitals mix, they form 4 sp³ hybrid orbitals arranged at 109.5° (tetrahedral).

> **sp³ in Molecules with Lone Pairs: NH₃ and H₂O**
> NH₃: N has 4 electron domains (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) → sp³ hybridized. 3 sp³ hybrids hold σ bonds with H; 1 sp³ hybrid holds the lone pair.
> H₂O: O has 4 electron domains (2 bonds + 2 lone pairs) → sp³ hybridized. 2 sp³ hybrids hold σ bonds with H; 2 sp³ hybrids hold lone pairs.
> **IMPORTANT: The hybridization is still sp³ for both! The lone pairs occupy sp³ orbitals just like the bonding pairs do. The MOLECULAR SHAPE is different (trigonal pyramidal for NH₃, bent for H₂O), but the HYBRIDIZATION is the same.**

#### 3.4.4 sp³d and sp³d² Hybridization (5 and 6 electron domains)

Elements in Period 3 and beyond can use d orbitals in hybridization, allowing 5 or 6 electron domains. This is why only Period 3+ elements form expanded octets.

| **Hybridization** | **Electron Domains** | **Domain Geometry & Angles** | **Examples** |
|---|---|---|---|
| sp | 2 | Linear, 180° | BeF₂, CO₂, HCN, C₂H₂ |
| sp² | 3 | Trigonal planar, 120° | BF₃, C₂H₄, CH₂O, SO₃ (resonance hybrid) |
| sp³ | 4 | Tetrahedral, 109.5° | CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, CCl₄, PCl₃ |
| sp³d | 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | PCl₅, SF₄, ClF₃, XeF₂ |
| sp³d² | 6 | Octahedral | SF₆, IF₅, XeF₄ |

#### 3.4.5 How to Determine Hybridization

> **Hybridization Shortcut**
> **Count the total number of electron domains on the central atom:**
> **2 domains → sp \| 3 domains → sp² \| 4 domains → sp³**
> **5 domains → sp³d \| 6 domains → sp³d²**
> Remember: electron DOMAINS include lone pairs! (domains = bonds + lone pairs)

> **Example 3.7: Determine hybridization of each atom in CH₂O (formaldehyde)**
> Lewis structure: H₂C=O → C has 3 electron domains (2 single bonds to H + 1 double bond to O)
> **C: 3 domains → sp² hybridized. The unhybridized p on C forms the π bond with O.**
> O: has 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs = 4 domains → sp³ hybridized.
> In practice: treat each atom separately. C in C=O with no lone pairs has 3 domains → sp². The O bonded to C with double bond can be treated as sp² or sp³ depending on whether lone pairs are in hybrid or unhybridized orbitals (in this context, treat O as sp² since it participates in π bond).

> **Example 3.8: HCN**
> Lewis structure: H–C≡N (C has triple bond to N, single bond to H)
> **C: 2 electron domains (1 single bond to H + 1 triple bond to N) → sp hybridized**
> **N: 2 electron domains (1 triple bond to C + 1 lone pair) → sp hybridized**
> Molecular shape: LINEAR. Both C and N are sp hybridized. The triple bond consists of 1 σ bond (sp-sp overlap) + 2 π bonds (two perpendicular p-p sideways overlaps).

### 3.5 Multiple Bonds

Multiple bonds combine one sigma (σ) bond with one or more pi (π) bonds. Understanding this distinction is crucial for hybridization, resonance, and molecular geometry.

#### 3.5.1 Sigma (σ) Bonds

- Formed by direct (head-on) overlap of orbitals along the bond axis.
- Cylindrical symmetry of electron density around the internuclear axis.
- ALL single bonds are sigma bonds.
- Every double and triple bond contains exactly ONE sigma bond.
- Sigma bonds are FREE TO ROTATE, and this allows single bonds in a molecule to flex.
- Sigma bonds can be formed by: s-s, s-p, s-sp, sp-sp, sp-sp², sp²-sp², sp³-sp³, etc.

#### 3.5.2 Pi (π) Bonds

- Formed by sideways (lateral) overlap of two parallel p orbitals.
- Electron density is found ABOVE and BELOW the bond axis (in two lobes).
- WEAKER than sigma bonds (less overlap efficiency in sideways orientation).
- Pi bonds CANNOT rotate freely, so that rotation would destroy the sideways overlap.
- A double bond = 1 σ + 1 π. A triple bond = 1 σ + 2 π.
- **Pi bonds require unhybridized p orbitals to be available. It’s** **only possible if the central atom is sp or sp² hybridized (sp³ hybridization uses all p orbitals in hybridization, leaving none for π).**

> **Important:** **σ** **vs** **π** **Bond Summary**
> **Single bond: 1σ, 0π** **\| Double bond: 1σ, 1π** **\| Triple bond: 1σ, 2π**
> Count σ bonds: = number of single bonds + 1 for each double bond + 1 for each triple bond
> Count π bonds: = 0 for single bonds + 1 per double bond + 2 per triple bond
> In acetylene (H-C≡C-H): 3 σ bonds (H-C, C-C, C-H) + 2 π bonds (the 2 extra bonds in the triple bond)

#### 3.5.3 Resonance, Delocalization, and Pi Bonding

When resonance occurs, the π electrons are not confined to one location but spread (delocalize) over multiple atoms. This concept connects Chapter 8 resonance structures with the quantum mechanical reality of π bonding.

> **Delocalized π Bonding**
> Delocalization occurs when p orbitals on multiple adjacent atoms overlap, spreading the π electron density over the entire system rather than between just two atoms.
> Conditions for delocalization: ALL atoms in the sequence must have an unhybridized p orbital available (i.e., sp or sp² hybridized).
> **Evidence: all bonds in a resonance-stabilized structure have equal lengths and intermediate bond orders (greater than a single bond, less than a double bond).**

> **Example 3.10: Delocalized π bonding in NO₃⁻**
> N is sp² hybridized (3 electron domains: 1 double bond + 2 single bonds). Each O is also sp² hybridized.
> N has 1 unhybridized p orbital perpendicular to the plane of the molecule. Each O ALSO has an unhybridized p orbital in the same plane (perpendicular orientation).
> All four p orbitals (N + 3 O) overlap side-by-side, creating a delocalized π system that spans ALL four atoms.
> **Result: 2 π electrons are spread equally over all 3 N-O bonds. Bond order = 1 + (2/6) ≈ 1.33 (between single and double). All 3 N-O bonds are identical — confirmed experimentally.**
