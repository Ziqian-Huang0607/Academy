### 2.1 Lewis Symbols and the Octet Rule

A Lewis symbol for an element consists of the chemical symbol plus one dot for each valence electron.

The octet rule states that atoms tend to form bonds to achieve 8 valence electrons.

### 2.2 Bond Types — Ionic, Covalent, Metallic

The type of bond formed between two atoms depends primarily on the difference in electronegativity (ΔEN) between them.

| **Electronegativity Difference** | **Bond Type** | **Examples** |
|---|---|---|
| ΔEN ≤ 0.4 | Nonpolar Covalent | Cl₂, N₂, H₂, C-H bonds |
| 0.4 < ΔEN < 2.0 | Polar Covalent | HCl (ΔEN=0.9), H₂O, NH₃ |
| ΔEN ≥ 2.0 | Ionic | NaCl (ΔEN=2.1), MgO |
| Metal + Metal | Metallic | Na metal, Cu wire, steel |

### 2.3 Drawing Lewis Structures

Lewis structures show all valence electrons as dots or lines (bonding pairs as lines, lone pairs as dots). Mastering this is CRITICAL for Chapter 9, since VSEPR and hybridization are both based on Lewis structures.

> **5-Step Guide to Drawing Lewis Structures**
> Step 1: Count total valence electrons. (Add 1 e⁻ per negative charge; subtract 1 per positive charge)
> Step 2: Choose central atom (forms most bonds). (H) is always a terminal atom.
> Step 3: Connect surrounding atoms to the central atom with single bonds. Each bond uses 2 electrons.
> Step 4: Complete octets of surrounding atoms (except H — give it 2).
> Step 5: Place remaining electrons on the central atom. If central atom lacks an octet, form double or triple bonds by relocating lone pairs from surrounding atoms.

> **Example: Lewis Structure of CO₂**
> Step 1: Valence electrons = C(4) + O(6) + O(6) = 16 total
> Step 2: C is central (less electronegative than O)
> Step 3: O-C-O with single bonds uses 4 electrons (12 left)
> Step 4: Give each O 3 lone pairs (uses 12 electrons). C has only 2 bonds = 4 electrons. Needs 4 more!
> **Step 5: Move 1 lone pair from each O → C to form double bonds: O=C=O**
> Final: :O=C=O: Each O has 2 lone pairs, C has no lone pairs. All octets satisfied. Total electrons used: 16 ✓

### 2.4 Formal Charge

Formal charge (FC) helps choose the best Lewis structure when multiple valid structures exist. It assumes electrons in bonds are shared EQUALLY between the bonded atoms.

> **Formal Charge Formula**
> **Formal Charge = (Valence electrons of neutral atom) − (Non-bonding electrons) − ½(Bonding electrons)**
> Or: FC = V − N − B/2
> The best Lewis structure: (1) minimizes formal charges on all atoms, (2) puts negative FC on more electronegative atoms, (3) has total FC equal to the ion charge (or zero for neutral molecules).

> **Example 2.4: Formal Charge in CO₂**
> **In O=C=O (two double bonds):**
> C: V=4, N=0 lone pairs, B=8 bonding electrons → FC = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0
> Each O: V=6, N=4 (2 lone pairs), B=4 bonding electrons → FC = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0
> **All formal charges = 0 → This is the BEST structure!**
> Compare with single bond O–C≡O: left O has FC=-1, right O has FC=+1 → not ideal because negative FC is on less electronegative C side.

### 2.5 Resonance Structures

When more than one valid Lewis structure can be written for a molecule or ion, differing only in the placement of electrons (not atoms), these are called resonance structures. The actual molecule is a resonance hybrid — a blend of all resonance structures.

**Key features of resonance:**

- All bonds in a resonance-stabilized structure are equivalent.

### 2.6 Exceptions to the Octet Rule

Three categories of exceptions exist — understanding them is critical because Chapter 9 features molecules like SF₆and PCl₅with expanded octets.

| **Exception Type** | **Explanation** | **Examples** |
|---|---|---|
| Odd electron (radical) | Molecules with an odd total number of valence electrons cannot follow the octet rule for all atoms. | NO (11 electrons), NO₂ (17 electrons), ClO₂ |
| Fewer than 8 electrons | Some elements in Period 2 with few electrons form stable molecules before filling an octet. | BF₃ (B has 6 electrons), BeCl₂ (Be has 4 electrons) |
| Expanded octet (> 8 e⁻) | Elements from Period 3 onwards can use empty d-orbitals, allowing the central atom to hold more than 8 valence electrons (expanded octet). | PCl₅ (10 e⁻ on P), SF₆ (12 e⁻ on S), XeF₄ (12 e⁻ on Xe) |

### 2.7 Bond Properties: Bond Order, Length, and Energy

These bond properties are the link between Chapter 8 and Chapter 9's discussion of hybrid orbitals and multiple bonds.

| **Property** | **Definition** | **Relationship** |
|---|---|---|
| Bond Order | Number of shared electron pairs between two atoms | Single=1, Double=2, Triple=3 |
| Bond Length | Distance between two bonded nuclei at minimum energy | Single > Double > Triple (LONGER) |
| Bond Energy | Energy required to break one mole of bonds in gas phase | Single < Double < Triple (STRONGER) |
| Sigma (σ) bond | Formed by head-on orbital overlap with cylindrical electron density symmetry. | All single bonds; 1 in each double/triple bond |
| Pi (π) bond | Formed by sideways overlap of parallel p-orbitals; electron density lies above and below the bond axis. | 1 in double bond; 2 in triple bond |

### 2.8 Chemical Bonding (Ionic, Covalent, Metallic)

### 2.8.1 Ionic Bonding

Electrostatic attraction between cations (+) and anions (−); formed by electron transfer.

It forms between metals + nonmetals (large electronegativity difference ≥ 2.0).

**Ion Formation:**

- Cation: Metals lose valence electrons. Transition metals form multiple cations (use Roman numerals).
- Anion: Nonmetals gain electrons; charge = group number − 8. Suffix changes to -ide.

**Key Properties:**

High melting/boiling points, hard & brittle.

Solid: non-conductive; molten/aqueous: conductive.

Exist as formula units (simplest ion ratio).

Lattice Energy:

Energy to separate 1 mol solid ionic compound into gaseous ions.

Higher ion charge / smaller ion size → larger lattice energy → stronger ionic bond.

## 2.8.2 Covalent Bonding

Electrostatic attraction between shared valence electrons and atomic nuclei; formed by electron sharing.

Formed by electron sharing between nonmetals (ΔEN < 2.0).

- More shared pairs → shorter bond length → higher bond energy
- Larger atoms → longer, weaker bonds

**Two Covalent Structures**

1. Molecular covalent: Discrete molecules, weak intermolecular forces, low melting/boiling points.

2. Giant (network) covalent: Atoms bonded by continuous covalent bonds, extremely high melting points.

**Octet Rule Exceptions**

1. Odd valence electrons

2. Less than 8 electrons

3. Expanded octet (3rd period & below, use d-orbitals; e.g. PCl5, SF6).

**2.8.3. Metallic Bonding**

Attraction between metal cations and delocalized "sea of valence electrons".

Exists in pure metals and metal alloys.

**Key Properties**

Good thermal & electrical conductivity (free moving electrons).

Malleable & ductile (cations slide easily, and no repulsion).

Most are solid at room temperature (exception: Hg = liquid), metallic luster.

**Bond Strength**

↑ delocalized electrons + ↓ ionic radius = ↑ metallic bonds = ↑ melting points.

## 2.9 Naming Chemical Compounds and Formulas

**Binary Ionic (Metal + Single Nonmetal Ion):**

Rules：

1. Name cation first (same as element name).

2. Name anion with suffix -ide.

3. Transition metals (multiple charges): add Roman numeral for cation charge.

Formula Writing: Balance total positive and negative charges (crisscross method).

**Ionic Compounds with Polyatomic Ions:**

Polyatomic ions act as one unit, and use parentheses if more than one is needed.

**Suffix rules for oxyanions:**

- -ate: more oxygen; -ite: less oxygen
- per- + -ate: most oxygen; hypo- + -ite: fewest oxygen

**Binary Molecular Compounds (Two Nonmetals):**

Prefixes:mono(1), di(2), tri(3), tetra(4), penta(5), hexa(6)…

Rules:

1. Use prefixes for each element and omit mono- for the first element.

2. Second element ends with -ide.
