## CH 8.1 Covalent Bonding

**The Nature of Covalent Bonding**

- Ionic bonds form when there is electrons transferred between metals and nonmetals.
- A covalent bond forms when atoms share electrons
- Elements in Groups 4A, 5A, 6A, 7A (nonmetals & metalloids) tend to form covalent bonds.
- Atoms share electrons to achieve noble gas electron configurations in covalent bonds, while atoms give up ions and some gain ions to form an ionic bond.

**Multiple covalent bonds**

- Single covalent bond: one pair of shared electrons. Examples: H2 and CH4
- Double covalent bond: two pairs of shared electrons. Example: C=O in CO2
- Triple covalent bond: three pairs of shared electrons. Example: N≡N in N2

**Rules for drawing Lewis structures**

- Calculate total valence electrons
- Connect atoms with single bonds
- Complete octets for outer atoms
- Place remaining electrons on the central atom;
- form multiple bonds if electrons are insufficient in CO3

**Coordinate covalent bonds**

- Definition: One atom provides both electrons for the shared pair.
- Examples: CO}, NH4+, H3O+
- Polyatomic ions: Charged groups of atoms acting as a single unit.
- Compounds containing polyatomic ions have both ionic and covalent bonds.

**Exceptions to the octet rule**

- Molecules with an odd total number of valence electrons, e.g. NO2, where there are 5+6+6=17 e-
- Central atoms with fewer than 8 valence electrons, BF3 (any trigonal planar)
- Central atoms with more than 8 valence electrons ( some elements after Period 4, for example SF4)

**Bond dissociation energy** **and bond length**

- Definition: Energy required to break one mole of covalent bonds, unit: kJ/mol
- A larger bond dissociation energy means a stronger bond and shorter bond length.
- Down a group: Bond length increases, bond energy decreases.
- Bond length: Single bond > Double bond > Triple bond
- Bond strength: Single bond < Double bond < Triple bond
- The two are opposites

**Naming & formulas of ionic compounds with polyatomic ions**

- ◦ Naming suffix rules
- -ate: ions with more oxygen atoms; -ite: ions with fewer oxygen atoms
- per- + -ate: the most oxygen atoms; hypo- + -ite: the fewest oxygen atoms
- Common polyatomic ions: CO3^2-, HCO3-, PO4^3-, OH-, CN-, NH4+
- Formula writing: Use parentheses for repeated polyatomic ions.

**Molecules and molecular compounds**

- A molecule: A neutral group substance composed of non – metallic or metalloid atoms by sharing valence electrons through covalent bonds. A molecule could be an element (e.g. H2, O2, Br2…)
- Diatomic molecule: A molecule composed of two atoms. (e.g. HCl, O2…)
- Molecular compound: A molecule containing two or more kinds of nonmetallic atoms formed by a covalent bond

**Representation of molecules**

- Molecular formula: Shows the actual number of each atom in a molecule; subscripts are not always the simplest whole-number ratio.
- Structural formula: Lewis Structure, shows how atoms are bonded together
- ball-and-stick model: Display molecular spatial structure and atom arrangement.
- Formula unit: The simplest whole-number ratio of ions for ionic compounds.

**Comparison: Molecular compounds & Ionic compounds**

- Basic unit: Molecule / Formula unit
- Constituent elements: Nonmetals only / Metal + Nonmetal
- Melting & boiling points: Relatively low (gas/liquid/solid at room temperature) / Relatively high

**Naming molecular compounds**

- Naming rules: List elements in formula order; use prefixes for atom numbers; add suffix -ide to the second element, while taking last part of the element out.
- Prefixes: mono-(1), di-(2), tri-(3), tetra-(4), penta-(5), hexa-(6), hepta-(7), octa-(8), nona-(9), deca-(10)
- For example: SF4 is Sulfur tetrafluoride
- Special cases: Omit mono- for the first element; H2O} (water), NH3 (ammonia), NH4^1+ (ammonium) CH4 (methane) have fixed common names.

**Molecular Orbitals**

- Atomic orbitals overlap to form molecular orbitals. A bonding orbital holds two bonding electrons of a covalent bond.
- Sigma bond (σ)
- Formed by head-on overlap of atomic orbitals, symmetrical along the bond axis.
- Formed by s-s, p-p or s-p orbital overlap.
- Pi bond (π)
- Formed by side-by-side overlap of p orbitals. Electrons lie above and below the bond axis.
- Weaker than sigma bonds due to less orbital overlap.
- One Bond = 1 σ bond; Double bond = 1 σ bond + 1 π bond; Triple bond = 1 σ bond + 2 π bonds (the sigma bond is surrounded by 2 pi bonds while being at the center).

**VSEPR** **(Valence Shell Electron Pairs Repulsion)** **Theory**

- Core principle: Valence electron pairs repel each other and stay as far apart as possible, determining molecular shapes.
- Shared pair means 1 bond for anyone who doesn’t understand
- Lone pair & Lone pair have largest repulsion
- Shared Pair & Lone pair have medium repulsion
- Shared pair and Shared pair have smallest repulsion.
- Linear shape: Bond angle 180°, examples: N2 (all molecules with 2 atoms are linear)
- Trigonal planar shape: Bond angle 120°, example: BCl3
- Tetrahedral shape: Bond angle 109.5°, example: CH4
- Pyramidal shape: Bond angle 107°, example: NH3 (one unshared electron pair on central atom, unlike trigonal planar with no lone pair on central atom)
- Bent shape: Bond angle 104.5°, example: H2O (one or two unshared electron pairs on central atom)

**Hybrid Orbitals** **/ Hybridization**

- Definition: Atomic orbitals mix to form equivalent hybrid orbitals during bonding. Hybridization relies on orbital overlap & electron sharing — the core of covalent bonding. Ionic bonding has no such orbital interaction, so hybridization does not occur.
- sp³ hybridization: 1 s orbital + 3 p orbitals → 4 sp³ orbitals; Tetrahedral shape (109.5°), CH4, NH3, H2O
- sp² hybridization: 1 s orbital + 2 p orbitals → 3 sp² orbitals; Trigonal planar shape (120°)
- sp hybridization: 1 s orbital + 1 p orbital → 2 sp orbitals; Linear shape (180°),

**How to find hybridization?**

- count how many covalent bonds
- Single bond, double bond, triple bond → all = 1 bond when calculating hybridization
- One lone pair of electrons → 1 lone domain
- the x in sp^x = number of bonding pairs + number of lone pairs - 1
- CH4 has four single bonds which means 4 bonding pairs. No lone pairs, so 4+0-1 = 3. Thus the hybridization is Sp^3.
- H2O has two pairs of bonds, and on the central atom oxygen there is 2 lone pairs. 2+ 2-1 = 3 sp^3
- N2 has three covalent bonds (but when calculating hybridization this triple bond counts as one bond) and it has one lone pair on one of the atoms. 1+1-1 = 1 sp^1 = sp

## 8.4 Polar bonds and molecules

**Electronegativity & Bond Polarity**

- Electronegativity: The ability of an atom to attract electrons when forming a chemical bond within a compound
- Used to predict bond type
- Nonpolar covalent bond: Electronegativity difference between the two atoms ≤ 0.4
- Polar covalent bond: 0.4 < Electronegativity difference between the two atoms < 2.0
- Ionic bond: Electronegativity difference between the two atoms ≥ 2.0
- Representation of polar bonds: Use δ⁺ / δ⁻ ( lowercase delta – and delta +) for partial charges, or an arrow pointing to the more electronegative atom. Include a vertical line above the less electronegative atom

**Molecular polarity**

- Polar Molecule: molecule in which the centers of positive and negative charge do not coincide; a molecule that possesses a nonzero dipole moment.
- Polarity can be interpreted: When an electric field is absent. NonPolar molecules orient randomly, while polar molecules also. But with a field, polar molecules align orderly: Their positive sides point to other negative sides.
- If there are no lone pairs on central atom, and all surrounding atoms are same element, the molecule is nonpolar. Otherwise, it is polar.
- Symmetric molecules: No lone pairs on central atom + same surrounding atoms
- Asymmetric molecules: not all pairs of electrons are shared due to uneven molecular shape.
- Bent & pyramidal molecules are always polar.
- Linear, trigonal planar and tetrahedral molecules with identical outer atoms are nonpolar.

**Intermolecular forces (Van der Waals forces)**

- Dispersion forces: Caused by electron motion; the weakest intermolecular force; exist in all molecules. Strength increases with the number of electrons.
- Dipole interactions: Attraction between partial charges of polar molecules; stronger than dispersion forces.
- Hydrogen bonding: A special strong dipole interaction between H (δ⁺) and F/O/N (δ⁻); the strongest intermolecular force.

**Comparison: Ionic & Molecular Compounds**

- Representative unit: Formula unit / Molecule
- Bond formation: Electron transfer / Electron sharing
- Elements involved: Metal + Nonmetal / Nonmetals only
- Physical state: Solid / Solid, liquid or gas
- Melting point: High / Low
- Electrical conductivity: Good in aqueous state / Poor or non-conducting
