**7-4 Ionization Energy**

-Ionization Energy: Amount of energy required to remove an electron (valence first) from the ground state of a gaseous atom or ion.

=GASEOUS: to exclude intermolecular forces

=REQUIRED: Always positive

=First Ionization Energy (IE1): Energy required to remove the first valence electron

Unit: J/atom, kJ/mol

Reactant Side Li(g)Li(g)++e- Product

**Trend**

-Down a Group:

=1) Zeff is the same

=2) Valence electrons farther from the nucleus (see atomic radius 7-3 for detail, shielding effect)

=3) Less energy required—Decrease

-Across a Row (Left to Right):

=1) Zeff increases with more protons in the nucleus

=2) Harder to remove electron—Increase

-First to Second Ionization Energy (same for second to third, third to fourth, etc.):

=1) Removing an electron from a more positively charged ion

=2) Requires more energy to remove each successive electron—Increase

=When all valence electrons have been removed, ionization energy takes a quantum leap to break through another shell

**Discontinuities**

1)2A to 3A—Decrease

2A [ns2] 3A [ns2 np1]

-Electron removed from p-orbital rather than s-orbital

=(p-orbital) At a higher energy level and less attracted to the nucleus

2)5A to 6A—Decrease

5A [ns2 np3] 6A [ns2 np4]

**7-5 Electron Affinity and Electronegativity**

**Electron Affinity**

-Electron Affinity: Energy change accompanying addition of electrons to a gaseous atom

=CHANGE: ‘+’: Absorb; ‘-’: Release

-The more negative the electron affinity, the greater the attraction to the added electron

Ne(g)+eNe-(g)

**Trend**

-Down a Group:

=1) Zeff is the same

=2) Greater atomic radius

=3) Harder to add electron—More positive

-Across a Row:

=1) Zeff increases, higher attraction

=2) Releases energy—More negative/Exothermic

**Discontinuities**

1)1A to 2A—Less Negative

1A [ns1] 2A [ns2]

-For 2A, the added electron must go in the p-orbital (farther from the nucleus and requires more energy)

=Electron feels repulsion from s-orbital electrons

2)4A to 5A—Less Negative

4A [ns2 np2] 5A [ns2 np3]

-5A has no empty orbitals, so the extra electron must go into an occupied orbital

=Repulsion exists, more energy required

**Electronegativity (EN)**

-Electronegativity: The ability of an atom to attract bonding electrons when the atom is in a compound

=Using the Pauling Scale

**Trend**

-Down a Group:

=1) Zeff is the same

=2) Valence shell is less strongly held by the nucleus (increasing shielding effect)

=3) Harder to attract electrons—Decrease

-Across a Row (for representative elements):

=1) Zeff increases, greater attraction

=2) Easier to attract electrons—Increase

-Highest (Strong ability to gain): F

-Lowest (Strong ability to lose): Fr

**7-6 Metal, Nonmetals, and Metalloids**

-Metals tend to form cations(+)

-Nonmetals tend to form anions(-)

Exceptions: Hydrogen is a nonmetal but can form both cation and anion

**Metals**

-Lustrous—Shine

-Malleable: Can be forced into any shape

-Ductile: Can be drawn into wires

-Good conductors of heat and electricity

-Mostly solid at room temperature (25 degrees Celsius)

=Exception: Mercury Hg(L)

-Compounds formed between metals and nonmetals tend to be ionic

=Due to a great difference in EN (Metals usually lower and nonmetals higher)

-Metal oxides tend to be basic

**Nonmentals**

-Dull, brittle (for those in the solid state) substances

-Poor conductors of heat and electricity

-odorless, colorless gas (for those in the gas state), except F and Cl

-Tend to gain electrons in reactions with metals to acquire noble gas configurations

-Molecular Compound: A substance containing only nonmetals and forming covalent bonds

-Most nonmetal oxides are acidic

**Metalloids**

-Under some conditions behave as metal, some as nonmetal

=e.g., Si looks shiny, has metallic luster, acts as a semiconductor, has high hardness, and has a relatively high melting point

-B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po

**8-1 Lewis Symbol and Octet Rule**

-Chemical Bond: The electrostatic (positive-negative) attraction force that holds two atoms together

=Ionic Bond: Attraction between cations and anions—Metal and Nonmetal

=Covalent Bond: Attraction between the nucleus and shared electrons—Nonmetal and Nonmetal

=Metallic Bond: Attraction between metal cations and delocalized electrons (shared among more than 2 atoms in a molecule)—Metal and Metal

-The formation of a chemical bond helps decrease chemical potential energy

-Octet Rule: All elements tend to have electron configurations consisting of 8 electrons in the outermost shell—Stable Octet

=Except H, He

-Lewis Symbols: Element’s chemical symbol + a dot to represent each valence electron

=First on four sides, then get paired

**8-2 Ionic Bonding**

**Cation**

-Elements with low EN tend to form positive ions by losing electrons (metals, H)

e.g., Na ([Ne] 3s1) Na+ ([He]2s2 2p6)

-For representative elements

- metals: the charge of its ion = number of valence electrons (group number)
- nonmetals: the charge of its ion = number of valence electrons (group number) - 8

-For transition elements, they form various ions with different charges, because d-orbitals and f-orbitals are involved

=2+ and 3+ are common (e.g., Cr, Fe)

-For elements 1B to 4A in period 4 to 6, they form pseudo-noble gas notation, which is relatively stable

=e.g., Pb ([Xe] 4f14 5d10 6s2 6p2), if electrons from 6p orbital are lost, it will have a 2+ charge; if from 6p and 6s orbitals are lost, it will have a 4+ charge

**Anion**

-Elements with high EN tend to form anions by gaining electrons (nonmetals)

e.g., Cl ([Ne] 3s2 3p5)Cl-([Ne] 3s2 3p6)

-For nonmetal, the charge = difference between its valence electrons and 8

**Naming Ions**

-Monatomic ions: Consist of a single atom with a positive or negative charge resulting from the loss or gain of 1 or more valence electrons

-Cations: Names as elements, followed by the word “ion” or “cation”

e.g., Na+, Sodium ion (or cation)

-Anions: Start with the stem of the element name and end in -ide

e.g., F-, Fluoride

-Metals that form more than 1 ion (Transition metals): Place a Roman numeral in parentheses

e.g., Fe2+, Iron (II) Ion

Exceptions: Zn2+ Zinc Ion, Ag+ Silver Ion (they have only 1 common charge)

**Naming Ionic Compounds**

-Ionic Compounds are electically neutralCharges of cation+anion must balance

-Exist as collections of positively and negatively charged ions arranged in a repeating pattern

AXBY

-Composed of 2 elements: A-metal, B-nonmetal

-x,y: Subscripts needed to balance the charge (crisscriss method, but remember to simplify and leave the smallest ratio)

=Formula Unit: The lowest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic compound

-Place cation first, then anion

-If the metallic element has more than one common ionic charge, a Roman numeral must be included

e.g., CuOCopper (II) Oxide

**Properties**

-High melting point/boiling point and hardness

=+,- have high attraction, and are difficult to break bonds

-Brittle: Ordered crystal lattice structure

=When a force is exerted, layers shift, and the same charges will be in corresponding positions and repel each other

-Solid does not conduct electricity

=Ions are in a fixed position

-Molten or aqueous solutions of ionic conductors can conduct

=Free-moving ions, and for aqueous solutions, water binds to the ions, and compounds dissolve

-Ionic crystals vary in shape due to size and the relative numbers of ions bonded

-Lattice Energy: Energy required to completely separate one mole of a solid ionic compound into its gaseous ions

NaCl(s)Na+(g)+Cl-(g)

-The strength of the ionic bond

-Greater attraction force between cations and anions, greater lattice energy (more positive)

-Larger Charges: Greater attractionMore positive lattice energy

-Smaller Sizes (Distance): Greater attractionMore positive lattice energy

**8-3** **Covalent Bonding**

**What is a Covalent Bond**

-Covalent Bond is the electrostatic force between sharing electrons and the nucleus

-Formed when both atoms have high EN (Electron Negativity)

-Sharing of electrons between atoms

**Single Covalent Bonds**

-A single covalent bond is when 2 atoms share only 1 pair of electrons

-AKA: Single bond

-E.g. F2 Cl2 H2O

-Group 4 elements can form 4 single bonds, Group 5 elements can form 3 single bonds, Group 6 elements can form 2 single bonds, Group 7 elements can form only 1 single bond.

**Sigma (σ) Bonds**

-A sigma bond is formed when 2 s or p orbitals perform head-on overlap

-All single bonds are sigma bonds

-Sigma bonds are free to rotate on the bond axis

-Can be formed between s-s, s-p, or p-p orbitals

**Pi (π) Bonds**

-Pi bond is formed between 2 parallel p orbitals

-In a pi bond, the electrons tend to be found on sausage shaped regions above and below the bond axis

-Pi bond overlaps less than sigma bond, so pi bonds tend to be weaker

-A pi bond can only form if a sigma bond already exists between the 2 atoms

-Pi bond is not free to rotate

**Multiple Covalent Bonds**

-More than one pair of electron is shared between 2 atoms

-Double bond: 2 pairs of electrons are shared

-Triple bond: 3 pairs of electrons are shared

-Only one sigma can be formed between 2 atoms

-Bond association energy is the energy required to break the bond between 2 covalently bonded atoms

-Energy is released when chemical bonds are formed

-Bond dissociation energy indicates the strength of a chemical bond.

-Bond length has an inverse relationship with the bond dissociation energy, and has direct relationship with the number of shared electrons.

-Atoms of form longer bonds when the atoms are bigger in size

**8-4** **Bond Polarity and Electronegativity (EN)**

- **Electronegativity**

-Electronegativity is defined as the ability of an atom in a molecule to attract electrons to itself

-American scientist Linus Pauling developed first and most widely used EN scale which is called the Pauling scale.

-Pauling scale is a relative scale running from 0.7(Fr) to 3.98(F) and is a dimensionless quantity

-In general, EN values decrease from top to bottom, and increase from left to right

- **Polarity of the Bond**

-Polarity refers to a separation of positive and negative charge.

-In a nonpolar bond, the bonding electrons are shared equally

-In a polar bond, electrons are shared unequally

-In a compound, the atom with a lower EN is called the partially positive end, and the atom with a higher EN is called a partially negative end

-You can express a polar bond in 2 ways

-The symbol in the second picture is lower case delta (δ) which expresses H as the partially positive charge and Cl as the partially negative charge

-How to determine the type of bond if you know the difference of the electronegativity

**8-5 Drawing Lewis Structures**

Molecular formula: Shows all the numbers of atoms in one molecule

Lewis Structure: Uses letter symbols and bonds to show relative position of atoms

Structural formula: Similar to Lewis structure but unpaired electrons are omitted

**Guide to writing Lewis structures**

**Formal Charge** **(CHECK AFTER DRAWING ONLY IF CENTRAL IS PERIOD 3 OR AFTER)**

-Formal charge= v.e.-(bonding electrons/2 + non-bonding electrons)

-First write the symbol, then the number (ex. -1/+2)

-The sum of the formal charges of the atoms should be equal to the overall charge of the compound

-The best Lewis Structure is the one with the fewest formal charges, and also puts the negative charge on the atom that has the largest EN

Ex. The last Lewis structure is the best because it has formal charge closer to 0 for each atom, and places the negative charge on the atom that has the largest EN which is oxygen

## 8.7 Exceptions to the Octet Rule

**-Odd number of electron**

Ex. Lewis structure for NO

-Nitrogen and oxygen both have a formal charge of 0, so this is the best Lewis structure

-examples: NO, NO2, etc

**-Less than an octet**

Ex. Boron trifluoride

-For compounds that has less than 8 electrons for the central atom, it can easily combine with other compounds, forming a coordinate covalent bond

-Coordinate Covalent Bond is formed when both of the shared electrons are donate by the same atoms (Usually represented by an arrow)

Example of coordinate covalent bond

-One pair of electrons is completely donated by oxygen, so it is a coordinate covalent bond

**-More than eight valence electron (expanded octet)**

-An expanded octet can be explained by considering the d orbitals that occurs in the energy level of elements in 3rd period or higher

-Can only appear for elements after or on the third period

Ex. PCl5
