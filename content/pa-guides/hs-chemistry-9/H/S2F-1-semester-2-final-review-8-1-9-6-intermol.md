**Ionization Energy**

Defined as the amount of energy required to remove an electron (valence electron first) from the ground state of a gaseous atom or ion.

As one goes down a column, less energy is required to remove the first electron due to decreasing atomic size and hence weaker electrostatic attraction.

Generally, as one goes across a row, it gets harder to remove an electron due to increasing nuclear charge and stronger electrostatic attraction.

When all valence electrons have been removed, the ionization energy experiences a quantum leap to break through another shell.

The first discontinuity occurs between Groups IIA and IIIA. As the electron is removed from a p-orbital rather than an s orbital, which, on average, is further away and less attracted to the nucleus.

The second discontinuity occurs between Groups VA and VIA. An electron removed comes from a doubly occupied orbital. Repulsion from other electrons in the orbital helps in its removal.

**Electron Affinity**

Defined as the energy change accompanying the addition of an electron to a gaseous atom

The more negative the electron affinity, the greater the attraction to the added electron.

In general, electron affinity becomes more exothermic as you go from left to right across a row and more positive down a group.

The discontinuities are similar to those of IE, but take place between IA to IIA and IVA to VA.

**Electronegativity**

The ability of an atom to attract electrons when it is in a compound (measured by the Pauling scale from 0.7(Fr) to 3.98(F)).

Within a group, EN decreases from top to bottom as the number of electron shells increases. The valence shell is getting less strongly held by the nucleus. It’s getting harder for the atom to attract one electron.

Across a period, EN increases (representative elements). As the proton number increases, the attractions from the nucleus is getting greater. It’s getting easier for the atom to attract one electron.

**Compound** **Distinction** **(8.2, 8.3, 8.8, 8.9)**

**Chemical bond** is the electrostatic attraction force that holds two atoms together.

**Ionic bond:** attraction between cations and anions.

**Covalent bond**: attraction between nucleus and shared electrons.

**Metallic bond:** attraction between metal cations and delocalized electrons.

**Ionic Bonding**

Elements with a low electronegativity tend to form cations by losing electrons (Mostly Metal).

Elements with high electronegativity tend to form anions by gaining electrons (Mostly Nonmetal).

Ionic Bonding occurs between atoms that have very different electronegativities (Difference > 2.0).

Ionic compounds do not exist as discrete units, but as collections of positively and negatively charged ions arranged in lattice structures.

The chemical formula of an ionic compound refers to a ratio known as a formula unit (the lowest whole number ratio of ions in an ionic compound).

Bond Strength calculated by Coulomb’s Law: ${E}_{el}=K\frac{{Q}_{1}{Q}_{2}}{{d}^{2}}$ (Q is the charge, d is the distance between the nuclei).

**Properties:**

High melting point and hard due to strong ionic bonding.

Brittle due to repulsion force between like charges when layers shift by a force.

Conducts electricity in aqueous or molten form due to free-moving ions. Solid form does not conduct electricity since ions stay in fixed positions.

**Covalent Bonding**

**Covalent bond** is the electrostatic attraction force between sharing electrons and nucleus formed by the sharing of valence electrons.

**Occurs between atoms that** **both** **have** **high and** **similar electronegativities (Difference < 2.0).**

Covalent bonds are very strong but have a very low melting point because melting does not break covalent bond rather only rearranges the molecules.

Contains two types of bonds, Sigma bond and Pi bond

**Properties:**

Low melting and boiling point due to weak intermolecular forces.

**Metallic Bonding**

Metals consist of closely packed cations and loosely held valence electrons rather than neutral atoms.

The attraction forces between the free-floating valence electrons and the positively charged metal ions are known as Metallic Bonding.

**Properties:**

Metallic luster, shine.

Solid (exception: Hg) because of strong bond strength.

Good conductors of heat and electric current due to free-moving electrons.

Malleable and ductile due to changing shape does not break metallic bonds.

**Lewis Symbols (8.1)**

**Octet Rule**

All elements tend to have electron configurations consisting of eight electrons in the outermost shell, which is called a stable octet. (Except for Hydrogen and Helium).

**Lewis Symbols**

The Lewis symbol for an element consists of the element’s chemical symbol plus a dot for each valence electron.

-First on four sides, then get paired

**Lewis Structures (8.5,8.7)**

**Drawing Lewis Structure**

STEP 1 Sum the valence electrons from all atoms.

STEP 2 Identify the central atom. The central atom generally forms more bonds than its surrounding atoms.

STEP 3 Complete the octets around the surrounding atoms

STEP 4 Place any leftover electrons on the central atom.

STEP 5 If there are not enough electrons to give the central atom an octet, try multiple bonds.

**Drawing** **the** **Best Lewis Structure**

**Formal Charge=valence electrons of the atom-(bonding electron/2+ nonbonding electrons)**

The best Lewis structure is the one with the fewest charges. (The total formal charge in a molecule should be as close to zero as possible, with as few charges on the molecule as possible.)

Put a negative charge on the most electronegative atom.

**Odd Number of Electrons**

When the total amount of electrons in a molecule is an odd number, an atom may not have a complete octet. Eg. NO, NO2, ClO2

**Limit** **Octet**

When an atom cannot have a complete octet. Eg. $BF$3

-B, Be

**Expand Octet**

An expanded octet can be explained by considering the d orbital that occurs in the energy level of elements in the 3rd period or higher. Eg. $P{Cl}_{5}$

**Note: Only elements in 3+ period can have an expanded octet**

**Coordinate Covalent Bonds**

Bond formed when both electrons are donated by the same element (represented by an arrow).

**Properties and Determinants (8.6)**

**Resonance Structure**

Condition that occurs when more than one valid Lewis structure can be written for a molecule or ion.

Differ only in the position of electron pairs, not the atom positions.

The actual structure of the molecule is a blend of all its resonance structures, which is called the resonance hybrid.

The bond of the molecule becomes what is known as a hybrid bond.

A delocalized pi bond is formed, which will be covered in chapter 9.

**Molecular Geometry (9.1, 9.2,** **8.4,** **9.3)**

**Molecular Shapes**

The shape of a molecule can be represented in one of two ways:

By noting the number of bonding and nonbonding electron pairs, we can predict the shape of the molecule.

**VSEPR Model**

The VSEPR (Valence-Shell-Electron-Pair-Repulsion Theory) Model states that the repulsion between electron pairs causes molecular shapes to adjust so that the valence-electron pairs stay as far apart as possible.

**Electron Domains:** A localized region of electron density around a central atom of a molecule. This can include a bonding pair (single, double, or triple bond) or a lone pair of electrons.

**Important Assumptions:**

1. Lone pair exerts a greater repulsion force than a shared pair - decrease ~2.5°

2. Although double/triple bonds affect bond angles, they are ignored for testing purposes

For larger molecules, discuss the geometry of a particular atom rather than the geometry of an atom as a whole.

**Bond Polarity**

As a recap of Chapter 8, Bond polarity is a measure of how equally or unequally the electrons in any covalent bond are shared. Usually, a nonpolar covalent bond is one in which the electrons are shared equally, whereas in a polar covalent bond, one of the atoms exerts a greater attraction for the bonding electrons than the other.

**Nonpolar** **Covalent Bonds:** Usually, a nonpolar covalent bond consists either of two identical atoms, or two atoms with an electronegativity difference <= 0.4. Note that the electronegativity table is provided on the test. Common nonpolar pairs like CH should be noted, however. They have no dipole.

**Nonpolar** **Covalent Bonds:** Usually, a polar covalent bond consists of two atoms with an electronegativity difference between 0.4 and 2.0, non-inclusive. They have both a partial positive and a partial negative end, generating a dipole moment.

**Molecular Polarity**

**Polar Molecule:** A molecule has one end with a positive charge and another end with a negative charge.

**Nonpolar Molecule:** A molecule that does not have positive or negative ends.

**Determinants:**

- If a molecule is symmetrical (Geometrically and Atom-wise), it is a non-polar molecule

-Including linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, and square planar

- If all of its bonds are nonpolar bonds, it is generally a nonpolar molecule

Exception! O3, it is polar due to its bent geometry.

- Otherwise, it is likely a polar molecule

**Bonding Properties** **(9.4, 9.5, 9.6)**

**Valence-Bond Theory**

There are four notable “stages” of the Valence-Bond Theory

- When the atoms are infinitely far apart, its potential energy approaches the asymptote of zero. This can be attributed to the limited interactions between atomic orbitals.
- When the atoms approach each other, their orbitals overlap, bringing electrons and nuclei closer, hence decreasing the potential energy.
- The lowest point on the graph represents the optimal overlap – the covalent bond length that maximizes the electrostatic attraction between the two nuclei.
- When the bonding distance decreases beyond the optimal point, the electron clouds overlap too much, and internuclear repulsion dominates, increasing the potential energy.

**Hybrid Orbitals**

**Hybridization:** The mixing of atomic orbitals in an atom to generate a set of hybrid orbitals. The number of hybrid orbitals formed depends on the number of electron domains mixed. From 2 – 6, they are named sp, sp2, sp3, sp3d, and sp3d2, respectively.

Do not overthink hybridization; focus solely on the # of electron domains.

**Multiple Bonds**

**Sigma Bond:** The single bond formed by two atoms along its axial overlap.

**Sigma Bond Properties:**

- σ bonds are strong due to their high-density orbital overlap
- All single bonds are σ bonds
- σ bond is free to rotate.

**Pi Bond:** The additional bonds formed by two atoms along their sideways overlap.

**Pi Bond Properties:**

- The π bond is weaker than the σ bond due to its limited spatial overlap
- π bonds are only formed after the formation of the σ bond
- π bonds are not free to rotate
- Delocalized π bonds usually form resonance structures.

## Chapter 10 (Intermolecular Forces)

**Intramolecular Force:** the forces that exist inside the molecules.

-They only exist within covalent bonds

**Intermolecular Force:** the forces of attraction between neighboring molecules.

Intermolecular Forces are substantially weaker than Intramolecular Forces

Intermolecular Force ↑, Boiling Point & Melting Point ↑

**The Types of IMF**

**Dispersion Forces:**

This occurs when a spherical atom temporarily loses its shape and becomes a dipole, causing an induced dipole in neighboring atoms.

Dispersion Forces increase with increasing atomic size and mass

**Dipole-Dipole Forces:**

This denotes the attraction between opposite charges of neighboring permanent dipoles.

Permanent Dipoles = Polar Molecules for the sake of the exam

**Hydrogen Bonding:**

The intermolecular bonding that occurs between H-F, H-O, and H-N. They are particularly strong due to the large difference in electronegativities.
