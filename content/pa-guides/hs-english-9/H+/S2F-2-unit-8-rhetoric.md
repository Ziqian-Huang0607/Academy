**Rhetorical devices**

- Rhetoric: the art of effective persuasive speaking or writing. It includes not only figures of speech, but also structure, tone, appeals, evidence, audience awareness, and purposeful stylistic choices.
- **The SPACECAT Framework:**
  - S - Speaker: Who is speaking/writing? What role, credibility, bias, background, and limitations shape the message?
  - P - Purpose: What does the speaker want the audience to think, feel, or do after reading/listening?
  - A - Audience: Who is the intended and actual audience? What values, fears, beliefs, or doubts does the speaker address?
  - C - Context: What historical, political, social, or personal situation shapes the text? Would the message change under a different background?
  - E - Exigence: The urgent event, problem, controversy, or pressure that makes the speaker respond now.
  - C - Choices: What specific rhetorical moves does the speaker make? Consider diction, imagery, metaphor, repetition, syntax, organization, tone shifts, and appeals.
  - A - Appeals: Which appeals are used: ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), and logos (reason/evidence)? How do they work together?
  - T - Tone: What is the speaker's attitude, and where does the tone shift? Tone should be described with precise adjectives, not just 'good' or 'bad'.
- **Rhetorical Devices**
  - **Definition:** Any stylistic device or resource of language that an author or speaker uses to help persuade or make a desired impact on the audience
  - Works alongside a strong thesis, effective evidence, and a logical and progressive organization to create an effective style
  - **Types**
    - Simile: a comparison using like or as (i.e. He is as brave as a lion)
    - Metaphor - a comparison without using like or as (i.e. All the world's a stage)
    - Personification - assigning human qualities to a nonhuman or inanimate object (i.e. The star danced through the sky)
    - Rhetorical question - a question asked for effect (i.e. How many times do I have to repeat myself?)
    - Allusion - A brief reference to a famous person or event that simplifies a complex idea (i.e. Noah's Ark- The CEO recalled all employees from the falling branch two by two, like animals entering the ark, before the investigation destroyed everything.)
      - Greek mythology: "The boy flew too close to the sun"
      - Bible: "She was a good samaritan yesterday when she helped the old woman carry her groceries"
    - Hyperbole - An obvious and deliberate exaggeration to emphasize something (can be used for humorous purposes) (i.e. I told you a million times)
    - Euphemism - A mild word or phrase that is used instead of an unpleasant or offensive one (i.e. promoted to a customer instead of fired)
    - Types of repetition
      - Anaphora - Repeating words at the beginning of a line (i.e. Winston Churchill - We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills)
      - Epistrophe - The repitition of words at the end of a line (i.e. Abraham Lincoln - and that government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth.)
      - Mesodiplosis - The repetition of successive words or phrases (i.e. Bible - We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; cast down, but not destroyed.)
    - Parallelism - Successive words, phrases, clauses expressed with the same or very similar grammatical structure (i.e. JFK - we shall pay any price, bear any burden, meet any hardship, support any friend, oppose any foe to assure the survival and success of liberty)
    - Juxtaposition - posing 2 contrasting elements together to create a surprising or witty effect (i.e. A Tale of 2 Cities - It was the best of times, it was the worst of times...)
    - Anecdote - A short, personal story used to illustrate a point, capture attention, or make abstract ideas more relatable
      - Engages audience emotions and grounds complex arguments in reality
    - Understatement - A statement that minimizes the significance of what it is saying for comedic effect (i.e. Ladies and gentlemen, this is your captain speaking. We have a small problem. All four engines have stopped)
      - Contrast with euphemism - used for irony and humor
    - Oxymoron - Combining 2 words of opposite meaning for dramatic / comedic effect (i.e. tragic comedy)

**Example Texts**

- How to analyze a rhetorical choice: name the choice, quote or paraphrase the evidence, explain the immediate effect, then connect it to purpose and audience.

○ Weak analysis only identifies a device: “The speaker uses repetition.” Strong analysis explains function: “The repeated phrase creates rhythm and urgency, pressuring the audience to see delay as morally unacceptable.”

○ Diction: word choice. Look for emotional, formal, religious, patriotic, violent, scientific, or moral vocabulary. Diction creates tone and shapes audience judgment.

○ Connotation: the feelings or associations attached to a word. For example, “slender” and “skinny” may describe similar size, but they create different impressions.

○ Imagery: descriptive language appealing to sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell. In rhetoric, imagery often makes an abstract issue feel concrete and emotionally real.

○ Analogy: a comparison between two situations used to clarify logic. King often uses analogies to make civil disobedience and moral law easier for readers to understand.

○ Antithesis: balanced contrast between opposing ideas. Example: “not because they are easy, but because they are hard.” It makes a choice sound principled and memorable.

○ Tricolon: a list of three parallel items. It creates rhythm, completeness, and emphasis, especially in speeches.

○ Asyndeton: leaving out conjunctions in a list to create speed, intensity, or urgency. Polysyndeton: repeating conjunctions to slow the rhythm or create accumulation.

○ Inclusive pronouns: words like “we,” “our,” and “us” can create unity and make the audience feel personally involved in the speaker’s mission.

○ Tone shift: a change in attitude, such as from respectful to urgent, mournful to hopeful, or calm to accusatory. Tone shifts often reveal the speaker’s deeper purpose.

○ Counterargument: acknowledging an opposing view before refuting it. This can strengthen ethos because the speaker appears reasonable and informed.

○ Call to action: a direct or indirect push for the audience to act, believe, support, remember, or change.

○ Exam sentence frame: Through [rhetorical choice], the speaker [immediate effect] in order to [larger purpose for audience].

○ Example: Through vivid descriptions of violence against Black children, King transforms segregation from an abstract legal debate into an urgent moral crisis that moderate readers can no longer ignore.

- 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' by Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr.

| S- Speaker | Speaker: Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., a Baptist minister and president of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC). He writes as both a religious leader and a civil rights activist, using moral, legal, and religious authority to defend direct action. |
|---|---|
| P-Purpose | 1. Defend nonviolent civil disobedience against segregation.; 2. Explain why Birmingham was chosen as a site of protest.; 3. Reject the demand that African Americans wait for a “more convenient season.”; 4. Criticize white moderates who prefer order and negative peace over justice. |
| A- Audience | Immediate audience: the eight white Alabama clergymen who called the protests “unwise and untimely.” Broader audience: white moderates, religious readers, the American public, and supporters of the civil rights movement who needed a moral defense of direct action. |
| C- Context | Context: Birmingham, Alabama, 1963, during intense civil rights protests against segregation. Police brutality, arrests, and legal resistance made Birmingham a symbol of racial injustice. King writes from jail, which strengthens the ethical seriousness of the letter. |
| E-Exigence | Exigence: the public statement by eight white clergymen condemning King’s protests as “unwise and untimely.” King responds because their criticism represents a larger moderate argument that tells oppressed people to wait. |
| C- Choices | Allusions to Biblical figures, Augustine, Aquinas, Socrates, and American democratic ideals establish ethos and connect civil rights to respected moral traditions.; Anaphora and parallel structure create emotional momentum.; Analogies make abstract ideas such as just/unjust law more concrete.; Direct address allows King to appear respectful while firmly correcting his audience. |
| A- Appeals | Ethos: King presents himself as a minister, SCLC leader, and moral thinker who understands theology and law.; Pathos: vivid descriptions of humiliation and violence, especially toward children, make delay feel cruel.; Logos: King explains the four steps of a nonviolent campaign and distinguishes just laws from unjust laws through careful reasoning. |
| T-Tone | Tone: respectful but increasingly urgent, morally firm, disappointed, and corrective. King begins with measured politeness, but his tone intensifies when he exposes the pain of waiting and the failures of the white moderate. |
| Key takeaway | King’s main rhetorical strength is balance: he remains controlled and intellectually serious while making injustice emotionally impossible to ignore. |
| Useful analysis line | King uses religious and philosophical allusions not to show off knowledge, but to place civil disobedience inside a long tradition of moral courage and legitimate resistance. |

- "In Event of Moon Disaster" by Willliam Safire

| S- Speaker | The speech was written by William Safire for President Richard Nixon in case the Apollo 11 astronauts were stranded on the moon. The speaker would be Nixon, using presidential authority to mourn the astronauts and protect public faith in space exploration. |
|---|---|
| P-Purpose | 1. Express grief and honor the astronauts’ sacrifice.; 2. Console the American public and the astronauts’ families.; 3. Reframe the failed mission as noble courage rather than simple disaster.; 4. Preserve faith in the space program despite tragedy. |
| A- Audience | Audience: the American public, the astronauts’ families, NASA, and the wider world watching the Space Race. Many audience members would feel grief, shock, fear, or anger at the disaster. |
| C- Context | Context: Cold War and Space Race. A failed moon mission would have emotional and political consequences because the United States was using space exploration to demonstrate national strength, scientific progress, and ideological confidence. |
| E-Exigence | Exigence: the hypothetical possibility that the astronauts could not return from the moon. The speech prepares the president to respond immediately if the mission ended in permanent loss. |
| C- Choices | Elevated diction turns the astronauts into heroic figures rather than victims.; Short paragraphs slow the pace and create solemnity.; References to mankind and exploration universalize the loss beyond one nation.; The speech avoids technical blame and instead emphasizes courage, sacrifice, and human destiny. |
| A- Appeals | Pathos: mournful language and the image of men left on the moon create grief and reverence.; Ethos: the president’s role gives authority to national mourning and reassurance.; Logos is less important here because the speech is ceremonial, not argumentative; its purpose is emotional and symbolic. |
| T-Tone | Tone: solemn, mournful, reverent, and quietly hopeful. The speech does not sound defensive; instead, it transforms tragedy into heroic sacrifice and collective memory. |
| Key takeaway | This speech is a strong example of ceremonial rhetoric: it does not argue a policy in detail, but shapes how a nation should interpret disaster. |
| Useful analysis line | Safire’s solemn diction reframes technological failure as human nobility, helping the audience mourn without losing faith in exploration. |

- Moon speech - JFK

| S- Speaker | Speaker: John F. Kennedy, president of the United States. He speaks as a national leader responsible for defining the moon mission as urgent, honorable, and achievable. |
|---|---|
| P-Purpose | 1. Explain why the United States should commit to the moon landing.; 2. Strengthen national confidence during the Space Race.; 3. Turn a difficult scientific goal into a moral and patriotic challenge.; 4. Persuade listeners that difficulty is a reason to act, not a reason to stop. |
| A- Audience | Immediate audience: students and faculty at Rice University in Texas. Broader audience: the American public, scientists, taxpayers, and Cold War observers who questioned the cost, risk, and necessity of the space program. |
| C- Context | Context: Cold War and Space Race. The Soviet Union’s early successes in space created pressure for the United States to prove technological leadership and ideological strength. The speech turns space exploration into a test of national purpose. |
| E-Exigence | Exigence: the need to justify the ambitious and expensive moon program after Soviet achievements such as Yuri Gagarin’s orbit. Kennedy needed to turn anxiety into determination. |
| C- Choices | Anaphora and repetition of “we choose” emphasize agency and collective will.; Antithesis in “not because they are easy, but because they are hard” makes difficulty sound honorable.; Historical comparisons to earlier human achievements place the moon mission in a tradition of progress.; Inclusive pronouns create unity and make the audience part of the mission. |
| A- Appeals | Ethos: Kennedy speaks with presidential authority and shows awareness of science, risk, and national responsibility.; Logos: he argues that technological progress is part of American history and that space leadership has strategic importance.; Pathos: patriotic language, shared ambition, and the heroic framing of difficulty inspire pride and resolve. |
| T-Tone | Tone: confident, urgent, ambitious, and inspirational. The tone turns danger and difficulty into proof that the mission is meaningful. |
| Key takeaway | Kennedy’s speech succeeds because it converts fear of difficulty into pride in difficulty; the moon mission becomes a test of national character. |
| Useful analysis line | Kennedy’s antithesis makes hardship persuasive: the mission matters precisely because it demands effort, courage, and national unity. |
