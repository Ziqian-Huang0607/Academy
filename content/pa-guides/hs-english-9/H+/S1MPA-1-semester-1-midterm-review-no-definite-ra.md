Subject Verb Agreement

the verb must match the singular or plural form of its subject.

Ex: These violent delights have violent ends.

Ex: The lovers are dead.

Literary Devices

1. Simile: Comparison using like or as

2. Metaphor: Direct comparison without like or as

- “Juliet is the sun”

3. Personification: Giving human traits to non-human things

4. Hyperbole: Exaggeration to intensify the effect

5. Oxymoron: Two opposite words together

- “Jumbo Shrimp”, “Bittersweet”

6. Alliteration: Repetition of initial consonant sounds

- “From forth the fatal loins of these two foes”

7. Allusion: Reference to a famous story, person or event

8. Allegory: A story with hidden symbolic meaning

9. Irony: Contrast between expectation and reality (Situational, Dramatic, Verbal)

10. Repetition: Repeated words or phrases to emphasize an idea

11. Symbolism: An object that stands for an idea beyond its literal meaning

12. Juxtaposition: Placing two things side by side to contrast

Myth

Purposes of myths: to explain, to teach, to entertain

4 Functions of myths

Metaphysical Function: Address fundamental questions about human existence, such as the meaning of life, death, and humanity's place in the universe.

- Pandora’s box (explores why suffering and hardship exist in human life) Cosmological Function: Explain the origin and structure of the universe and natural phenomena, such as creation myths explaining how the world came to exist.
- Persephone and Hades (explains the seasons)

Sociological Function: Establish, justify, and reinforce a society's social order, customs, traditions, and cultural values

- Athena and Arachne (warns against disrespecting the gods)

Pedagogical Function: Teach people how to live by communicating moral lessons, wisdom, and guidance through stories and characters. It focuses on recurring human themes.

- Icarus (warns against excessive hubris)

Collective consciousness: Shared beliefs, values and ideas held by a group of people within a society.

Collective unconsciousness: Inherited universal memories, symbols and instincts shared by all humans, not learned from life.

Archetype: A universally recognizable character, symbol, situation, or theme that appears repeatedly across different cultures, myths, and literary works.

Orpheus and Eurydice

Orpheus is a gifted musician whose lyre can charm animals, trees, and even stones. His wife, Eurydice, dies from a snake bite. Orpheus travels down to the Underworld, where his music moves Hades and Persephone. They agree to let Eurydice return to the world of the living, on the condition that Orpheus must not look back at her until they have both left the Underworld. However, Orpheus doubts the agreement and glances back too early.

Core themes:

- Love, loss, faith, trust
- Human limitations: The inability of humans to overcome death and fate
- Temptation and weakness

The Old Testament

Adam and Eve

God creates Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden and forbids them from eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge of good and evil. A serpent tempts Eve, who eats the fruit and gives some to Adam. As a result, they become aware of their nakedness and experience shame. God discovers their disobedience and expels them from Eden.

Core themes:

- Temptation and disobedience: the consequences of giving in to temptation and disobeying authority
- Knowledge and innocence

Original Sin: the Christian belief that humanity inherited a sinful condition as a result of Adam and Eve's disobedience to God in the Garden of Eden.

Cain and Abel

Cain and Abel are the sons of Adam and Eve. Abel is a shepherd, while Cain is a farmer. Both offer sacrifices to God, but God favors Abel's offering. Cain becomes jealous and murders Abel. God later curses Cain, condemning him to wander the earth.

Core themes:

- Jealousy and envy
- Sin
- Human nature

New Testament

The Word Became Flesh

This passage describes the divine Word (God) coming into the world as a human being, Jesus. Jesus is the light entering a dark world. He takes human form to live among people.

Core themes: Incarnation, divine light, salvation

Joseph Accepts Jesus as His Son

When Joseph learns that Mary is pregnant before their marriage, an angel appears to reassure him. Joseph chooses to accept Mary and raise Jesus as his own son, trusting God's plan.

Core themes: Faith, obedience, trust

Jesus Is Tested in the Wilderness

After his baptism, Jesus fasts for forty days in the wilderness. Satan tempts him three times with food, power, and divine testing. Jesus rejects all three temptations using scripture, demonstrating his obedience to God.

Core themes: Temptation, resistance, faith, obedience

Jesus Heals the Sick

Jesus performs many healing miracles. These acts reveal his divine power and demonstrate mercy toward marginalized and suffering people.

Core themes: Mercy, divine compassion, healing

Jesus Heals the Man with Leprosy

A man with leprosy approaches Jesus and begs to be healed. Jesus touches him, despite the social and religious stigma associated with the disease. The man is cured immediately. Jesus tells him to show himself to the priest, as the law requires.

Core themes: Compassion, breaking social barriers, acceptance

Feeding the 5,000

A large crowd follows Jesus into a remote place. There are only five loaves of bread and two fish. Jesus blesses the food, which miraculously multiplies. Everyone eats, and several baskets of food are left over.

Core themes: Miracles, abundance, faith, divine provision

Walking on Water

The disciples sail across the lake at night when a storm arises. They see Jesus walking on the water and are terrified. Jesus tells them not to fear. Peter steps out onto the water to meet Jesus but begins to sink when he doubts. Jesus saves Peter and calms the wind.

Core themes: Faith and doubt, trust in God, divine power, overcoming fear

Comparing and contrasting the old and new testaments

Similarities

- Both focus on the relationship between God and humanity.
- Both show God's power and influence over human lives through miracles, divine intervention, and judgment.
- Both explore human behavior, including people's ability to make good and bad choices.
- Both use stories to communicate religious beliefs and teach people how to live.

Differences

Old Testament:

- Focuses on the creation of the world, the origins of humanity, and the history of the Israelites.
- Emphasizes God's authority and the importance of following his commandments.
- Often shows the consequences of disobedience through punishment and divine judgment.
- Includes stories about figures such as Adam, Eve, Cain, Abel, Abraham, and Moses.

New Testament:

- Focuses on the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus.
- Places greater emphasis on forgiveness, mercy, and salvation.
- Shows Jesus helping people through healing, miracles, and his teachings.
- Focuses more on how people should treat one another and live according to Jesus' teachings.

The Old Testament mainly establishes the relationship between God and humanity, showing how it develops through creation, laws, and the actions of different people. The New Testament builds on this by introducing Jesus and showing how he brings a new understanding of people's relationship with God through his teachings and sacrifice.

Perseus

With gifts from the gods, Perseus cuts off Medusa's head without looking directly at her, using his reflection to avoid her deadly gaze. Hayden explores the dualism between Perseus and Medusa, particularly the contrast between their identities as hero and villain.

Core themes:

- Moral dualism: The contrast between good and evil, and the complexity of distinguishing heroes from villains

Prometheus and Pandora's Box

Prometheus steals fire from the gods to give warmth and knowledge to humans. Zeus becomes angry and, to punish humanity, creates Pandora, the first woman. Pandora receives a sealed box and is warned not to open it. However, her curiosity leads her to open it, releasing evil, sickness, pain, and greed into the world. Only Hope remains inside when she closes the lid.

Core themes:

- Curiosity and the consequences of giving in to it
- Explains the origins of human suffering and hardship

The Myth of Sisyphus

Sisyphus cheats death twice. As punishment, the gods force him to roll a heavy boulder up a steep hill for eternity. Every time he almost reaches the top, the rock slips back down, forcing him to repeat the task. Camus uses this myth to explore the concept of absurdism. He argues that even seemingly meaningless tasks can be viewed positively through one's attitude toward them.

Psyche and Eros

Psyche is a beautiful mortal woman whose beauty makes Aphrodite jealous. When Aphrodite discovers that her son Eros has fallen in love with Psyche, she sets out to separate them. Eros and Psyche eventually marry, but Eros only visits her at night and forbids her from looking at his face. Later, Psyche's sisters convince her that her husband may be a terrible monster. Unable to resist her curiosity, Psyche lights a lamp to see his face but accidentally spills hot oil on Eros. He flees, leaving Psyche behind. To reunite with Eros, Psyche completes three seemingly impossible tasks set by Aphrodite. Eventually, she becomes immortal and reunites with Eros.

Core themes:

- The importance of trust in relationships
- Love that persists through difficulties
- Consequences of giving in to impulsive curiosity
- The limits of human knowledge, especially when it comes to the gods

The Odyssey: The Sirens

The sweet song of the sirens hypnotizes sailors and causes them to crash their ships. Odysseus wants to hear the song, so he orders his men to tie him tightly to the mast. The crew plugs their ears with wax, allowing the ship to pass safely. Odysseus struggles and shouts for them to release him, but his men ignore his pleas and keep rowing.

- From Odysseus’s perspective

- Homer presents the sirens as deadly monsters and temptresses

Siren Song

This poem is written from the Siren’s own point of view. The Siren claims that her song is a cry for help and pretends to be trapped and miserable. However, the song turns out to be a trap designed to lure men. Atwood shows that the Siren is not just a monster but also a prisoner. The poem challenges the traditional myth of the female temptress.

Romeo and Juliet

Pyramus and Thisbe

Pyramus and Thisbe are young lovers from two feuding families in Babylon. They live next to each other and secretly communicate through a crack in the wall separating their houses. They plan to meet at a mulberry tree outside the city. Thisbe arrives first but sees a lioness with blood on its jaws. She runs away, dropping her veil, which the lioness tears. When Pyramus arrives and finds the torn veil, he mistakenly believes Thisbe is dead. He takes his own life. Thisbe later returns, finds Pyramus dying, and takes her own life with the same sword. Their blood stains the white mulberries dark red.

Core themes:

- Forbidden love: The obstacles created by their families' conflict prevent them from being together.
- Fate: A series of unfortunate misunderstandings leads to their tragic ending.

Summary

Act 1

Scene 1: The play opens with a street fight between the servants of the Montague and Capulet families. Prince Escalus warns both families that anyone who fights again will be punished with death.

Scene 2: Lord Capulet talks with Paris about the possibility of Paris marrying Juliet. Capulet invites Paris to a ball.

Scene 3: Lady Capulet and the Nurse talk to Juliet, asking her to consider Paris as a potential husband.

Scene 4: Romeo, Benvolio, and Mercutio head to the Capulet ball to see Rosaline.

Scene 5: Romeo sneaks into the Capulet ball, hoping to see Rosaline. However, he sees Juliet and falls instantly in love with her. They kiss and later discover that they belong to rival families.

Act 2

Scene 1: After the ball, Romeo sneaks into the Capulet garden.

Scene 2: In the balcony scene, Romeo and Juliet declare their love for each other and agree to marry secretly.

Scene 3: Romeo visits Friar Laurence, who agrees to marry them, hoping that their marriage will end the long-standing family feud.

Scene 4: Mercutio and Benvolio look for Romeo. The Nurse arrives to speak with Romeo about Juliet.

Scene 5: The Nurse brings Juliet Romeo's message about their wedding plans while Juliet waits anxiously.

Scene 6: Romeo and Juliet meet Friar Laurence and get married.

Act 3

Scene 1: Tybalt, Juliet's cousin, is furious that Romeo attended the Capulet ball. He challenges Romeo to a fight. Romeo refuses to fight Tybalt because he is now secretly

married to Juliet. Tybalt then stabs Mercutio, who dies from his injuries. Angered by Mercutio's death, Romeo kills Tybalt. The Prince banishes Romeo from Verona.

Scene 2: Juliet learns about Tybalt's death and Romeo's banishment. She feels heartbroken and conflicted.

Scene 3: Romeo is devastated by his banishment. Friar Laurence comforts him and gives him a plan.

Scene 4: Lord Capulet agrees to arrange Juliet's marriage to Paris.

Scene 5: Juliet refuses to marry Paris. Her father becomes furious with her behavior.

Act 4

Scene 1: Juliet goes to Friar Laurence for help. He gives her a sleeping potion that will make her appear dead for 42 hours. According to the plan, her family will place her in the Capulet tomb. The friar will then send a letter to Romeo, telling him to come and rescue her when she wakes up.

Scene 2: Juliet pretends to agree to marry Paris.

Scene 3: Juliet takes the potion alone.

Scene 4: The household prepares for Juliet's wedding.

Scene 5: The Nurse finds Juliet apparently dead the next morning. The family mourns and carries her to the tomb.

Act 5

Scene 1: Balthasar tells Romeo that Juliet has died. The letter never reaches Romeo because the messenger is quarantined due to the plague. Romeo only hears that Juliet is dead, so he buys poison.

Scene 2: Friar Laurence finds out that his message failed to reach Romeo. He hurries to the tomb.

Scene 3: Romeo arrives at the tomb and kills Paris. He then drinks the poison and dies beside Juliet. Juliet wakes up, sees Romeo dead, and takes her own life with his dagger.

The two families discover their bodies. Their overwhelming grief finally brings an end to the feud between the Montagues and Capulets.

Summed up

- Act 1: Ball scene: Romeo and Juliet meet and fall in love
- Act 2: Balcony scene: Romeo and Juliet declare their love and secretly marry
- Act 3: Fight scene: Mercutio and Tybalt die; Romeo is banished
- Act 4: Friar gives Juliet a potion to fake her death and avoid marrying Paris
- Act 5: Tomb scene: Romeo and Juliet die; the family feud ends

Character traits

- Romeo: He is romantic, passionate, and emotional, but also impulsive and easily influenced by his feelings.
- Juliet: She is intelligent, courageous, and independent. She becomes more determined to make her own decisions as the play progresses.
- Mercutio: He is witty, humorous, outspoken, and loyal to Romeo.
- Benvolio: He is peaceful, reasonable, and loyal. He often tries to prevent conflicts.
- Tybalt: He is aggressive, prideful, and hot-tempered. He is very protective of his family's honor.
- Friar Laurence: He is wise and well-intentioned, but sometimes too hopeful and reckless in his plans.
- Nurse: She is caring, talkative, and often provides comic relief. She acts as a mother figure to Juliet.
- Lord Capulet: He is authoritative and protective, but also controlling and short- tempered.
- Paris: He is respectable, persistent, and conventional. He follows the expectations of his social class.

Themes

- Love: Romeo and Juliet's love develops despite their families' hatred and the obstacles they face.

- Fate: The events leading to their deaths seem unavoidable, even though they are partly caused by the characters' own choices.
- Impulsiveness: Romeo and Juliet often act on their emotions without thinking about the consequences.
- Society: The feud between the Montagues and Capulets shows how family reputation, social status, and expectations can influence people's lives and relationships.

Tragedy (On tragedy – Aristotle)

- Tragic hero: A character of high status or noble background who has a tragic flaw.
- Hamartia: An error in judgment or a personal flaw that contributes to the hero's downfall.
- Catharsis: The emotional release experienced by the audience through the tragedy.
- Purpose: To make the audience feel pity for the hero and fear that similar misfortunes could happen to them.

Quotes

“A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life.” — Chorus (Prologue)

Introduces Romeo and Juliet as lovers whose fate is doomed from the beginning.

“But, soft! what light through yonder window breaks?” — Romeo (Act 2, Scene 2) He compares Juliet to the sun when he sees her on the balcony.

“What's in a name? That which we call a rose - By any other name would smell as sweet.” — Juliet (Act 2, Scene 2)

She questions why their family names should prevent them from loving each other.

“O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?” — Juliet (Act 2, Scene 2)

She questions why Romeo has to be a Montague.

“Deny thy father and refuse thy name.” — Juliet (Act 2, Scene 2)

She wishes Romeo could give up his family name so they could be together.

“Parting is such sweet sorrow.” — Juliet (Act 2, Scene 2)

Juliet uses an oxymoron to describe saying goodbye to Romeo.

“My only love sprung from my only hate!” — Juliet (Act 1, Scene 5)

Juliet exclaims that her only love comes from her family’s greatest enemy.

“These violent delights have violent ends.” — Friar Laurence (Act 2, Scene 6) He warns Romeo that the most intense of passions can lead to the most destructive of consequences.

“A plague o' both your houses!” — Mercutio (Act 3, Scene 1)

He blames both families for the violence caused by their feud, which ultimately leads to his death.

“O, I am fortune's fool!” — Romeo (Act 3, Scene 1)

“Thus with a kiss I die.” — Romeo (Act 5, Scene 3)

He dies beside Juliet after kissing her.

“For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo.” — Prince Escalus (Act 5, Scene 3)

He concludes the play by emphasizing the tragedy of their deaths.
