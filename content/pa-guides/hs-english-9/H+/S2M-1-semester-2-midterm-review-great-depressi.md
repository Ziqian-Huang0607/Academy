1. Marxist Terms (and connection to OMAM)

-Bourgeoisie (The Owning Class): Those who own the means of production (the land, the tools, and the capital). In the context of the 1930s, this is the ranch owner or the boss. Their power comes not from laboring, but from owning the space where others labor.

-Proletariat (The Working Class): Individuals who do not own the means of production and must sell their labor power to survive. George and Lennie are quintessential examples; they possess nothing but their physical ability to work.

-Lumpenproletariat: Often referred to as the "underclass." These are individuals who have been cast out of the productive workforce entirely (the "unemployable" or the extremely marginalized) Characters like Candy (age and disability) or Crooks (systemic racism) exist on this edge.

-Alienation: A condition where the worker feels foreign to the product of their labor, the process of labor, and other laborers. During the Depression, the "migrant" nature of work meant men were constantly moving, prevented from forming roots, leading to the loneliness George describes.

-Commodification of Labor: The process of treating human work as a mere object to be bought and sold at the lowest possible price. When the “tool” is broken, it will be discarded.

You can use these terms for literary analyses.

Terminology

-Content Knowledge Terms / Lexis List For each term, you should be able to both (A) give your own definition of the term AND (B) be able to give an example of its use in a text we studied (from Of Mice and Men or various texts, articles, and poems related to the Harlem Renaissance).

**The Great Depression:** Economic crisis (1929-1939) Started with wall street crash, characterized by mass unemployment, oppression of workers.

Example: OMAM was set in the Great Depression. It sets the background for the marginalization and atomization of workers.

**The Dust Bowl:** A period of severe dust storms that greatly damaged the ecology and agriculture of the American and Canadian prairies during the 1930s.

Example: The Dust Bowl is why so many migrant workers flooded into California, creating the desperate competition for jobs George and Lennie face.

**Hoovervilles:** Shanty towns built by the homeless during the Depression, named mockingly after President Herbert Hoover.

Example: Although the ranch hands live in a bunkhouse, the broader context of Of Mice and Men implies that without their jobs, they would end up in Hoovervilles like thousands of other displaced workers.

**Poverty Cycles:** The self-reinforcing mechanism that keeps individuals in poverty across generations.

Example: In "Mother to Son," the "tacks" and "splinters" represent the hardships of poverty cycles that the speaker has had to climb through her whole life.

**The American Dream:** The belief that hard work and perseverance lead to land ownership and success.

Example: Lennie and George’s plan to "live off the fatta the lan’" is a tragic pursuit of The American Dream that fails due to systemic inequality.

**The Farm**: A symbol of autonomy, safety, and a break from the capitalist cycle of labor. The physical manifestation of the goal of the American Dream.

Example: For the ranch hands, The Farm represents a sanctuary where they can finally escape the drudges.

**Curley:** The ranch owner’s son who represents inherited class power and aggressive social hierarchy. Embodiment of nepotism.

Example: Curley asserts dominance over the laborers not through work, but through his status as the son of the Bourgeoisie.
**Candy’s Dog:** A symbol of the fate that awaits those who are no longer useful (in terms of labor) in a harsh economic system.

Example: The shooting of Candy’s Dog mirrors the lack of social safety nets for the elderly and disabled during the 1930s.

**Migrant Farmers:** Laborers who move seasonally to find work, often living in isolation and poverty.

Example: Steinbeck depicts migrant farmers as rootless individuals who are denied the stability of a permanent home. George and Lennie dream to find a land (evading from this migrant status).

**Marginalization:** The process of pushing specific groups to the edges of society, denying them rights and resources.

Example: In "A Report from Occupied Territory," Baldwin details the systemic marginalization and policing of Black communities in Harlem.

**The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade:** involved the transportation by slave traders of enslaved African people to the Americas. This is the historical foundation of American racial hierarchies and economic structures.

Example: In "Heritage," Countee Cullen explores the psychological distance and connection to Africa resulting from the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.

**AAVE:** Short for African American Vernacular English / Black Vernacular English.

Example: The rhythmic, soulful voice in "Mother to Son" utilizes AAVE to emphasize the speaker’s resilience and heritage.

**Jim Crow Laws:** Segregationist laws that enforced racial hierarchy and disenfranchised Black citizens.

Example: The threat to have Crooks lynched in Of Mice and Men shows that the threat of Jim Crow Laws followed Black men even into the West.

**The Emancipation Proclamation:** The 1863 order that legally freed enslaved people in Confederate states. (Did not actually bring equality)

Example: Alice Walker’s "Women" describes a generation of mothers who fought for the education that the Emancipation Proclamation did not immediately provide.

**Frederick Douglass:** An abolitionist leader and writer who championed the link between literacy and freedom.

Example: The intellectual tradition of Frederick Douglass directly inspired Harlem Renaissance writers to use the pen as a weapon against injustice.

**Langston Hughes:** Poet who celebrated the working class and incorporated Marxist themes of labor and revolution.

Example: In the poem "Lenin," Langston Hughes honors the leader of the proletariat, reflecting the era’s interest in alternative economic systems.

**The Great Migration:** The mass movement of Black Americans from the rural South to the urban North.

Example: Claude McKay’s "Joy in the Woods" reflects the tension between the rural past and the industrial future brought on by The Great Migration.

**Dual/Double Consciousness:** The internal conflict of seeing oneself through the eyes of a society that oppresses the very individual reflecting upon it.
Example: Du Bois explores Double Consciousness in "Of Our Spiritual Strivings," describing the "two-ness" of being both American and Black.

Of Mice and Men

**Chapters 1 & 2**

**Themes: Predatory environment, naturalism, American Dream**

-George and Lennie start at a clearing by the Salinas River. George tells the story of the small farm they hope to own, establishing a sanctuary that stands in contrast to their lives.

-The introduction of the Boss and Curley establishes the power of ownership. Curley immediately targets Lennie, showing how those with inherited status often use it to intimidate those who provide the physical labor.

-George identifies the ranch as a "mean place" and instructs Lennie to return to the riverbed if trouble arises, creating a sense of foreshadowed doom.

**Chapters 3 & 4**

**Themes: Human need for connection, Atomization of workers, alienation**

-Candy overhears the dream and offers his compensation money for his lost hand. This shifts the farm from a fantasy to a potential reality, briefly suggesting an escape from the endless cycle of labor.

-Curley attacks Lennie, leading to a violent clash where Lennie’s crushes Curley’s hand. This demonstrates Lennie’s raw and uncontrollable strength. He is potentially a representation of the unconscious id.

-While most men head to town, the marginalized figures(Lennie, Candy, and Crooks) gather in the stable.

-Crooks describes the struggle of being forced to live alone due to his race. When Curley’s wife enters, she uses her social position to threaten and silence him, proving that even at the bottom of the hierarchy, some carry more systemic power than others. (Culture of blame reinforces hierarchy, link to ICTA)

**Chapters 5 & 6**

**Themes: Fragility of Innocence, Burden of Responsibility (Human connection is hard to sustain)**

-Lennie accidentally kills a puppy and then Curley’s wife while trying to stroke her hair.

-The discovery of the body leads to the hunting down of Lennie. The pursuit is led by Curley, who seeks only revenge.

-George finds Lennie at the riverbed from the first chapter. He recites the vision of the farm and the rabbits one last time to keep Lennie calm.

-George shoots Lennie to prevent him from suffering a more brutal death at the hands of the mob. The story ends with the other men failing to understand the emotional weight of what just occurred, highlighting the total lack of empathy in a world built on competition.

**Device 1: [cyclical structure]: The book begins and ends at the same place.**

**Device 2:** **[symbolism of Candy’s Dog]: In capitalist society, once a being loses its economic value, it is eliminated.**

Essay-writing Tips:

**Thesis Approach:** Include your **Literary Devices**, your **Mechanism** (how they work), and your **Universal Theme**.

Formula: “By using [Device A] and [Device B], the author creates [Effect], ultimately illustrating [Universal Theme]."

How to make strong claims? If your point is (e.g., "Lennie is strong"), you should evaluate the significance. Ask: So what? (e.g., "Lennie’s strength is a **commodity** that makes him a tool in a predatory system.")

Finally, when writing the thesis/topic sentences, keep the wording concise so you have room to expand in the **explanation** part. This provides a layered analysis, allowing your argument to gain complexity as it progresses.

In-Class Texts
**"To a Mouse"** **by Robert Burns (Probably won’t be tested but good for understanding OMAM themes)**

This is an ode to a mouse, expressing the author’s sympathy for the mouse and his home. Steinbeck also took the title of his book from this poem. Burns wrote this after accidentally destroying a mouse's winter nest with his plough. The "plough" represents an indifferent force, much like the **Great Depression** or the **Dust Bowl**, that destroys the “small and innocent creatures”. The "promised joy" in the poem is the equivalent of the "little farm" in the novella.

**Section A.** **Poetry of the Harlem Renaissance & Beyond**

**"Mother to Son”** **by** **Langston Hughes**

Summary**:** An elderly mother uses the metaphor of a staircase to describe her life. She warns her son that her path has not been a "crystal stair" but was instead filled with tacks, splinters, and bare boards.

Theme: Generational Resilience. The poem illustrates the persistent striving required to navigate a society against the marginalized. It emphasizes that while the system of the world is broken and unfair, the act of "climbin' on" is a moral victory.

Note the **AAVE** in this poem. It establishes Black identity and links to a shared cultural history.

**"Heritage”** **by** **Countee Cullen**

Summary: The speaker notes the distance between his current American life and his ancestral African roots. He asks, "What is Africa to me?" while feeling a pull (African Identity) that conflicts with his converted Christian identity.

Theme: Double Consciousness. This is a direct example of the "two-ness" described by Du Bois.

**"Women” by** **Alice Walker**

Summary: Walker notes the striving of the mother’s generation: women who were "stout of step" and "battered down doors" like "fists." They fought to ensure their children had access to the education and books they themselves could not attain.

Theme: Sacrifice and vision. These women sacrifice their own comfort to break the poverty cycle for the next generation, seeking opportunities of education and success.

**"Lenin”** **by** **Langston Hughes**

Summary: A tribute to the revolutionary leader, depicting him as a symbol of the common worker’s power and the hope for a world without capitalists and oppression.

Theme: Proletarian Power. The poem reflects the desire to break down a predatory economic architecture and advocate for a socialist society. Note that Langston Hughes is a socialist.

**"Joy in the Woods"** **by** **Claude McKay**

Summary: A worker trapped in the grueling, mechanical life of a factory laborer dreams of escaping to the natural world (the woods) to find a sense of self that the industrial city has stripped away.

Theme: Alienation from Nature and Self. Much like the "little farm" in Of Mice and Men, the woods represent a sanctuary that is always out of reach due to the demands of a capitalist labor system.

**“I, Too”** **by Langston Hughes (No handout but this appeared on the PPT)**

**Summary:** A marginalized African American speaker asserts his rightful place in the nation despite being physically segregated and sent to eat in the kitchen.

**Themes:**

Jim Crow Laws: This poem describes the literal and social segregation represented by these laws. Double Consciousness: The speaker is aware of how "they" see him (the darker brother in the kitchen) vs. how he sees himself (beautiful and strong).

**Section B** **Prose and Essays**

**"Of Our Spiritual Strivings" (from** **The Souls of Black Folk)** **by** **W.E.B. Du Bois**

Summary: Du Bois introduces the concept of the "Veil" and "Double Consciousness," describing the feeling of always looking at oneself through the eyes of others as well as the struggle to separate the two identities while attempting to preserve both as a whole.

Theme: Marginalization. It explores the striving of a human soul to be both "Black and American" without having the doors of opportunity nailed shut.

**"A Report from Occupied Territory"** **by** **James Baldwin**

Summary: An essay detailing the policing and systemic abuse of Black citizens in Harlem. Baldwin describes the neighborhood as a territory occupied by a hostile force (the police), where the law serves to marginalize and contain.

Theme: State Power and Surveillance. It analyzes the physical and mental architecture of oppression, illustrating how a society "occupies" the lives of those it deems "other." It also notes the issue how white people attempt to solve the issue by finding people like James Baldwin while still preserving an attitude of racism towards all black people.

**The "Turtle" (from** **The Grapes of Wrath)** **by** **John Steinbeck, Extended Metaphor**

Summary: A famous extended metaphor in Chapter 3 where a turtle crosses a highway. It is avoided by one driver and hit by another, but it ultimately survives, carrying a seed in its shell to be planted on the other side.

Theme: The Indomitable Life Force. The turtle symbolizes the migrant workers and the poor. Despite the "drivers" of the economic system trying to crush them, the "striving" continues, and life persists in spreading its seeds. (Cultural Diffusion!)

Grammar

**1. Active vs. Passive Voice**

Active Voice: The subject of the sentence performs the action.

Example: The catcher caught the ball.

Passive Voice: The action is performed on the subject. This is formed using a form of the helping verb **"be"** (is, was, will be) plus the **past participle** of the verb.

Example: The ball was caught by the catcher.

**Usage**: While the passive voice provides variety, the active voice is generally preferred for being more direct and interesting.

**2. Verb Tense Consistency (Compatibility)**

Same Time: When multiple events occur at the same time, verb tenses must remain the same.

Incorrect: When Paul registered for the summer class, he is filling out seven forms.

Correct: When Paul registered for the summer class, he filled out seven forms.

Different Times: If one event occurs before another, you must shift tenses to clarify the sequence.

Example: By the time the police arrived (past), the thief **had escaped** (past perfect).

Practice Guide:

**T - Title:** What does the word "Passion" suggest to you in a literal sense?

**P - Paraphrase:** Translate the poem into your own words. What has the speaker seen, and what is missing?

**C - Connotation:** Analyze the **Anaphora** (the repetition of "I have seen"). How does this repetition emphasize the speaker’s exhaustion or determination?

**A - Attitude:** What is the speaker’s attitude toward the world? Wonder? Disillusionment?

**S - Shifts:** Identify the shift that occurs toward the end of the poem. Mark the change in sentence structure or focus.

**T - Title (Re-evaluation):** On a deeper level, what is the "Passion" the speaker is actually seeking?

**T - Theme:** List the abstract ideas (e.g., searching, unfulfillment) and write a complete sentence stating the theme.
