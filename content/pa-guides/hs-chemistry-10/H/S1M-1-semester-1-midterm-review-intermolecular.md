Review: Molecular Geometry and Polarity, Naming

Molecular Shape and Polarity

Polar vs Nonpolar molecule:

- Polar molecule: A molecule that has one end with a positive charge and the other end with a negative charge

- Non-polar molecule: a molecule that does not have positive or negative ends
- Having a polar bond (electrons being shared unevenly between two atoms) doesn’t guarantee the molecule is polar
- Polarity dependent on both bond polarity and molecular geometry
- Nonpolar: if molecular shape if symmetrical, it is a nonpolar molecule (polar bonds can cancel out)

- Polar: if shape is asymmetrical, then bond polarities cannot cancel each other out and there must be at least 1 polar bond to make it polar (thus there will be an overall dipole moment)

- Exception: Ozone (O3) has nonpolar bonds but is a polar molecule overall

Naming and Formulas

- Ionic compounds: Compounds with electrostatic attraction between cation and anion

- Neutral compounds, so charges must balance (crisscross method and swap numbers to form ratios)

-Formula unit: smallest whole number unit, represents the

ratio of the two ions

Naming ionic compounds:

- name of cation followed by anion (-ide)

Monatomic ions:

- cations formed from metal atoms have the same name as the metal, but anions are named by replacing the ending of the name with “-ide”

Polyatomic ions:

- some specific ones such as ammonium or hydroxide

Oxyanions: polyatomic ions composed of an element bonded to one or more oxygen atoms

- more O atoms, “-ate” (chlorate)

- less O atoms, “-ite” (chlorite)

- most number of O atoms, per-…-ate (e.g. perchlorate)

- least number of O atoms, hypo-…-ite (e.g. hypochlorite)

Naming molecules — Binary Molecular Compounds

- first element (entire name) + second element (root of element + ide), but difference with ionic compounds is that prefixes are used to indicate the number of atoms of each type (e.g. CO = Carbon Monoxide)

- if first element is 1, then no need for prefix

mono

Naming Acids:

- acid is a compound that contains one or more hydrogen atoms and produces hydrogen ions when dissolved in water (e.g. HCl -> Cl- and H+)

- if the anion is “ide”, then acid is “hydro-…-ic acid”

- if anion is “ate”, then acid is “..-ic acid”

- if anion is “-ite”, then acid is “—-ous acid”

Naming Bases:

- a base is compound that produces hydroxide ions when dissolved in water (generally is an ionic compound)

- rule: name of cation s followed by name of anion (e.g. KOH = Potassium Hydroxide)

Intermolecular forces

- Intramolecular forces: forces that exist inside the molecules (only covalent bonds)

- Intermolecular forces: forces that exist between neighboring molecules

- Intermolecular forces are much weaker than intramolecular forces

3 Types of Intermolecular forces: Dispersion, Dipole-dipole, and Hydrogen Bonds

- Melting and boiling involve overcoming intermolecular forces, not breaking covalent bonds.
- Stronger intermolecular forces generally result in higher boiling points.

1. Dispersion forces

-Caused by temporary and induced dipoles (temporary positive or negative charges on atoms).

-Present in all atoms and molecules.

- The only intermolecular force in noble gases and nonpolar molecules such as H₂ .

- Increase in strength with increasing molecular or atomic size and mass.

- Example: Dispersion force strength and boiling point: Br₂ > F₂ > H₂ .

2. Dipole–dipole forces

-Attractions between opposite partial charges of neighboring polar molecules.

-Occur between molecules with permanent dipoles.

-Examples of polar molecules: HCl and H₂O.

-Examples of nonpolar molecules: Cl₂ and CO₂.

-Molecular polarity depends on bond polarity and molecular geometry.

- Polar bonds can cancel out if the molecule is symmetrical

3. Hydrogen bonding

-A particularly strong type of dipole–dipole attraction, the strongest intermolecular force

-Occurs when H is covalently bonded to N, O, or F and is thus attracted to N, O, or F in another molecule.

-Examples: H₂O, HF, and NH₃ .

-Explains the unusually high boiling points of H₂O, HF, and NH₃. .

Intermolecular Forces and Boiling Points

- Stronger intermolecular forces require more energy to overcome, meaning higher boiling points.

-Larger molecules generally have stronger dispersion forces, meaning higher boiling points.

- Noble gas boiling points increase with atomic size and mass.

-Hydrogen bonding can produce unusually high boiling points.

Intermolecular forces and organic chemistry

Hydrocarbons

-Molecules made of only carbon and hydrogen

- General formula representation: C       H

- Carbon typically forms four covalent bonds

- Hydrocarbons are generally nonpolar, and Dispersion forces are their main intermolecular forces.

-Alkanes: Hydrocarbons with n carbon atoms and 2n+2 hydrogen atoms

Homologous Series

-A series of closely related organic compounds.

-Members have the same general formula, similar chemical properties but changing physical properties

- As the number of carbon atoms increases, molecular mass and dispersion forces generally increase

Molecular and Structural Formulae

-Empirical formula: Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.

-Molecular formula: Actual number of each type of atom. --

-Full structural formula: Shows all atoms and bonds.

-Condensed structural formula: Groups atoms together and omits most bonds.

-Skeletal formula: Shows the carbon backbone; carbon atoms are assumed at line ends and intersections.

-To obtain an empirical formula, divide all subscripts by their greatest common factor.

Example 1: C4H8 has the empirical formula CH₂ .

Example 2: C3H8 cannot be simplified, so its empirical formula is also C₃H₈ .

Structural Isomerism

-Isomers have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.

- Structural isomers can have different physical properties.

- Chain isomerism: Different arrangements of the

carbon chain

- Position isomerism: The same functional group or

feature occurs at different positions.

- Functional group isomerism: Same molecular formula but different functional

groups.

Boiling Point Trends in Organic Compounds

-Larger molecules generally have stronger dispersion forces.

-Branched-chain isomers usually have lower boiling points than corresponding straight-chain isomers, as the former have a smaller surface area and thus weaker dispersion forces

-Branched molecules generally have less surface contact with neighboring molecules, resulting in weaker dispersion attractions.

-For molecules with similar molar masses, the boiling point order given in the class notes is: Alkanes < Aldehydes < Alcohols < Carboxylic acids.

-Alkanes: Mainly dispersion forces.

-Aldehydes: Dispersion and dipole–dipole forces.

-Alcohols: Dispersion, dipole–dipole forces, and hydrogen bonding. -Carboxylic acids: Strong intermolecular attractions, including hydrogen bonding.

Using IMF to explain properties

Physical properties of halogens:

-form colored gases, are poisonous, form diatomic molecules (color, density and B.P. All increase going down a group)

Understanding properties of liquids:

-Surface Tension: amount of energy required to stretch or increase the surface of a Iiquid by a unit area (because spheres have the smallest SA for their volume, water droplets assume an almost spherical shape)

-Cohesion vs Adhesion Forces: former is attraction between like molecules, latter is attraction between unlike molecules

-Capillary action: Movement of a liquid upward through a narrow tube due to cohesion and adhesion.

- Water in glass: Adhesion > cohesion, producing a concave meniscus.

- Mercury in glass: Cohesion > adhesion, producing a convex

meniscus. Viscosity: A liquid’s resistance to flow.

- Stronger intermolecular forces generally result in higher viscosity.

-Vapor Pressure: Pressure exerted by a vapor when the liquid and vapor are in dynamic equilibrium.

- Dynamic equilibrium: Evaporation and condensation occur at equal rate.

-Volatile liquids: Liquids that evaporate readily.

-Higher temperature → more molecules have enough kinetic energy to escape → higher vapor pressure.

-Stronger intermolecular forces → lower vapor pressure; weaker intermolecular forces →

higher vapor pressure.

Boiling Point:

-A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the external pressure.

-Normal boiling point: Boiling temperature at 1 atm

-Higher external pressure → higher boiling point, lower external pressure → lower boiling point.

Phase Changes

Melting: Solid → liquid; absorbs energy.

Freezing: Liquid → solid; releases energy.

Vaporization: Liquid → gas; absorbs energy.

Condensation: Gas → liquid; releases energy.

Sublimation: Solid → gas; absorbs energy.

Deposition: Gas → solid; releases energy.

-During a phase change, temperature remains constant while energy is absorbed or released (the flat part on the phase change diagram)

Phase Diagrams (different from the heating curve, or phase change graph mentioned above):

- A phase diagram shows the conditions under which a substance exists as a solid, liquid, or gas.

X-axis: Temperature.

Y-axis: Pressure.

- Each region represents a phase: solid, liquid, or gas.

- Curves separating regions represent phase-change boundaries.

Triple point: Temperature and pressure at which solid, liquid, and gas coexist in

equilibrium. Critical point: Endpoint of the liquid–gas boundary.

Heating Curve:

-Sloping sections: Temperature increases within a single phase.

- Flat sections: A phase change occurs while temperature remains constant.

Solid warms → melting → liquid warms → vaporization → gas warms.

- Energy is absorbed throughout the heating process.

Structure of Solids

- Crystalline solid: Particles have a regular, repeating arrangement with long-range order.

-Amorphous solid: Particles lack a well-defined arrangement and long-range

order.

- Unit cell: Basic repeating structural unit of a crystalline solid.

Lattice point: Position representing an atom, molecule, or ion in a crystal.

Primitive lattice: A lattice with points only at the corners of the unit cell

- Three common cubic cells: Simple cubic, body-centered cubic, and face-centered cubic.

Types of Crystals:

Ionic crystals:

-Lattice points occupied by cations and anions

-Hard, brittle, and have high melting points.

-Poor conductors of heat and electricity in solid, but molten and (aq) state they can conduct

Examples: NaCl, LiF, MgO, CaCO₃ .

Covalent crystals:

- Lattice points occupied by atoms.

-Held together by covalent bonds.

- Hard and have high melting points(they are covalent network solids)

- Generally poor conductors of electricity, except graphite.

Examples: Diamond and quartz (SiO₂ ).

Molecular crystals

- Lattice points occupied by molecules.

- Held together by intermolecular forces.

- Soft and have low melting points.

- Poor conductors of heat and electricity.

Metallic crystals

- Lattice points occupied by metal atoms.

- Held together by metallic bonds(sea of delocalized electrons with metallic cations that

are attracting each other)

-Range from soft to hard and have low to high melting points.

-Good conductors of heat and electricity.

Examples: Na, Mg, Fe, Cu

Reaction Stoichiometry (3.6, 3.7)

- Stoichiometrically equivalent quantities

- the exact proportions of reactants in a chemical reaction where all starting materials are consumed completely without any left over

- notation: ≏

e.g. for 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O,2 mol H2, 1 mol O2, and 2 mol H2O are stoichiometrically equivalent quantities

2 mol H2 ≏ 1 mol O2 ≏ 2 mol H2O

- Limiting & excess reactants

- limiting reactant: the reactant that is completely consumed (determines the amount of product formed)

- ther reactants are excess reactants

Yield (3.7)

- Theoretical yield & percent yield

- theoretical yield: amount of product calculated to form when all of a limiting reactant is consumed

- actual yield: amount of product actually obtained, almost always less than (and can never be greater than) the theoretical yield due to various reasons actual yield
- percent yield = theoretical yield × 100%

Investigation 1: Hydrated Crystals

- Hydrates

- compounds that incorporate water molecules in their crystalline structures (e.g. CuSO4 · 5H2O, CoCl2 · 6H2O)

- the ratio of moles of water to one mole of the compound is a small whole number

- the “·” shows that the water molecules are sitting in the lattice structure, rather than covalently bonded to the lattice structure

- the water is called water of hydration or water of crystallization

- the un-hydrated compound (e.g. CuSO4) is called the anhydrous compound

- naming: compound name + Greek prefix + hydrate (e.g. CuSO4 · 5H2O - copper (II) sulfate pentahydrate)

- Experiment

- the ratio of moles of water to one mole of a hydrate can be determined experimentally by heating the hydrate to remove water
