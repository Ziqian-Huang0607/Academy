The Spread of Islam

The Origins of Islam

Before Muhammad

- Arabs are Semitic-speaking people originating from Southwestern Asia
- Islam is an Abrahamic, monotheistic religion, sharing a religious connection to Abraham with Judaism and Christianity
- The central region of Arab civilization is the Arabian Peninsula
- The region was originally inhabited by the Bedouin, whose religious practices involved the spirituality of objects and the worship of multiple gods, with Allah as the chief deity
- The Ka'aba is a sacred shrine in Mecca, traditionally associated with a sacred black stone. Before Muhammad, it was visited by people of different faiths
- Mecca was an important trading center on the Arabian Peninsula and the location of the Ka'aba

Muhammad

- Born into a wealthy family, Muhammad lived in Mecca and worked as a merchant for many years
- He became concerned about the differences in values between the Bedouins and the wealthy merchant class

- In 610 CE, he received his first revelation from Allah and began preaching his religious teachings
- The Qur'an was revealed to him over approximately two decades
- As he preached, he faced opposition and was eventually forced to leave Mecca
- Why?
- His teachings of monotheism threatened the existing religious and political order
- Merchants feared that discouraging the worship of other gods would reduce the number of pilgrims and traders visiting Mecca
- In 622 CE, Muhammad migrated to Medina. This migration is known as the Hijra (Hegira)
- With the support of the Bedouins, he established the first Muslim community, known as the Umma
- In 630 CE, Muhammad led a military force that captured Mecca

Islamic Religious Texts

Qur'an

- The central religious text of Islam; contains religious teachings, ethics, and guidance on law

Hadith

- A collection of the sayings and actions of Muhammad

Tafsir

- Interpretations and explanations of the Qur'an (exegesis)

Sira:

- Biographical accounts of Muhammad's life

5 Pillars of Islamic Belief

Shahada – The Creed

- Declaration of faith: "There is no God but Allah, and Muhammad is His Prophet"

Salat – The Prayers

- Muslims are obligated to pray five times a day: Fajr, Zuhr, Asr, Maghrib, and Isha

Sawm – The Fasting

- Fasting from dawn to sunset during the month of Ramadan

Hajj – The Pilgrimage

- Traveling to Mecca at least once in one's lifetime, if physically and financially able

Zakat – The Almsgiving

- Giving a portion of one's wealth to charity

Rashidun Caliphate

Definitions

- Caliph: the political leader of the religious community
- Caliphate: a dynasty of Islamic caliphs

Establishment

- Muhammad died in 632 CE without explicitly naming a successor
- A major debate over succession emerged between:
- Abu Bakr: Muhammad's father-in-law
- Ali: Muhammad's son-in-law and cousin
- This disagreement eventually contributed to the Sunni-Shia split:
- Sunnis supported Abu Bakr's succession
- Shias believed Ali should have succeeded Muhammad
- Abu Bakr was eventually appointed the first caliph, establishing the Rashidun Caliphate

The Four Caliphs

- The Rashidun Caliphate was ruled by four caliphs: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali
- Ali eventually became caliph but was assassinated in 661 CE
- Today, approximately 85% of Muslims are Sunni Muslims

Military Expansion

- Under the Rashidun Caliphate, Arab forces became a powerful, unified military force
- They began expanding into neighboring territories

- 636 CE: Defeated the Byzantine army at the Battle of Yarmouk
- 640s CE: Conquered Cairo from the Byzantine Empire
- 651 CE: Conquered the Sasanian Empire
- Later expansion: Continued conquests across North Africa
- Impact:
- Significantly weakened the Byzantine Empire
- Expanded Islamic political and military influence

Treatment of Non-Muslims

- Dhimmi: Non-Muslims, particularly Jews and Christians, who lived under Muslim rule and received protected status
- Jizya: A tax imposed on non-Muslims who did not convert to Islam
- The People of the Book (Jews and Christians) were granted certain protections in exchange for paying jizya

Umayyad Caliphate

Establishment

- Following Ali's assassination, Mu'awiya took power
- He moved the capital to Damascus and established the Umayyad Caliphate

Expansion

- Continued Islamic expansion across: The Mediterranean, Spain, North Africa
- The Battle of Tours (732 CE) marked a turning point in Arab expansion into Western Europe

- Umayyad forces confronted the Frankish military

Decline

- Several factors contributed to the eventual decline of the Umayyad Caliphate:
- Religious divisions (particularly the Shi’ite Schism)
- Bureaucratic corruption and excessive spending
- Decentralization of political power
- Growing resentment and rebellions in conquered territories

Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258 CE)

Establishment

- The Umayyads were overthrown by Abu al-Abbas, who established the Abbasid Caliphate
- The capital was moved to Baghdad

Political and Social Changes

- Baghdad became a major center of trade and urbanization
- The Abbasids introduced new governmental structures
- Society experienced significant changes, including
- Greater equality between Arab-born Muslims and converts
- Increased intermarriage
- Greater cultural exchange

Harun al-Rashid

- One of the most influential caliphs during the Abbasid Golden Age
- Supported scholarship, science, and cultural development
- Encouraged communication and exchange between different region
- Presided over a period of economic prosperity
- Helped establish Baghdad as a major center of knowledge
- Associated with the development of the House of Wisdom

Decline

- Political power gradually became decentralized.

- The vizier (prime minister) increasingly gained influence over the caliph.
- Extravagance and corruption weakened the government.
- The growing influence of the Seljuk Turks contributed to the decline of Abbasid political authority.

The Islamic Golden Age (750-1350)

- Dramatic growth in:
- Trade and commerce (assimilation with different cultures, including Greek, Persian, Indian, etc.)
- Science, mathematics, and medicine (Indo-Arabic numerals
- Flexibility in the social structure (people could work their way up the social ranks)
- Schools and libraries

The Byzantine Empire

Justinian

Rise to Power

- Emperor Justinian took the Byzantine throne in 527 CE
- Attempted to restore the imperial power of the former Western Roman Empire throughout the Mediterranean
- Established the Justinian Code, a major collection and organization of Roman laws
- Ruled alongside his wife, Empress Theodora

Economic Policies

- Established a strong imperial economy through heavy taxation, which placed a significant burden on poorer populations
- Taxes also helped fund the military and imperial expansion

The Nika Revolt (532 CE)

- A major rebellion in Constantinople involving two rival chariot-racing factions: Greens and Blues
- The revolt was fueled by political tensions and resentment over high taxes
- Burned large parts of Constantinople

- Following the revolt, Justinian rebuilt the Hippodrome, the center of chariot racing

Byzantine Politics

Military and Taxation

- Taxes were directed toward funding the military
- Despite these efforts, the Byzantine Empire gradually lost territories over the following centuries

Political Structure

- Emperor: The head of the empire, with authority over government and court rituals
- Bureaucracy: An administrative system composed of highly educated scholars and officials
- Provincial Governors: Responsible for managing local affairs

Byzantine Cultural Shifts

Cultural Influences

- Byzantine culture combined Hellenistic influences (Greek traditions and culture) with Orthodox Christian values
- Cultural diversity was reflected in: mosaics, architecture, language and writing systems

Changes Within the Church

- A patriarch was appointed to lead the Church (as opposed to the Pope)
- Significant value differences between the Pope and the Patriarch led to the Great Schism in 1054, separating the Orthodox Church from the Roman Church

Cultural Changes

- Syncretism: The blending of different religions and cultural traditions to create new beliefs, customs, and practices

Political Changes

- Independent city-states developed around important trading ports
- Political authority balanced local leadership with foreign commercial influence
