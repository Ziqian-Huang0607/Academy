Years' War

The Black Death

Origins and Spread:

- Originated in Mongolia in the 1200s.
- Spread to China, Europe, when Genoese trading ships left the Black Sea port of Caffa and sailed to Messina, Sicily, Spain, and France, then the rest of Europe.
- By fleas on rats infested ships, towns, and homes.
- One in three people died, worse than any war in history.

How people reacted:

- turned to magic and witchcraft for cures.
- plunged into wild pleasures (believing they would die soon anyway).
- God's punishment and beat themselves with whips to repent their sins.
- Christians blamed Jews (falsely) for poisoning wells. Result: thousands of Jews were slaughtered.

Economic Effects:

- Production declined (workers and employers died).
- demanded higher wages → labor costs soared.
- Inflation (rising prices).
- Landowners converted croplands to sheep raising (requires less labor).
- Villagers forced off land moved to towns, but guilds limited their advancement.
- revolts erupted in England, France, Germany, and elsewhere.

Overall impact: Europe took more than 100 years to fully recover.

Upheaval in the Church

Background:

- Many priests and monks died during the plague.
- Survivors asked: "Why did God spare some and kill others?”

The Move to Avignon (1309):

- Pope Clement V moved the papal court to Avignon, outside southern France.
- Remained there for about 70 years under French domination.
- Popes lived in lavish, worldly courts → critics lashed out against this.

The Great Schism (1378-1417):

- Reformers elected their own pope to rule from Rome.
- French cardinals chose a rival pope.
- For decades, two (sometimes three) popes claimed to be the true "vicar of Christ."

Resolution:

- Church council at Constance, Germany (1417) removed authority from all three popes.
- Elected a compromise candidate: Pope Martin V returned papacy to Rome.

New heresies (ideas contrary to Church teachings):

- John Wycliffe: Bible, not Church, is source of Christian truth; followers translated Bible into English

- Jan Hus: Led call for reforms; followers called Hussites

The Hundred Years' War (1337-1453)

Causes:

- English rulers wanted to hold onto French lands of their Norman ancestors.
- French kings wanted to extend their own power in France.
- Edward III of England (whose mother was French princess) claimed the French crown in 1337.
- Rivalry over control of the English Channel and trade.
- Growing sense of national pride made both sides unwilling to give up.

Key to English success: The longbow wielded by English archers.

Joan of Arc (1412-1431)

What happened:

- 1429: 17-year-old peasant woman appeared at court of Charles VII (uncrowned king of France).
- Claimed God sent her to save France.
- Charles authorized her to lead an army.

Results:

- Inspired battered French troops to fight anew.
- Led French to several victories in one year.
- Planted seeds for future triumphs.

Capture and death:

- Captured by allies of the English.
- English had her tried for witchcraft to discredit her.
- Convicted and burned at the stake (1415).

Aftermath:

- Her execution rallied the French; they saw her as a martyr.
- French took the offensive.
- With cannon (new powerful weapon), they attacked English-held castles.
- By 1453: English held only the port of Calais in northwestern France.
- Later, the Church declared Joan a saint.

Military Changes:

- Castles and armored knights became obsolete (could not withstand longbow or cannon).
- Monarchs needed large armies, not feudal vassals.
- Monarchs turned to hired soldiers.

Long-term recovery:

- Population expanded.
- Manufacturing grew.
- Increased trade.
- Italian cities flourished as centers of trade and shipping.
