**Geography influences politics and trade**

- Mountains divided Greece
  - Isolated communities with limited communication
  - Many small, independent city-states(poleis)developed instead of empire
  - Poor farmland and limited agricultural production = flat land rare
    - Food shortages and overpopulation
    - Colonization and trade expansion around the Mediterranean and Black Seas
    - Fertile plains became densly populated and politically powerful centers
- Long coastline with many harbors
  - Sea trade thrived--encouraged development of merchant class
  - maritime power
- Polis
  - Each included a city and surrounding countryside
  - Each had independent government and unique identity
    - Loyalty to a city state instaad of to “Greece” as a whole
  - Rivalry = civil wars were common
  - Made unification dificult when against foreign threats (eg. Persians)
  - Structure:
  - Acropolis: high city with temples
    - Religious center and source of civic pride
  - Main city (agora): marketplace, homes, public buildings
    - Urban community fostered commerce and economic growth
    - spread of ideas, culture, and innovations through city state
- Traded olive oil, wind, and marble for grain and metals
- Adpoted Phoenician alphabet through trade

**Geography—Main Land Features**

- Balkan Peninsula: mainland Greece, where most city states were located
- Peloponnesus (Peloponnesian Peninsula): southern peninsula of Greece, home to Sparta, Corinth, and Olympia
- Isthmus of Corinth: narrow strip of land connecting the peloponenesus to mainland Greece, major trade route
- Attica: Peninsula in eastern Greece, region around Athens
- Thessaly: Northern Greek region, fertile plains
- Macedonia: Northern area beyond mainland Greece

**Seas and Waterways**

- Aegean: between Greece and Asia Minor
  - Heart of early Greek trade and colonization
- Mediterranean Sea: south of Greece
  - Linked Greek traders with Egypt and Phoenicia
- Ionian Sea: northeast of the Aegean
  - Greek colonies
- Hellespont (Dardanelles): Narrow strait connecting Aegean to the Black Sea

**Major Regions of Colonization**

- Asia Minor: along Aegean coast (Ionia, Doris)
  - Key region for trade and cultural exchange with the Near East
- Aegean Islands: dotted across Aegean Sea, linking mainland Greece to Asia Minor, (Lesbos, Samos, Rhodes)
  - Steppingstones for sea trade
- Black Sea Region: northern coast of Asia Minor and southeastern Europe (Byzantium, Sinope)
  - Access to grain, fish, timber, metal trade routes
- Magna Graecia: southern Italy and Sicily (Syracuse)
  - Fertile land and rich coastal trade, became major centers of Greek life abroad
  - Cultural contact with Romans
- North Africa (modern Libya)
  - Fertile highlands and trade links across the Mediterranean
- Western Mediterranean (Massalia, Emporion)
  - Trade with Gaul and Iberia, extended Greek influence

**Evolution of Government**

- Monarchy – rule by hereditary king
  - Landowners gained military power because of bronze weapons and chariots, challenged king’s authority
- Aristocracy – power held by landowning nobles
  - Trade expanded, created new middle class. Demanded political participation because contributions to economy / military
- Oligarchy – power held by small wealthy elites (merchants, artisans, farmers)
- Tyranny- ruled by single leader who seized power, often with support of common people
  - Emerged during unrest under oligarchies helped poor people gain power
  - Weakened aristocratic power, sometimes paved way for democratic Reforms

**Key Figures in Athenian’s transition to Democracy**

- Solon (594 B.C.):
  - Outlawed debt slavery.
  - Opened high offices to more citizens.
  - Expanded the assembly’s role.
  - Encouraged trade (olive oil, wine).
- Pisistratus (546 B.C.):
  - Tyrant; helped poor farmers and gave jobs to the poor.
  - Weakened aristocracy.
- Cleisthenes (507 B.C.):
  - Created Council of 500 (citizens chosen by lot).
  - Made assembly a legislature.
  - Broadened participation in government.

**New Warfare: The Phalanx**

- Gained iron weapons through trade
  - Cheaper, ordinary citizens could fight
- Phalanx formation
  - Encouraged unity
    - Solidarity among citizen soldiers
    - Ordinary citizens had direct responsibility to city state, pride
    - Reduced exclusive power of aristocrats in warfare because wealth alone didn’t guarantee victory
  - Had Class Differences
    - Soldiers with more resources and experience could still have better equipment or positions

## Ch 4 Sec 3 Conflict in the Greek World

**Persian Wars (c. 499-479 B.C)**

- Cause: Persian Empire ruled Greek city-states in Ionia; Ionians revolt in 499 B.C., Athens sent help.
- Battle of Marathon (490 B.C.):
  - Athenians, though outnumbered, defeated the **Persians.**
  - Victory boosted Athens’ confidence but revealed the need for strong defenses.
    - Miltiades: athenian general at Marathon
    - Themistocles: pushed for Athens to build strong navy after Marathon
- Xerxes’ Invasion (480 B.C.):
  - Sparta and Athens allied to resist Persia.
  - Key battles:
    - Thermopylae: **Spartan** heroism delayed Persia, but they were defeated.
      - Leonidas: spartan king
    - Salamis: **Athenian navy,** using clever strategy, destroyed the Persian fleet.
- Impact: Temporary unity among Greek city-states; Athens strengthened its military and naval power.

**Athenian Democracy During the Persian Wars**

- Pericles’ reforms
  - Instituted direct democracy for the first time
  - paid stipends to officials, making public service a full time job + accesible to poor people who needed to work
  - supported arts, culture, and public works, increasing prosperity and civic pride
- **Impact:** personal achievement tied to public service, increased engagement in public life, increased civic honor

**Peloponnesian War (431-404 B.C.)**

- Cause: Resentment of Athens’ power gained from Persinan wars; formation of rival leagues:
  - Delian League: Athens’ allies, originally a defensive alliance against Persians
  - Peloponnesian League: Sparta and its allies, response to the Delian league
- Major Events:
  - Sparta invaded; Athens crowded city walls, leading to plague and Pericles’ death.
  - War was brutal and long (27 years), including alliances with Persia (sparta).
  - Athens finally surrendered in 404 B.C.; lost fleet and empire.
- **Impacts:**
  - Decline of Athens’ political and military dominance.
  - Cultural center remained, but spirit and influence weakened.
  - Greek disunity allowed Macedonia to rise as a new power.

**Key Takeaways**

- War united Greek city-states temporarily against external threats
- Internal rivalries weakened Greek unity, eventually leading to Athen’ decline

## Ch4 sec4 The Glory That Was Greece

**Greek Philosophers**

- Socrates (469-399 B.C.)
  - Critic of Sophists
  - Socratic method to question beliefs
- Plato (427-347 B.C.)
  - Student of Socrates
  - Distrusted democracy (his teacher got killed by vote)
  - Founded the Academy
  - Reason, ethical values, idea forms
- Aristotle (384-322 B.C.)
  - Student of Plato
  - Studied all fields
  - Analyzed gov. forms, “Golden Mean” in ethics

**Greek Art and Architecture**

- Focused on balance, harmony, and ideal forms.
- Architecture: Parthenon exemplifies symmetry, proportion, and grace; influenced later Western public buildings.
- Sculpture: Transitioned from rigid Egyptian-style poses to lifelike, idealized human forms showing movement and perfection.
- Pottery and painting: Depicted daily life, war, athletics; original statues were painted brightly.

**Greek Literature**

- Poetry: Homeric epics, Sapphic poetry
- Drama
  - Tragedy (Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides) : human suffering, moral dilemmas, gods and humans
  - Comedy (Aristophanes): satirical, mocked society, politics, customs

## Ch4 Sec5 Alexander and the Hellenistic Age

**Philip II Unites Greece**

- Macedonia: culturally Greek (greek immigrants), but own kingdom
- Cause: Philip admired Greek culture, wanted to expand power
  - Built strong army, used diplomacy, bribery, and force
- Effect: 338 B.C., defeated Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea united Greece

**Alexander’s Conquests of Persia**

- Father assasinated, wanted more expansion, planned to Conquer Persia
- Cause: Persian empire weak under Darius III, provinces rebelled
  - 334 B.C. crossed Dardanelles into Asia Minor
  - Key victories: Granicus River, Issus, Babylon (331 B.C), Egypt, Palestine
- Effect: Empire stretched from Greece to India, Greek culture begins spread
- Greece Egypt Persia India (stopped at Hindu Kush)

**Hellenistic Civilization**

- Cause: Cultural Fusion
  - Founded cities (most named Alexandria)
    - Introduced greek population
  - Encouraged Greek settlers to mix with local populations
  - Religious freedom and cultural politicies
- Effect: Blended Greek, Persian, Egyptian, and Indian cultures Hellenistic civ
- Alexandria (Egypt one): Cultural Capital of the Hellenistic Civ
  - Major port city, large population
  - Pharos lighthouse, library and museum of Alexandria
  - Center for scholarship, science, math, medicine, arts
- Women and Society: more education and social roles; philosophers, poets, rulers (Cleopatra VII)

**Hellenistic Arts, Science, Philosophy**

- Arts and Architecture: temples, palaces, and public buildings reflected power of rulers
- Philosophy: stoicism (Zeno), calm acceptance, moral equality, human rights
- Science and Math
  - Math: Pythagoras, Euclid
  - Astronomy: Aristarchus (heliocentric theory), Erastothenes (Earth’s circumference), Ptolemy (geocentric theory)
  - Physics/Inventions: Archimedes (lever, pulley)
  - Medicine: Hippocrates
