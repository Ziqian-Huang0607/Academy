## 15.1 — Conquest in the Americas

| **Topic** | **Review Notes** |
|---|---|
| **First encounters** | Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492 and met the Taíno people. Spanish contact soon led to conquest, forced labor, and disease across the Caribbean. |
| **Disease and population decline** | Smallpox, measles, and influenza spread quickly because Native Americans had no immunity. Disease killed far more people than warfare alone. |
| **Conquistadors** | Spanish conquerors such as Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro sought gold, land, and glory, while also claiming to spread Christianity. |
| **Cortés conquers the Aztecs** | Cortés landed in Mexico in 1519, made alliances with enemies of the Aztecs, used horses and guns, and took advantage of internal tensions and disease. Tenochtitlán fell in 1521. |
| **Pizarro conquers the Incas** | Pizarro captured Atahualpa, exploited Incan civil war and political division, and used Spanish military advantages. The Incan Empire fell quickly despite its size. |
| **Effects of conquest** | Spanish rule brought forced labor, conversion to Christianity, loss of land and political independence, and new colonial societies shaped by Spanish, Native American, and African cultures. |

### Short-Term vs Long-Term Effects

| **Type of Effect** | **Examples** |
|---|---|
| **Short-term** | Military conquest; seizure of gold and land; forced labor; spread of European diseases; collapse of Aztec and Incan political power. |
| **Long-term** | Spanish colonial rule; Catholic conversion; population decline; racial and cultural blending; growth of plantation and mining economies; development of the Atlantic slave trade. |

## 15.2 — Spanish and Portuguese Colonies in the Americas

### Spanish Rule in the Americas

- By the mid-1500s, Spain controlled a large empire divided into four main provinces: New Spain, New Granada, Peru, and La Plata.
- The Council of the Indies passed colonial laws from Spain; viceroys governed in the name of the king.
- The Catholic Church worked closely with the government to convert Native Americans. Franciscan and Jesuit missionaries founded missions that also spread European language, customs, and culture.
- Spanish colonial government and the Church reinforced each other: political control and religious conversion often happened together.

### How Spain Profited

| **System / Policy** | **What to Remember** |
|---|---|
| **Mercantilism** | Colonies existed mainly to enrich Spain. They exported raw materials such as silver and gold to Spain and imported finished goods from Spain. |
| **Control of trade** | The crown controlled valuable resources and restricted colonial trade to protect Spanish profits. |
| **Encomienda system** | Conquistadors received the right to demand labor or tribute from Native Americans in a specific area. Harsh conditions caused suffering and population decline. |
| **Las Casas and reforms** | Bartolomé de Las Casas criticized the abuse of Native Americans. In 1542, Spain passed New Laws meant to forbid Native slavery and reduce abuse, though enforcement was limited. |
| **African labor** | Because Native populations declined and colonies needed workers, enslaved Africans were brought to the Americas beginning in the 1500s. |

### Colonial Society and Culture

- Cultural blending: Spanish settlers adapted some Native American building styles, transportation, and art; African peoples brought farming knowledge, cooking styles, crops, music, and dance.
- Christianity spread but often blended with Native American and African traditions.
- Colonial society was hierarchical: peninsulares at the top, followed by creoles, mestizos, mulattoes, Native Americans, and enslaved Africans.
- Towns and cities became centers of government, trade, European culture, education, and religious life. Spanish-style buildings and monuments displayed colonial power and wealth.
- Education was important because the Church needed priests and educated officials. The University of Mexico was founded in 1551. Convents gave some women access to education; Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz became one of the most famous poets of colonial Latin America.

### Portuguese Brazil

- Portugal claimed Brazil and used land grants to encourage settlement and profit-sharing with the crown.
- Early profits came from brazilwood, which produced a valuable red dye.
- Disease badly weakened the Tupian Indians and other Indigenous groups.
- The economy later shifted toward plantation agriculture and cattle raising, which increased the demand for enslaved labor.
- France, England, and the Dutch challenged Spanish and Portuguese power through smuggling, illegal trade, and piracy.

## Key Terms to Know

| **Term** | **Definition** |
|---|---|
| **Conquistador** | Spanish conqueror in the Americas. |
| **Viceroy** | Royal official who ruled a colony in the name of the king. |
| **Council of the Indies** | Spanish council that made laws for colonies in the Americas. |
| **Mercantilism** | Economic policy where colonies supply raw materials and buy finished goods to enrich the mother country. |
| **Encomienda** | Forced labor/tribute system imposed on Native Americans. |
| **Peninsulares** | People born in Spain or Portugal; highest social class in the colonies. |
| **Creoles** | People of European descent born in the Americas. |
| **Mestizos** | People of mixed European and Native American ancestry. |
| **Mulattoes** | People of mixed European and African ancestry. |
| **Plantation** | Large estate that grows cash crops, often using forced or enslaved labor. |

## Quick Comparison: Spanish America vs Portuguese Brazil

| **Category** | **Spanish America** | **Portuguese Brazil** |
|---|---|---|
| **Main focus** | Mining, cities, missions, and structured government | Brazilwood, plantations, cattle, and later sugar/agriculture |
| **Government** | Council of the Indies and viceroys | Land grants to nobles and colonial officials under Portugal |
| **Labor** | Native forced labor, then growing African slavery | Indigenous labor declined; African slavery became central |
| **Major impact** | Aztec and Incan empires destroyed; Spanish colonial society formed | Indigenous populations declined; plantation economy expanded |

## Exam-Style Thesis / Summary

Spanish and Portuguese colonization transformed the Americas by replacing Native empires with European colonial rule, exploiting land and labor for economic gain, spreading Christianity, and creating new mixed societies. However, these changes came through conquest, disease, forced labor, slavery, and major Native American population decline.

## 15.4 — The Atlantic Slave Trade

- The Atlantic slave trade became part of a wider Atlantic trading system linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas.
- European merchants and colonial economies profited from plantation labor, while millions of Africans suffered kidnapping, forced migration, brutal conditions, and long-term social disruption.
- The slave trade helped build port cities and plantation economies, but it devastated African communities and turned human beings into traded commodities.

### Triangular Trade Across the Atlantic

| **Leg of Trade** | **Route** | **What Was Traded / Why It Mattered** |
|---|---|---|
| First leg | Europe to Africa | European manufactured goods such as guns, cloth, and cash were traded for enslaved Africans. |
| Second leg / Middle Passage | Africa to the Americas | Enslaved Africans were transported across the Atlantic and sold for plantation labor. This was the most brutal part of the route. |
| Third leg | Americas to Europe | Sugar, molasses, cotton, tobacco, and other colonial products were shipped to Europe for profit. |

### Why Triangular Trade Grew

- Plantation colonies in the Americas needed large amounts of labor to produce sugar, tobacco, cotton, and other cash crops.
- Disease and forced labor had already caused huge declines in Native American populations, creating labor shortages in European colonies.
- Merchants and investors could make large profits even though long-distance trade was risky.
- Port cities such as Nantes and Bristol in Europe, and Salem and Newport in North America, grew because of shipping, finance, and related industries.

### The Middle Passage: A Forced Journey

| **Stage** | **What Happened** | **Why It Is Important** |
|---|---|---|
| Capture and march | Many Africans were seized from inland villages, forced to march to coastal forts, and held until European ships arrived. | The violence began before the ocean crossing; people were separated from families and communities. |
| Aboard ship | Captives were packed below deck, usually chained, and forced into crowded, unsanitary spaces for weeks or months. | The conditions were so deadly that slave ships were called “floating coffins.” |
| Disease and death | Disease, especially dysentery and smallpox, killed many captives; others died from abuse, starvation, suicide, or failed resistance. | The Middle Passage showed the extreme human cost behind the profits of Atlantic trade. |
| Resistance | Some captives mutinied, refused food, jumped overboard, or otherwise resisted enslavement. | Enslaved Africans were not passive victims; they resisted captivity whenever possible. |

### Impact of the Atlantic Slave Trade

| **Group / Region** | **Main Effects** |
|---|---|
| European merchants and port cities | Gained enormous wealth from shipping, finance, insurance, and colonial goods. Cities and trade-related industries expanded. |
| American colonies | Plantation economies grew, especially in sugar, tobacco, cotton, and molasses production. Colonial profits depended heavily on enslaved labor. |
| African societies | Millions were taken from their homes; many communities were torn apart; some African states became involved in warfare and slave raiding. |
| Enslaved Africans | Faced forced labor, loss of freedom, family separation, violence, and cultural disruption, while also preserving cultural traditions and resisting oppression. |

### Numbers to Remember

- In the 1500s, about 2,000 enslaved Africans were sent to the Americas each year.
- In the 1780s, when the slave trade was at its peak, about 80,000 enslaved Africans were sent each year.
- By the mid-1800s, an estimated 11 million enslaved Africans had reached the Americas, while roughly 2 million died during the Middle Passage.

### Key Terms — 15.4

| **Term** | **Definition** |
|---|---|
| Triangular trade | A three-part Atlantic trade network linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas. |
| Middle Passage | The forced voyage of enslaved Africans from Africa to the Americas. |
| Commodity | Anything bought and sold. In this system, enslaved people were brutally treated as commodities. |
| Mutiny | A revolt or rebellion against authority, especially aboard a ship. |
| Olaudah Equiano | A formerly enslaved African whose published account helped expose the horrors of the slave trade. |

## 15.5 — Effects of Global Contact

- European exploration connected Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Americas more directly than before.
- This contact created the Columbian Exchange, transformed diets and populations, shifted global migration patterns, and encouraged new economic systems.
- The Commercial Revolution expanded capitalism, banking, joint-stock companies, and mercantilist policies, while also increasing inequality and colonial exploitation.

### The Columbian Exchange

| **What Moved?** | **Examples** | **Effects** |
|---|---|---|
| Foods from the Americas to Europe / Old World | Corn, potatoes, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, pumpkins, peppers, beans, squash | Improved diets and helped support population growth in Europe, Asia, and Africa. Potatoes and corn became especially important. |
| Plants and animals from Europe / Old World to the Americas | Wheat, grapes, bananas, sugar cane, cattle, pigs, goats, chickens, horses, donkeys | Changed farming, transportation, diets, and work patterns in the Americas. Horses transformed life for many Native American groups. |
| Diseases | Smallpox, measles, influenza | Devastated Native American populations because they lacked immunity. |
| People | European settlers, enslaved Africans, and migrants from many regions | Created new societies, cultural blending, forced migration, and long-term demographic change. |

### Why Global Population Increased

- New food crops spread across the world and provided more reliable calories.
- Corn, potatoes, manioc, beans, and tomatoes helped feed growing populations by the 1700s.
- At the same time, some populations declined because of disease, war, enslavement, and conquest, especially in the Americas.

### A Commercial Revolution

| **Change** | **Meaning** | **Why It Mattered** |
|---|---|---|
| Price revolution | A period of rapid inflation in Europe beginning in the 1500s. | More money circulated, especially silver and gold from the Americas, causing prices to rise. |
| Capitalism | An economic system in which private individuals or businesses invest money to make profit. | Overseas trade encouraged investment, risk-taking, and profit-seeking. |
| Entrepreneurs | People who take financial risks to start businesses or invest in trade. | They helped expand trade, manufacturing, banking, and colonial ventures. |
| Joint-stock companies | Businesses in which investors buy shares and share profits or losses. | They made it possible to fund expensive overseas voyages and colonies. |
| Mercantilism | Economic policy that aimed to strengthen national wealth through trade, colonies, and control of gold and silver. | Colonies supplied raw materials and bought manufactured goods from the parent country. |

### Mercantilism and Colonies

- Mercantilist countries believed national wealth depended on building gold and silver reserves and exporting more than they imported.
- Colonies existed to benefit the parent country by supplying raw materials and serving as markets for manufactured goods.
- European governments passed strict laws to control colonial trade. Colonists often could not sell to foreign countries or manufacture goods that competed with the parent country.
- Governments used tariffs, monopolies, standardized weights and measures, national currencies, roads, and support for new industries to increase national wealth.

### Impact on European Society

| **Social Group** | **How They Were Affected** |
|---|---|
| Merchants and bankers | Often gained wealth from overseas trade, investment, shipping, and colonial products. |
| Nobles | Many were hurt by inflation because their wealth was based mainly on land and fixed income. |
| Middle class | Merchants, skilled workers, and professionals grew in importance in cities. Some families enjoyed more comfortable lives. |
| Peasants and urban poor | Many saw little benefit and often suffered as prices rose faster than wages. |

| **Term** | **Definition** |
|---|---|
| Columbian Exchange | The global transfer of plants, animals, foods, people, diseases, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492. |
| Inflation | A rise in prices linked to an increase in the money supply. |
| Price revolution | The period in Europe when inflation rose rapidly in the 1500s. |
| Capitalism | An economic system based on private investment and profit. |
| Entrepreneur | A person who takes financial risks to start or invest in a business. |
| Mercantilism | A policy in which a country uses colonies and trade controls to build national wealth. |
| Tariff | A tax on imported goods, often used to protect local industries. |

| **Cause** | **Immediate Effect** | **Long-Term Effect** |
|---|---|---|
| European exploration | Direct links formed among Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Americas. | Europe gained growing global influence and colonial empires expanded. |
| Columbian Exchange | Foods, animals, diseases, and people moved across continents. | World diets, populations, ecosystems, and societies changed. |
| Gold and silver from the Americas | More money entered Europe. | Inflation and the price revolution changed European economies. |
| Growth of overseas trade | Merchants needed capital and shared risk. | Capitalism, entrepreneurs, and joint-stock companies became more important. |
| Mercantilist competition | European states controlled colonial trade and resources. | Colonies became tied to the economic goals of parent countries. |
