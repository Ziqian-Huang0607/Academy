**Geography**

- Apennine Mountains- run down the length of the peninsula
  - less rugged than Greek mountains, allowed easier movement
- Alps: northern boundary of Italy
  - Natural barrier against invasians
- Tyrrhenian Sea: west of Italy, along Rome’s coast
- Adriatic sea: east of italy, between italy and the balkans
- Ionian sea, south of italy, between Italy and Greece
  - Seas = access to trade routes
  - Naval expansion
  - Cultural change
- Tiber River: origins of Rome
  - provided water source
- Etruscans: lived north of rome, ruled central italy
- Greek colonies in the south (Magna Graecia)
  - Adapted culture and practices
- Rome: center of the Italian peninsula
  - Fertile plains and less rugged mountains
  - Easier to unify, fertile land for farming
  - Proximity to sea = trade routes
- Early settlers: Latins (800B.C.)
- agricultural societies merged to form Rome

**Formation of the Republic**

- originally a monarchy ruled by Etruscan kings
- Etruscan Kings expelled in 509 B.C. bc. Abuse of power
- Founded republic where officials were elected
- Senate: initially 300 patricians, served for life, controlled laws
- Consuls: two elected officials, one-year term, supervise government and army, can veto each other
- Dictator: temporary six month rule during emergencies
- Cursus Honorum (180B.C.) formalized career path for officials, based on merit and experience

**Political Rights and Social Struggles**

- patricians: landholding upper class, initially held all offices
- plebians: farmers, merchants, artisans, were not all poor

fought for rights, gained

  - tribunes: protect plebians, can veto harmful laws
  - access to consulship, other offices, eventually the Senate
  - laws of the Twelve Tables: written laws to prevent abuse, patricians were forced to draft it by plebians

**Roman Society and Family**

- family was central, patriarchical
- women had more rights (property, run businesses, public life, elite = politics)
- Education: boys and girls learned to read/write
  - Widespread literacy
  - Latin = common, unifying language across the italian peninsula (Greek = regional dialects)

**Religion**

- Polytheistic, many gods adapted from Greeks and etruscans
- State religion: festivals and temples
  - reinforced community loyalty

**Expansion of Rome**

- conquered Etruscans and Greek city states in Italy, by 270 B.C. most of peninsula
- Romany army: citizen soldiers, organized in legions, rewarded for bravery, punished for cowardice
  - Strong and unified military
- Treatment of conquered peoples
  - Allowed local customs and government
    - Less likely to rebel
  - Required taxes, military service
  - Citizenship for some
    - Social benefits

**Value of Roman Society and Government**

- Loyalty, discipline, respect for authority, courage, and merit-based advancement.
- Equality: plebeians gained political power and legal safeguards without violent revolt
- Rome’s governance inspired future systems (e.g., U.S. Constitution: Senate, veto, checks on power).

Reasons for Rome’s Success

- Skillful diplomacy and fair treatment of conquered peoples.
- Strong, disciplined, loyal citizen-soldier army.
- Strategic infrastructure (roads, military posts, language) that unified Italy.
- Geography: flat lands and trade routes, mountains

## CH5 Sec 2 Repuublic to Empire

**Rome Expands Around the Mediterranean**

- Punic Wars (264-146 B.C.)
- Cause:
  - 1st punic war
  - 2nd punic war
  - 3rd punic war
- Impact: Rome becomes master of western Mediterranean
- Also conquers Macedonia, Greece, Asia Minor; Egypt allies
- By 133. B.C. Roman Power stretched from Spain to Egypt

**Consequences of Expansion**

- Positive:
  - Enormous wealth and trade (loot, taxes, commerce)
  - Cultural influence from Hellenistic East
- Negative:
  - Rise of latifundia (slave run estates)
    - Small farms ruined
      - Increased wealth gap
      - Unemployment rises
    - Corruption and hoarding power

**The Roman Republic Declines**

- Senate (old power) vs. popular reformers
- Use of mercenaries Armies loyal to commanders enabled civil wars
- Economic gap (mentioned above)
- Government inefficiency: republic system was designed for small city state, not vast empire
- Attempts at reform: Tiberius (133 B.C.) and Gaius Gracchus (123 B.C.)
  - Proposed land redistribution to poor farmers and grain aid.
  - Angered the Senate → both brothers and thousands of supporters killed.
  - Significance: showed violence replacing compromise and growing political instability.

**Julius Caesar** **(100-44B.C.)**

- Conquers Gaul → becomes extremely popular.
- Crosses the Rubicon River → defies Senate, starts civil war, defeats Pompey.
- Declares himself dictator for life.
- Reforms:
  - Public works for jobless; land to poor.
  - Reorganized provinces; extended citizenship.
  - Julian calendar — basis of modern Western calendar.
- Death:
  - Assassinated by senators (44 B.C.) who feared he’d make himself king.
  - Rome plunges into civil war again (Antony vs. Octavian).

**Rise of the Roman Empire (27 B.C. – A.D. 14)**

- Octavian(Augustus) defeats Antony (Caesar’s general) and Cleopatra (Pharoah of Egypt) (31 B.C. )
- Octavian becomes first emperor of Rome
  - Marks end of Roman Republic, start of Empire

**Augustus’s Reforms and Government**

- Kept Senate but held absolute power
- More civil service
  - postal system
    - improved communication
  - new coins
    - stabilized economy and enabled trade
  - cencus
    - people counting = admin and tax fairness
- Public works: Roman road building flourished most during reign
  - Supported legion movement, trade, postal communication, governance
- Allowed local self-government in provinces
  - Reduced civil wars
- Problem: no clear rule of succession future power struggles

**Pax Romana (A.D. 27–180)**

- 200 years of peace & prosperity.
- Empire stretched from Britain to the Euphrates River.
- Trade flourished — grain (Egypt), gold/ivory (Africa), silk/spices (Asia).
- Cultural exchange and stability

**“Bread and circuses”**

- Free grain + gladiator games & chariot races = pacified poor.
- Hid deep social and economic problems.

| Aspect | Roman republic (509-27 B.C.) | Roman Empire; (27 B.C. – A.D. 276) |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | Consuls elected yearly, dicators in emergencies only | Emperor holds supreme power, hereditary / chosen successor |
| Citizen Participation | Citizens (mainly patricians, some plebians) actively voted | Citizens had little say, emperor and beaureaucrcacy made decision |
| Checks on power | System of checks and balances between consuls, senate, and assemblies | Power concentrated in emperor, few checks |
| Military | Citizen soldiers loyal to state | Professional army loyal to commander/emperor |

## Ch5 Sec 3 The Roman Achievement

Roman Literature, History, and Philosophy

- Romans borrowed heavily from Greek culture in art, literature, and philosophy.
- Poets: Virgil wrote the Aeneid to promote patriotism; Horace used gentle satire; Juvenal and Martial wrote sharper critiques of society.
- Historians: Livy highlighted Rome’s heroic past to inspire patriotism; Tacitus criticized emperors for eroding liberty.
- Philosophy: Romans adopted Greek Stoicism, emphasizing duty, fate, and concern for all, influencing later Christian ideas.

Roman Art and Architecture

- Art decorated homes, as seen in Pompeii’s frescoes and mosaics.
- Architecture emphasized grandeur over Greek simplicity, using arches, columns, concrete, and domes (e.g., Pantheon).

Daily Life and Archaeology

- Pompeii and Herculaneum preserved homes, businesses, and art, revealing everyday life, professions, music, and theater.
- Plaster casts of victims show the human side of disasters.

Science, Engineering, and Technology

- Romans excelled in practical applications: roads, bridges, harbors, aqueducts, baths.
- They relied on Greek scientists for research (e.g., Ptolemy, Galen, Pliny the Elder) but applied knowledge for practical use in medicine, maps, and engineering.

Law and Governance

- Roman law promoted justice and stability.
- Civil law applied to citizens; the law of nations applied to all under Roman rule; eventually merged when citizenship expanded.
- Key principles: presumption of innocence, right to face accuser, evidence-based guilt, fair trials (though penalties could vary by social class).

Key Themes

- Greek influence shaped Roman culture, literature, philosophy, and art.
- Romans applied science and math to practical problems like engineering, public health, and infrastructure.
- Law codification reflected Roman priorities: fairness, unity, and stability, leaving a lasting legacy.

## Ch 5 Sec 4 Rise of Christianity

**Emergence of Christianity**

- **Origins:** Christianity began in Judea (Palestine) during Roman rule.
- **Founder:** Jesus of Nazareth (born ~4 B.C.), a Jewish teacher who preached one God, love, forgiveness, and moral duty.
- **Key teachings:**
  - Love God and love your neighbor.
  - Forgiveness and mercy are central.
  - Promise of eternal life for believers.
- **Death of Jesus:** Crucified by Romans (~A.D. 30–33) for being seen as a potential revolutionary.
- **Resurrection belief:** Followers claimed Jesus rose from the dead and instructed them to spread his message.

**Spread of Christianity**

- **Early disciples:** Apostles, especially Peter (in Rome) and Paul (missionary to Gentiles).
- **Paul’s** **role:** Converted after a vision; traveled across the Mediterranean, establishing churches, writing letters (epistles) explaining doctrine.
- **Appeal:**
  - Offered hope to the poor and oppressed
    - inclusive of all social classes
  - integrated Greek philosophical ideas to appeal to educated Romans.
- **Persecution:** Christians refused to worship Roman gods or the emperor → seen as disloyal; many became martyrs (e.g., Peter, Paul). Persecution strengthened the faith.
- **Advantages of the Roman Empire:**
  - Roads, safe seas for missionary travel
  - common languages (Latin and Greek)
  - cultural exchange

**Acceptance and Official Status**

- **Constantine** **(early 4th century):** Vision of a cross → victory → embraces Christianity; issues Edict of Milan (A.D. 313), granting religious freedom.
- **Theodosius** **(late 4th century):** Makes Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire and suppresses other religions.

**Organization of the Early Church**

- **Hierarchy:**
  - Priests → bishops → patriarchs (Rome, Constantinople, Antioch, Alexandria, Jerusalem) → pope (Rome claims authority over all).
- **Sacraments & practices:** Baptism, Eucharist (bread & wine), weekly worship, equality of members.
- **Role of women:** Initially active as teachers/missionaries; later excluded from official roles.
- **Scholars/Theologians:** Clement, Origen (Alexandria), Augustine (Hippo) shaped Christian thought by integrating Greco-Roman philosophy.

**Key Concepts**

- Messiah: Savior or “anointed one” Jews expected.
- Martyr: Someone who dies for their faith.
- Clergy: Church officials conducting services.
- Heresy: Beliefs contrary to official teachings.
- Salvation: Eternal life through belief in Jesus.

**Factors for the Rise of Christianity**

**Causes:**

- Social unrest and spiritual search in the Roman Empire.
- Desire for moral guidance, equality, hope for the oppressed.
- Roman infrastructure enabled travel and communication.

**Effects:**

- Christianity spread rapidly across the empire.
- Survived persecution → strengthened followers.
- Became official religion → centralized Church authority.

## Ch 5 Sec 5 The Long Decline

- Long term decline: started since Death of Marcus Aurelius 180 A.D. end of Pax Romana.
  - Political instability, econ, social decay, foreign invasion

Political Causes:

- Frequent civil wars and power struggles: 26 emperors ruled in 50 years; most were assassinated or overthrown.

**Diocletian** **Divides the Empire into West and Eastern Rome (284)**

- establishing Constantinople under Constantine (312) temporarily stabilized the empire but weakened western unity.
  - Split resources and power: Diocletian kept the wealthier eastern provinces for himself. The western empire, with fewer resources and less economic strength, became more vulnerable.
  - Communication and coordination issues: The two halves were governed separately, making it harder to respond quickly to invasions or internal problems.
  - Dependence on eastern support: When the west faced attacks from Germanic tribes and other invaders, it couldn’t always rely on help from the eastern empire.
  - Political instability continued: Even with a co-emperor, the western throne still faced frequent overthrows, assassinations, and weak leadership.
- Corruption and oppressive government reduced loyalty among citizens.

Economic Causes:

- Heavy taxes to support army and bureaucracy burdened farmers and merchants.
- Over-cultivated farmland lost productivity; farmers left land and became tied to estates.
- Reliance on slave labor discouraged technological innovation.
- Population decline due to war, disease, and possibly climate change.
- Inflation was addressed by Diocletian with price controls, but problems persisted.

Social Causes:

- Decline in traditional Roman values: patriotism, discipline, and civic duty.
- Upper class focused on luxury; “bread and circuses” weakened self-reliance.
- Citizen-soldiers replaced by mercenaries, showing declining loyalty to Rome.

Military Causes:

- Roman legions weakened: discipline and training declined.
- Reliance on mercenaries (often Germanic) who were less loyal.
- Germanic tribes and Huns invaded: Visigoths (sacked Rome 410), Vandals (North Africa), and Huns (led by Attila) pressured Roman territories.
- Rome gradually lost Britain, Gaul, Spain, and Italy.

Religious Changes:

- Diocletian persecuted Christians; Constantine legalized Christianity and promoted it.
- Christianity shifted social and cultural priorities, influencing long-term European development.

**Fall of the Roman Empire vs. Fall of the Republic**

| Aspect | Fall of Roman Republic; (133-27 B.C.) | Fall of Roman Empire; (Western, A.D. 476) |
|---|---|---|
| Timeframe | About a century of decline | Gradual over 3-4 centuries |
| Cause of Instability | Political corruption, inequality, civil wars between generals and senate | Economic problems, overexpansio, political corruption, weak emperors |
| Social issues | Landless poor, reliance on slave labor, riots, class conflict | Continued economic inequality, declining populaiton, social unrest |
| Military problems | Armies loyal to generals not state, civil wars (eg. Caesar and Pompey) | Armies stretched thin defending borders, reliance on mercenaries |
| Economic factors | Grain influx hurt small farmers, wealth concentrated in elites | Heavy taxation, inflation, trade disruptino, reliance on slaves/mercenaries |
| Political Power shift | Rise of strong individuals (Carsar, Octavian) ended Republic | Weak emperors, internal power struggles, division of empire |
| Outcome | Republic replaced by Empire under augustus (27 B.C.) | Western Roman Empire Collapses , Eastern (Byzantine) survives |
