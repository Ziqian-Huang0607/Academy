# S2M-2 13.1 THE RENAISSANCE IN ITALY

> From the Grade 9 Resource Department's Semester 2 Midterm History S+ review guide (PA). Writers: Vivienne Yu, Katie Hsieh 9-3. Editor: Vivienne Yu, Katie Hsieh 9-3. Range: 8.5 12.2 13.1 13.2. Student-made reference material: where it differs from class, follow class.

Definition: "Rebirth" – began in 1300s, peaked around 1500. Marked transition from medieval times to early modern world.

Characteristics:

- Shift from agricultural to urban society.
- Trade assumed greater importance.
- Creative thinking and new technology allowed people to comprehend and describe world more accurately.

New Worldview:

- Renaissance thinkers saw their era as rebirth after disorder/disunity of medieval world.
- Reawakened interest in classical learning of Greece and Rome.
- Medieval scholars focused on religious beliefs and spirituality.
- Renaissance thinkers explored richness and variety of human experience in the here and now.
- New emphasis on individual achievement.
- Renaissance ideal: a person with talents in many fields ("Renaissance man").

Spirit of Adventure:

- Wide-ranging curiosity led people to explore new worlds or reexamine old ones.
- Navigators, scientists, writers, artists all shared this spirit.

Humanism:

- At the heart of the Italian Renaissance.
- Studied classical culture of Greece and Rome to increase understanding of their own times.
- Most humanists were pious Christians but focused on worldly subjects.
- Believed education should stimulate individual's creative powers.

Why Italy?

Italy's History and Geography:

- Italy had been center of Roman empire → architectural remains, statues, coins available to study.
- Rome was seat of Roman Catholic Church → important patron of arts.
- Rome inspired religious themes for artists and writers.

Italy's Location:

- Encouraged trade with eastern Mediterranean, northern Africa, northern Europe.
- Extensive banking, manufacturing, and merchant networks developed.
- Trade remained strong in Italy during Middle Ages (unlike rest of Europe).
- Trade provided wealth that fueled Renaissance.
- Trade routes carried new ideas.

Italy's Vibrant City-States:

- Italy divided into many small city-states (unlike kingdoms of rest of Europe).
- Each controlled by powerful family and wealthy merchant class.
- Merchant families exerted political and economic leadership.

The Medici Family of Florence:

- Richest merchants and bankers in Europe.
- Cosimo de' Medici gained control of Florentine government in 1434.
- Family continued as uncrowned rulers for many years.
- Lorenzo de' Medici ("the Magnificent"): clever politician; generous patron of arts.
- At Lorenzo's invitation, poets and philosophers visited Medici palace.
- Artists learned craft by sketching ancient Roman statues in Medici gardens.
- Florence symbolized energy and brilliance of Italian Renaissance (like ancient Athens).

Renaissance Art

Reflecting Humanist Thought:

- Portrayed religious themes but set religious figures against classical Greek/Roman backgrounds.
- Portraits of well-known figures reflected interest in individual achievement.
- Studied ancient Greek/Roman works and revived classical forms.
- Donatello: Created life-size statue of soldier on horseback (first since ancient times).

New Artistic Techniques:

- Returned to realism of classical times.
- Perspective: Making distant objects smaller than close ones; created 3D scenes.
- Shading made objects look round and real.
- New oil paints reflected light.
- Studied human anatomy and drew from live models → portrayed human body more accurately.

Architecture:

- Leon Alberti: Described architecture as "social art" – blend beauty with utility and improvement of society.
- Rejected Gothic style (disorderly); adopted columns, arches, domes of Greeks and Romans.
- Filippo Brunelleschi: Created majestic dome for Florence cathedral (modeled on Pantheon in Rome); also invented machines used to construct dome.

Major Italian Renaissance Artists

Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519)

- Endless curiosity; genius for invention.
- Made sketches of nature and models; dissected corpses to learn bones and muscles.
- Paintings: Mona Lisa (mysterious smile), The Last Supper (moving religious painting + masterpiece of perspective).
- Experimented with new type of paint → much of The Last Supper decayed (recently restored).
- Thought of himself as artist, but talents ranged over botany, anatomy, optics, music, architecture, engineering.
- Sketched flying machines and undersea boats centuries before invention.
- Many notebooks survive as testament to his genius.
- Called ideal "Renaissance man."

Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475-1564)

- Sculptor, engineer, painter, architect, poet.
- Called "melancholy genius" – work reflects lifelong spiritual and artistic struggles.
- Sculptures: David (heroic shepherd; harmony and grace of ancient Greek tradition), Pieta (Mary cradling dead son Jesus).
- Sistine Chapel: Painted huge murals on ceiling (biblical history from Creation to Flood). Took 4 years; left artist partially crippled.
- Architecture: Designed dome of St. Peter's Cathedral in Rome (model for many later structures, including U.S. Capitol).

Raphael (1483-1520)

- Admired for artistic talent and "sweet and gracious nature."
- Blended Christian and classical styles.
- Known for: Tender portrayals of Madonna (mother of Jesus).
- The School of Athens: Imaginary gathering of great thinkers (Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, Averroes). Included faces of Michelangelo, Leonardo, and himself.

Italian Renaissance Writers

Baldassare Castiglione – The Book of the Courtier

- Most widely read handbook of the era.
- Described manners, skills, learning, virtues for members of the court.
- Ideal man: Athletic but not overactive; good at games but not gambler; plays musical instrument; knows literature/history; not arrogant.

- Ideal woman: Graceful and kind; lively but reserved; beautiful ("outer beauty is true sign of inner goodness").

Niccolò Machiavelli – The Prince

- Guide for rulers on how to gain and maintain power.
- Unlike ancient writers (Plato), Machiavelli did not discuss high ideals.
- Looked at real rulers in age of ruthless power politics.
- Stressed that "the end justifies the means." Urged rulers to use whatever methods necessary.
- Saw himself as enemy of oppression and corruption, but critics attacked his cynical advice.
- "Machiavellian": term came to refer to use of deceit in politics.
- Later students of government argued he provided realistic look at politics.
- Work continues to spark debate about government and use of power.

## 13.2 THE RENAISSANCE IN THE NORTH

The Printing Revolution

Johann Gutenberg (c. 1455)

- From Mainz, Germany.
- Printed first complete edition of the Bible using a printing press with movable type. Impact of the Printing Press:
- Before printing press: only a few thousand books in all of Europe (copied by hand).
- By 1500: 15 to 20 million volumes produced on printing presses.
- Printed books were cheaper and easier to produce.
- With books more readily available, more people learned to read.
- Readers gained access to broad range of knowledge (medicine, law, mining).
- As printing presses established in Italy and other parts of Europe, printed books exposed educated Europeans to new ideas and new places.

Northern Renaissance Artists

General Characteristics:

- Northern Renaissance began in prosperous cities of Flanders.
- Spread from Flanders to Spain, France, Germany, England.
- Northern artists eagerly pursued realism.
- Oil painting allowed strong colors and hard surface that could survive centuries.
- Oils used to achieve depth and create realistic details.
- New emphasis on nature – recording what they actually saw.
- Landscapes became a major theme (not just backdrop).

Jan van Eyck (Flemish, 1400s)

- One of most important Flemish painters.
- Portrayals of townspeople and religious scenes abound in rich, realistic details.
- Refined and spread technique of oil painting.
- Portrait of Giovanni Arnolfini and His Wife: layered oil paints to create shimmering fabrics.

Pieter Bruegel (Flemish, 1500s)

- Used vibrant colors to portray lively scenes of peasant life.
- Nickname: "Peasant Bruegel."
- Addressed religious and classical themes but set them against background of common people.
- Winter Landscape With Skaters and a Bird Trap: conveys frozen reality of northern Europe in winter.

Peter Paul Rubens (Flemish, 1600s)

- Blended realistic tradition of Flemish painters with classical themes and artistic freedom of Italian Renaissance.
- Scholar and humanist with wide knowledge of mythology, Bible, classical history.
- Many enormous paintings portray these themes.

Albrecht Dürer (German)

- One of first northern artists profoundly affected by Renaissance Italy.
- 1494: Traveled to Italy to study Italian masters.
- Became pioneer in spreading Renaissance ideas to northern Europe.
- His own methods influenced artists in Italy.
- Called "Leonardo of the North" because of wide-ranging interests beyond art.

- Important innovation: Applied Italian painting techniques to engraving.
- Studied engraving in goldsmith father's workshop and perfected technique.
- Many engravings and paintings portray religious upheaval (powerful northern Renaissance theme).
- Self-portrait (1498, age 26): shows he viewed himself as important (in Italy "I am a gentleman, at home I am a parasite").

Northern Humanists and Writers

General Characteristics:

- Stressed education and classical learning.
- Hoped to bring about religious and moral reform.
- Humanist scholars wrote mainly in Latin.
- Other writers began writing in vernacular (everyday language).
- Vernacular appealed to new middle-class audience in northern towns and cities. Desiderius Erasmus (Dutch priest, born 1466)
- One of most important scholars of the age.
- Wrote texts on many subjects.
- Used knowledge of classical languages to produce new Greek edition of the Bible.
- Helped spread Renaissance humanism to wider public.
- Called for translation of the Bible into the vernacular.
- Scorned those who "don't want the holy scriptures to be read in translation by the unlearned... as if the chief strength of the Christian religion lay in people's ignorance of it."
- Believed individual's chief duties: be open-minded and show good will toward others.
- Disturbed by corruption in the Church and called for reform.

Sir Thomas More (English humanist)

- Erasmus's friend.
- Pressed for social reform.
- Utopia: describes ideal society where men and women live in peace and harmony; no one is idle; all are educated; justice used to end crime rather than eliminate criminal.
- Utopian: today describes any ideal but ultimately impractical society. François Rabelais (French humanist)
- Varied career: monk, physician, Greek scholar, author.
- Gargantua and Pantagruel: chronicles adventures of two gentle giants.
- On surface: comic tale of travel and war.
- Uses characters to offer opinions on religion, education, and other serious subjects.
- Deeply religious but had doubts about organized church.

William Shakespeare (English poet and playwright)

- Towering figure of Renaissance literature.
- Between 1590 and 1613: wrote 37 plays still performed worldwide.
- Ben Jonson (fellow playwright): "was not of an age, but for all time."
- Genius in expressing universal themes in everyday, realistic settings.
- Work explores Renaissance ideals: complexity of the individual, importance of the classics.
- Characters speak in language common people can understand and appreciate.
- Love of words vastly enriched English language.
- Romeo and Juliet(we all love!), Macbeth, Hamlet, Shakespeare sonnets, etc
- More than 1,700 words appeared for the first time in history
