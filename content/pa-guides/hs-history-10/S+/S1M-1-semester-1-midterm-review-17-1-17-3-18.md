## 17.1

The Enlightenment was rooted in reason and natural law, building on breakthroughs of the Scientific Revolution to rethink society, politics, and economics.

Hobbes believed human nature was selfish and brutal. Under the social contract, people give up freedom to a strong absolute government to maintain peace and order. Locke took a more optimistic view: people are reasonable and born with natural rights to life, liberty, and property. Governments exist to protect these rights, and citizens may overthrow a government that violates them.

Key philosophic ideas include:

- Montesquieu’s separation of powers, which splits government into branches to create checks and balances

- Voltaire’s defense of free speech and religious tolerance, alongside criticism of corruption and prejudice

- Diderot’s Encyclopedia, a large collection of writings designed to spread Enlightenment thought and push for education and free expression

- Rousseau’s argument that people are naturally good, and legitimate government should follow the “general will” of the community

Wollstonecraft challenged the limits placed on women, arguing that equal education would allow women to fully take part in public life.

New economic ideas emerged with the rise of laissez-faire. Adam Smith argued that free markets, shaped by supply and demand, function best with little government interference.

Overall, Enlightenment ideas challenged absolute monarchy and traditional religious authority, and became major influences on later revolutions and modern democratic systems.

## 17.3

Britain’s Rise & Colonial Conflict

- Britain became a global power by controlling trade routes, building colonies, and winning European wars.

- The French and Indian War left Britain with massive war debt. Parliament passed taxes (Sugar Act, Stamp Act) on the colonies to pay this debt.

- Colonists’ main protest: no taxation without representation — they had no representatives in Parliament. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act but claimed total authority over the colonies.

Key escalating events:

- Boston Massacre: British soldiers fired on colonial protesters

- Boston Tea Party: Colonists dumped British tea to protest tea taxes

- Parliament passed harsh punishment laws, uniting the colonies against Britain

- Lexington and Concord (April 1775): First shots of the American Revolution.

- Common Sense (Thomas Paine): Enlightenment-inspired pamphlet arguing the colonies should separate from Britain.

Declaration of Independence

- Written primarily by Thomas Jefferson; adopted July 4, 1776

- Core Enlightenment ideas (from Locke):

- Natural rights: life, liberty, pursuit of happiness

- Popular sovereignty: government power comes from the people’s consent

- People have the right to alter or abolish unjust government

American Revolution: War Advantages & Turning Points

- Colonists’ disadvantages: Fewer trained soldiers, limited money, no formal army, many Loyalists (colonists loyal to Britain)

- Colonists’ strengths:

- Fought on familiar home geography

- Strong leadership from George Washington (persisted through hardships like Valley Forge)

- Turning point: Battle of Saratoga (1777) → convinced France to ally with America, sending supplies, troops, warships

- End of war: British surrender at Yorktown; Treaty of Paris (1783) formally recognized U.S. independence

U.S. Constitution & Bill of Rights

- Articles of Confederation: America’s first government, too weak to rule effectively

- Constitutional Convention (1787, Philadelphia): Delegates (Washington, Madison, Franklin) rewrote the government framework, drawing on Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau

- Constitution structure:

- Federal republic: power divided between national (federal) government and state governments

- Separation of powers: 3 branches (legislative, executive, judicial)

- Checks and balances: each branch can limit the others to prevent too much power

- Bill of Rights (first 10 amendments, protects natural rights):

- 1st: freedom of religion, speech, press, assembly, petition

- 2nd: right to bear arms

- 3rd: no forced quartering of troops

- 4th: protection from unreasonable searches/seizures

- 5–8th: due process, fair trial, limits on punishment

- 9th: rights are not limited only to those listed

- 10th: powers not given to federal government belong to states or people

Impact: The Constitution became the nation’s supreme law and a symbol of freedom, inspiring reformers in Europe and Latin America

18.1-18.3 French Revolution

Causes & the Old Regime

France under the ancient régime had three rigid estates:

- First Estate: clergy, owned large land, paid almost no direct taxes

- Second Estate: nobility, held top government/military positions, tax exemptions

- Third Estate: everyone else (bourgeoisie, peasants, urban workers). Paid nearly all taxes, faced high bread prices and hunger. The bourgeoisie resented being locked out of political power.

Economic crisis: Years of deficit spending from wars and lavish royal costs. Bad harvests drove food prices up, leaving poor families starving. Louis XVI called the Estates-General (not summoned for 175 years) to fix the financial crisis.

- Voting rules favored the First and Second Estates (one vote per estate).

- Third Estate broke away, declared itself the National Assembly, and took the Tennis Court Oath: they would not disband until France had a constitution.

- July 14, 1789: Parisians stormed the Bastille, marking the start of the French Revolution.

Early Moderate Phase (1789–1791)

Peasant unrest spread, sparking the Great Fear: rumors of noble plots made peasants attack manor records and destroy feudal obligations. The National Assembly responded:

- Abolished feudalism and old noble privileges

- Issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen: natural rights to liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression; inspired by Locke and the American Revolution

- Olympe de Gouges wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Citizen,

challenging the document’s exclusion of women

- Women’s March on Versailles (Oct 1789): Thousands of women marched to Versailles over bread shortages; forced Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette to move to Paris

- Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790): put the Catholic Church under state control; many clergy and devout Catholics rejected it

- Constitution of 1791: Created a limited constitutional monarchy. Voting rights limited to tax-paying male citizens. Louis XVI tried to flee France but was captured; this destroyed trust in the monarchy. European rulers feared revolution spreading and threatened intervention.

Radical Phase (1792–1794)

War began against Austria and Prussia. Working-class city supporters called sans-culottes pushed for a republic. The Jacobins, a radical political club, allied with them.

- 1792: National Convention abolished the monarchy and set up the French Republic. Louis XVI was tried and executed for treason.

- Committee of Public Safety was formed to defend the revolution from foreign war and internal rebellion. Led by Robespierre.

- Reign of Terror (1793–1794): Suspected enemies of the revolution were arrested and executed, often by the guillotine. Robespierre argued terror was necessary to protect the revolution and achieve a “republic of virtue.”

- After thousands of deaths, Convention members turned against Robespierre; he was executed in July 1794, ending the Terror.

The Directory & Lasting Changes

Post-Terror, the Constitution of 1795 created the Directory: a five-man executive and two- house legislature. It was weak, corrupt, and unstable, facing food riots and royalist threats.

- Rise of Napoleon Bonaparte: a popular military hero who eventually seized power. Long-term changes from the revolution:

- End of feudal privileges, noble titles, and the old social order

- Rise of nationalism: strong loyalty to the nation, not a monarch

- New symbols: tricolor flag, “La Marseillaise” as national anthem

- Social reforms: state-run schools, abolition of slavery in French colonies, religious tolerance

Napoleon’s Rise to Power

- Napoleon Bonaparte was born in 1769 on Corsica, an island in the Mediterranean.

- He trained in the French military and quickly rose through the ranks during the French Revolution.

- His successes as a general made him popular with the French people and the army.

- In 1799, Napoleon took part in the Coup of 18 Brumaire, overthrew the Directory, and seized power.

- He first became First Consul, then in 1804 crowned himself Emperor Napoleon I.

Napoleon’s Reforms in France

- Napoleon centralized the government and made administration more efficient.

- He reformed the legal system with the Napoleonic Code, which gave France a clear set of uniform laws.

- The code protected some revolutionary gains, such as equality before the law and property rights.

- He reorganized education and established state-run secondary schools.

- He restored relations with the Catholic Church through the Concordat of 1801.

- He also supported economic recovery, improved roads and canals, and encouraged industry.

Napoleon Builds an Empire

- Napoleon conquered much of Europe through a series of military victories.

- By 1812, his empire included or controlled:

- Belgium

- the Netherlands

- Switzerland

- much of Italy

- large parts of Germany and Spain

- He placed family members and loyal supporters on the thrones of conquered countries.

- He spread some revolutionary ideas, including legal equality and reforms against feudalism.

- But he also forced conquered peoples to accept French rule and pay taxes.

Challenges to Napoleon’s Empire

- Nationalism became a major enemy. People in conquered countries resented French domination and wanted their own independent nations.

- Napoleon tried to make Europe submit to France through the Continental System, a blockade designed to hurt Britain.

- The blockade failed because Britain was too powerful at sea, and European countries resisted French control.

- Spain and Austria rebelled against Napoleon.

- The Peninsular War in Spain drained French resources.

- In 1812, Napoleon invaded Russia. The Russians used scorched-earth tactics and a harsh winter destroyed most of his army.

Napoleon’s Fall

- After the Russian disaster, Napoleon’s weakened position encouraged his enemies to unite.

- European powers formed coalitions against him and defeated France.

- In 1814, Napoleon abdicated and was exiled to the island of Elba.

- He returned briefly in 1815 in what is called the Hundred Days.

- His final defeat came at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

- He was then exiled to Saint Helena, where he died in 1821.

Congress of Vienna

- After Napoleon’s defeat, European leaders met at the Congress of Vienna in 1814– 1815.

- Their goals were to:

- restore stability to Europe

- strengthen countries around France

- bring back old royal families

- prevent one nation from becoming too powerful

- The Congress redrew the map of Europe and created a balance of power.

- It succeeded in avoiding a large general war for many years, but it also ignored many demands for national independence.

Main Themes

- Napoleon brought order and important reforms to France, but his power depended on military success.

- His empire spread revolutionary ideas while also imposing foreign rule.

- Nationalism and the cost of constant warfare helped destroy his empire.

- The Congress of Vienna tried to restore the old order and keep Europe balanced.
