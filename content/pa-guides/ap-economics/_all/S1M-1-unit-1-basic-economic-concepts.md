### 1.1 The Economic Way of Thinking

**Economics, Business, and Finance**

- **Economics** is the study of how society manages its scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants and needs. **Scarcity** refers to the limited nature of society's resources.
- **Business** studies how organizations make decisions about resources, operations, marketing, finance, and people.
- **Finance** studies how individuals, organizations, and institutions manage money and financial resources over time, including saving, borrowing, investing, and risk management.

**Principles of Economics**

Principles 1–7 below are the ones covered in this course. (In the textbook, Principles 8–10 concern the whole economy and belong to macroeconomics.)

**Principle 1: People face trade-offs.** All decisions involve trade-offs because resources are limited. Society faces an important trade-off between **efficiency** (society gets the most output or benefit from its scarce resources) and **equality** (prosperity is distributed more uniformly among members of society). Policies that promote equality may reduce incentives to work or produce, creating a potential trade-off between the two.

**Principle 2: The cost of something is what you give up to get it.** Making decisions requires comparing the costs and benefits of alternative choices. **Opportunity cost** is the value of the next-best alternative that must be given up when making a decision. It is the relevant cost for decision-making because choosing one option means sacrificing another.

**Principle 3: Rational people think at the margin.** Rational people systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their objectives. They compare **marginal benefits** and **marginal costs**: the additional benefit and cost of one more unit of an activity. A rational decision-maker undertakes an additional activity when its marginal benefit exceeds its marginal cost.

**Principle 4: People respond to incentives.** An **incentive** is something that induces a person to act, such as the prospect of a reward or punishment. When costs or benefits change, people may change their behavior in response.

**Principle 5: Trade can make everyone better off.** Rather than being self-sufficient, individuals and countries can specialize in producing particular goods or services and exchange them for other goods. Specialization and trade let people and countries benefit from differences in productivity and opportunity costs; countries can sell goods abroad at favorable prices and buy goods from other countries more cheaply than producing them at home.

**Principle 6: Markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity.** A **market** is a group of buyers and sellers. A **market economy** allocates resources through the decentralized decisions of households and firms as they interact in markets. Adam Smith called this coordination the **"invisible hand"**: individuals pursuing their own interests can contribute to efficient outcomes.

**Principle 7: Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes.** **Market failure** occurs when the market fails to allocate society's resources efficiently. Two important causes are:

- **Externality**: the production or consumption of a good affects bystanders (e.g. a factory creating pollution).
- **Market power**: a single buyer or seller has substantial influence over market prices (e.g. a monopoly).
- Public policy may improve efficiency by addressing these problems.

### 1.2 The Economist as a Scientist

**The Scientific Method and Economic Models**

- Economists play two roles. As **scientists**, they try to explain how the world works. As **policy advisers**, they recommend how the world should work.
- Economists use the scientific method to develop and test theories about economic behavior.
- Because the real world is complex, economists use **assumptions** to simplify situations.
- An **economic model** is a highly simplified representation of a more complex reality. Models help isolate important variables and explain the relationships between them.

**Positive and Normative Statements**

- **Positive statements** describe the world as it is. They are factual claims that can, in principle, be confirmed or refuted with evidence.
- **Normative statements** express opinions about how the world should be. They cannot be settled by evidence alone because they involve values.
  - Positive: "An increase in the minimum wage raises the cost of labor for some employers."
  - Normative: "The government should increase the minimum wage."

**Microeconomics and Macroeconomics**

- **Microeconomics** studies the behavior of individual consumers, firms, and markets.
- **Macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole, including total output, unemployment, inflation, and economic growth.

### 1.3 The Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF/PPC)

**Definition and Purpose**

The **Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)**, also called the Production Possibilities Curve (PPC), is a graph showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce using its available resources and technology. It illustrates scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency, and economic growth. Because resources are limited, producing more of one good generally requires producing less of another.

Figure 1. A production possibilities frontier.

**Interpreting the PPF**

- A point **on** the PPF is productively **efficient**: all resources are fully and efficiently used.
- A point **inside** the PPF is attainable but **inefficient**: some resources are unemployed or underused.
- A point **outside** the PPF is currently **unattainable** with existing resources and technology.

**Opportunity Cost on the PPF**

- Opportunity cost is how much of one good must be given up to produce more of another.
- **Straight-line PPF**: constant opportunity cost; the same amount of one good is given up for each extra unit of the other.
- **Bowed-out PPF**: increasing opportunity cost. Resources are not equally suited to every good, so as less suitable resources are moved into producing one good, more of the other good must be given up.

**Shifts in the PPF**

- **Outward shift** = economic growth: more of one or both goods can be produced. Caused by more or better resources, better technology, or higher productivity.
- **Inward shift** = reduced productive capacity. Caused by destruction of resources, a smaller labor force, or damage to infrastructure.

**Using the PPF to Calculate Opportunity Cost**

- Opportunity cost of one more unit of Good A = units of Good B given up ÷ extra units of Good A produced.
- This stays constant on a straight-line PPF and generally increases on a bowed-out PPF as production moves toward one good.

### 1.4 Comparative Advantage and Gains from Trade

**Absolute vs Comparative Advantage**

- **Absolute advantage**: the ability to produce more of a good with the same resources, or the same amount with fewer resources. It measures **productivity**.
- **Comparative advantage**: the ability to produce a good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another producer. It determines **specialization** and gains from trade.
- A producer can have an absolute advantage in both goods but a comparative advantage in only one, so absolute advantage alone does not decide what to specialize in.

**Calculating Comparative Advantage**

- Calculate the opportunity cost of each good for each producer.
- The producer with the **lower opportunity cost** of a good has the comparative advantage in that good.
- When each producer specializes in its comparative-advantage good, total output rises and both can gain from trade.

**Terms of Trade**

- For trade to benefit both sides, the price (terms of trade) must lie **between** the two producers' opportunity costs. Then each side gets the traded good for less than it would cost to make it themselves.
- Example: Producer X gives up 2 units of Good B to make 1 unit of Good A; Producer Y gives up 4 units of Good B. X has the comparative advantage in A. Any price for 1 unit of A between 2 and 4 units of B benefits both producers.
