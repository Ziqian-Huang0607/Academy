### 2.1 Markets and Competition

**Markets and Competitive Markets**

- A **market** is a group of buyers and sellers of a particular product.
- A **competitive market** has many buyers and sellers, each with a negligible effect on the market price.
- In a **perfectly competitive market**, all goods are identical and there are so many buyers and sellers that no one can influence the price. Each buyer and seller is a **price taker**: they accept the market price.

**Demand and Quantity Demanded**

- **Demand**: the different quantities consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices.
- **Quantity demanded**: the amount consumers are willing and able to buy at one particular price.
- **Law of demand**: other things equal, when a good's price rises, quantity demanded falls. This gives the downward-sloping demand curve.
- A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the demand curve, not a shift.

**Demand Curve Shifters**

- **Income**: for a **normal good**, higher income increases demand (shift right); for an **inferior good**, higher income decreases demand (shift left).
- **Prices of related goods**: **substitutes** replace each other (if pizza's price rises, demand for hamburgers increases). **Complements** are used together (if college tuition rises, demand for textbooks may decrease).
- **Tastes and preferences**, **expectations** about future prices or income, and the **number of buyers**.
- An increase in demand shifts the curve **right**; a decrease shifts it **left**.

### 2.2 Supply

**Supply and Quantity Supplied**

- **Supply**: the different quantities sellers are willing and able to sell at different prices.
- **Quantity supplied**: the amount sellers are willing and able to sell at one particular price.
- **Law of supply**: other things equal, when a good's price rises, quantity supplied rises, because higher prices make production more profitable. This gives the upward-sloping supply curve.
- A change in the good's own price causes a **movement along** the supply curve; a change in any other determinant **shifts** the whole curve.

**Market Supply and Individual Supply**

**Market supply** is the **horizontal sum** of all individual sellers' supply: at each price, add the quantities each seller is willing to supply.

**Supply Curve Shifters**

- **Input prices**: higher wages or raw-material prices raise costs and decrease supply (shift left); lower input prices increase supply (shift right).
- **Technology**: better technology raises productivity and lowers costs, increasing supply (shift right).
- **Number of sellers**: more sellers increase supply; fewer sellers decrease it.
- **Expectations**: if sellers expect a higher future price, they may supply less today (current supply shifts left).
- **Government policies**: e.g. subsidies change producers' costs and incentives.

### 2.3 Market Equilibrium

**Equilibrium Price and Quantity**

- **Market equilibrium** occurs when quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, where the demand and supply curves intersect.
- The **equilibrium price** is the price at which buyers want to buy exactly the amount sellers want to sell. The **equilibrium quantity** is the amount bought and sold at that price.

Figure 3. A surplus above the equilibrium price and a shortage below it.

**Surplus and Shortage**

- **Surplus**: quantity supplied > quantity demanded, usually because price is **above** equilibrium. Sellers cannot sell everything, so price falls; quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls until the market reaches equilibrium.
- **Shortage**: quantity demanded > quantity supplied, usually because price is **below** equilibrium. Buyers compete for the limited goods, so price rises; quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises until the market reaches equilibrium.

**Changes in Market Equilibrium**

| **Change (other curve constant)** | **Equilibrium price** | **Equilibrium quantity** |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases | Rises | Falls |
| Demand and supply both increase | Ambiguous | Rises |

When both curves shift at the same time, the result depends on the direction and size of each shift. One variable always becomes **ambiguous** (it depends on which shift is larger).

### 2.4 Welfare Economics

**Welfare economics** studies how the allocation of resources affects the economic well-being of buyers and sellers. Consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus measure the benefits from market transactions.

**Willingness to Pay and Consumer Surplus**

- **Willingness to pay (WTP)**: the maximum a buyer will pay for a good; it measures how much the buyer values it.
- **Consumer surplus** = willingness to pay − price paid.
- On a graph: the area **below the demand curve and above the price**, up to the quantity bought. It is the net benefit buyers get from the market.

Figure 4. Consumer surplus (above price) and producer surplus (below price).

**Cost and Producer Surplus**

- A seller's **cost** is the value of everything the seller must give up to produce the good, including the opportunity cost of resources and the seller's time.
- For a single unit, producer surplus is price received minus the marginal opportunity cost of supplying that unit. For the firm as a whole in the short run, PS = TR − TVC. Economic profit = PS − TFC, so producer surplus and profit differ by fixed costs.
- On a graph: the area **above the supply curve and below the price**, up to the quantity sold. It is the net benefit sellers get from the market.

**Total Surplus and Allocative Efficiency**

- **Total surplus** = consumer surplus + producer surplus = total value to buyers − total cost to sellers. It represents the total gains from trade.
- **Allocative efficiency**: resources are allocated so that total surplus is maximized. In a competitive market without market failures, this happens at equilibrium, where **marginal benefit to consumers = marginal cost of production**.
- Producing **less** than the efficient quantity: some mutually beneficial trades do not happen.
- Producing **more** than the efficient quantity: the cost of the extra units exceeds the benefit to consumers.

### 2.5 Elasticity

**Definition of Elasticity**

- **Elasticity** measures how much one variable responds to a change in another.
- **Price elasticity of demand** measures how much quantity demanded responds to a change in price; **price elasticity of supply** measures how much quantity supplied responds.

**Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**

- **PED** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
- Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is negative with signs. AP Microeconomics classifies elasticity using the **absolute value**.

**The Midpoint Method**

- Uses the **average** of the starting and ending values, so the answer is the same whichever value is the starting point.
- % change in quantity = change in quantity ÷ average quantity × 100%.
- % change in price = change in price ÷ average price × 100%.
- Example: quantity rises from 40 to 60 while price falls from ＄30 to ＄20. % change in Q = 20 ÷ 50 = 40%; % change in P = 10 ÷ 25 = 40% (absolute). |PED| = 40% ÷ 40% = **1** (unit elastic).

**Types of Price Elasticity of Demand**

| **Type** | **\|PED\|** | **Meaning** | **Curve** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic | > 1 | Quantity changes by a larger % than price (10% price rise, more than 10% drop in Qd) | Flatter at the same P and Q |
| Unit elastic | = 1 | Quantity changes by the same % as price | – |
| Inelastic | < 1 | Quantity changes by a smaller % than price (10% price rise, less than 10% drop in Qd) | Steeper at the same P and Q |
| Perfectly inelastic | 0 | Quantity does not change when price changes | Vertical |
| Perfectly elastic | ∞ | Buyers buy at one price but nothing at any higher price | Horizontal |

**Factors Affecting PED**

- **Close substitutes**: more substitutes → more elastic, since consumers can switch easily.
- **Necessity vs luxury**: necessities are more inelastic than luxuries.
- **Definition of the market**: a narrowly defined good (e.g. one brand) has more substitutes, so it is more elastic than a broad category.
- **Time**: demand becomes more elastic over time as consumers adjust and find substitutes.

**Elasticity Along a Linear Demand Curve**

- A straight-line demand curve has a **constant slope** but **changing elasticity**.
- Upper part (high price, low quantity): **elastic**. Lower part (low price, high quantity): **inelastic**. Midpoint: **unit elastic**.
- So slope alone does not determine elasticity; elasticity depends on **percentage** changes.

**Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)**

- **PES** = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price.
- Elastic: PES > 1. Inelastic: PES < 1. Unit elastic: PES = 1.
- Perfectly inelastic: PES = 0 (vertical supply curve; quantity supplied cannot change). Perfectly elastic: PES → ∞ (horizontal supply curve).
- To compare steepness and elasticity, use the same axis scales and the same price–quantity point. Flatness alone does not determine elasticity. For example, every straight-line supply curve through the origin has PES = 1.
- Main determinant: **how easily producers can adjust output**. Supply is more elastic when firms have more time to change output, get resources, or expand capacity; in the short run it is often relatively inelastic.

### 2.6 Government Policies: Subsidies

**Definition of a Subsidy**

- A **subsidy** is a government payment to an individual or firm to encourage the purchase or supply of a good.
- A **producer subsidy** is paid to producers to lower production costs or raise the revenue they receive.
- Lump-sum subsidy: a fixed payment independent of the quantity produced or sold. It can be paid once or recur. It does not change MC or the short-run supply curve of an existing firm.
- **Per-unit subsidy**: a fixed payment for each unit produced.
- Governments use subsidies to increase production, support an industry, or encourage activities with social benefits.

Figure 5. A per-unit producer subsidy shifts supply to the right.

**Effects of a Per-Unit Producer Subsidy**

- The subsidy lowers the effective cost of each unit, so the **supply curve shifts right** and equilibrium quantity rises.
- The **price buyers pay falls**; the **effective price sellers receive** (market price + subsidy) **rises**. The gap between the two equals the subsidy per unit.
- **Government expenditure** = subsidy per unit × quantity subsidized.
- Subsidies have a fiscal cost. If they push production beyond the socially efficient quantity, they create **inefficiency** (deadweight loss).

### Module 54: The Production Function

**Inputs and Time Periods**

- **Production** = converting inputs into output.
- **Fixed input**: quantity cannot be changed for a period of time (e.g. the size of a store, machinery).
- **Variable input**: quantity the firm can change at any time (e.g. labor, raw materials).
- **Short run (SR)**: at least one input is fixed. The firm has a fixed plant.
- **Long run (LR)**: all inputs are variable. Firms can change plant size and can enter or exit the industry.

**Exam trap:** SR and LR are not fixed lengths of time (e.g. "1 year"). They are defined only by whether any input is fixed.

**Production Function**

A **production function** shows the relationship between the quantity of inputs a firm uses and the quantity of output it produces. The total product curve eventually gets flatter as more of the variable input is added (because of diminishing returns).

**Total, Marginal and Average Product**

- **Total product (TP)**: the total quantity of output produced.
- **Marginal product (MP or MPL)**: the change in TP when labor changes by one unit. MP = ΔTP / ΔL.
- **Average product (AP)**: output per unit of labor. AP = TP / L.

| **Labor (L)** | **TP** | **MP = ΔTP/ΔL** | **AP = TP/L** | **Stage** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | – | – |  |
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10.00 | Increasing marginal returns |
| 2 | 25 | 15 | 12.50 | Increasing marginal returns |
| 3 | 45 | 20 | 15.00 | Increasing marginal returns |
| 4 | 60 | 15 | 15.00 | Diminishing marginal returns |
| 5 | 70 | 10 | 14.00 | Diminishing marginal returns |
| 6 | 75 | 5 | 12.50 | Diminishing marginal returns |
| 7 | 75 | 0 | 10.71 | Diminishing (TP at maximum) |
| 8 | 70 | −5 | 8.75 | Negative marginal returns |

**Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns**

Assumptions: all units of the variable input are of equal quality, and technology is fixed.

As more of a **variable input** (workers) is added to a **fixed input** (machines, store space), the extra output from each new worker will **eventually fall**.

- **Why:** at first, adding workers lets idle machinery be used and lets workers specialize, so MP rises (increasing marginal returns).
- Later the fixed plant becomes crowded; workers wait to use equipment, so each new worker adds less (diminishing marginal returns). TP still rises, but more slowly.
- If too many workers are hired, they get in each other's way and TP falls (negative marginal returns, MP < 0).

**MP and AP Relationship**

- MP is the slope of the TP curve. TP is at its maximum when MP = 0.
- When **MP > AP**, AP rises. When **MP < AP**, AP falls.
- Therefore **MP crosses AP at AP's maximum** (in the table, between L = 3 and L = 4, where AP = 15).
- AP responds to diminishing returns later than MP, because it is an average of all workers so far.

**Analogy:** your average test score rises only if your next score (the marginal) is above your current average.

### Module 55: Firm Costs (Short Run)

**Total Cost Concepts**

- **Total fixed cost (TFC)**: costs that do not change with output; paid even at Q = 0 (e.g. rent, insurance, interest payments).
- **Total variable cost (TVC)**: costs that change with output; 0 at Q = 0 (e.g. wages, materials, fuel, electricity, transport).
- **Total cost (TC)** = TFC + TVC.

**Per-Unit (Average) Costs and Marginal Cost**

- **AFC** = TFC / Q
- **AVC** = TVC / Q
- **ATC** = TC / Q = AFC + AVC
- **MC** = ΔTC / ΔQ (also = ΔTVC / ΔQ, since TFC does not change). MC is the slope of the TC curve.

**Why MC Falls, Then Rises: Link to MP**

- When labor is the only variable input and the wage is constant, MC = wage / MP and AVC = wage / AP.
- While MP is **rising** (increasing returns), MC is **falling**.
- Once MP **falls** (diminishing returns), MC **rises**.
- In the same way, when AP is rising, AVC is falling; when AP falls, AVC rises.
- Key fact: maximum MP corresponds to minimum MC. MP and MC move inversely, as do AP and AVC, under a constant wage with labor as the only variable input. Their graphs use different horizontal axes (labor versus output), so they are not literal mirror images.

**Shapes of the Cost Curves**

- **TFC**: horizontal line.
- **TVC**: starts at 0. Rises at a decreasing rate while MC falls (increasing returns), then at an increasing rate once MC rises (diminishing returns).
- **TC**: same shape as TVC, shifted up by TFC. The vertical gap between TC and TVC is always TFC.
- **AFC**: always falls, because the same fixed cost is spread over more units.
- AVC falls while MC < AVC and rises while MC > AVC. It may keep falling after diminishing marginal returns begin, because MC can rise while remaining below AVC.
- **MC**: U-shaped (or "swoosh"-shaped); falls due to increasing marginal returns, rises due to **diminishing marginal returns**.
- **ATC**: U-shaped. Vertical gap between ATC and AVC = AFC, so the two curves get closer as Q rises.

Figure 7. Left: TC is TVC shifted up by TFC. Right: MC passes through the minimum points of AVC and ATC.

**Short-Run Cost Relationships (Must Know)**

| **Relationship** | **Explanation** |
|---|---|
| ATC = AFC + AVC | The vertical distance between ATC and AVC equals AFC at each output. |
| MC and ATC / AVC | MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minimum points. If the last unit cost less than the average, the average falls; if it cost more, the average rises. |
| MC and diminishing returns | MC is lowest where MP is highest. After that, diminishing returns set in and each extra unit costs more. |
| Order of minimums | Min MC comes first, then min AVC, then min ATC (ATC's minimum is to the right of AVC's, because falling AFC keeps pulling ATC down). |

**Why the ATC Curve Is U-Shaped (Self-study)**

Increasing output has two opposing effects on ATC:

- **Spreading effect**: more output spreads the fixed cost over more units, so AFC falls. Dominates at **low** output, so ATC falls.
- Diminishing returns effect: falling MP raises MC. Eventually MC exceeds AVC, so AVC rises. At sufficiently high output, the rise in AVC outweighs the fall in AFC and ATC rises.
- The output where ATC is lowest is the **minimum-cost output** (3 cases in the textbook's Selena's Gourmet Salsas example).

**Does the MC Curve Always Slope Upward? (Self-study)**

- No. A more realistic MC curve has a **"swoosh" shape**: it falls at very low output and then rises.
- At low output, hiring more workers allows **specialization**, giving increasing returns, so MC falls.
- Once specialization is achieved, diminishing returns set in and MC rises.
- With a swoosh MC, AVC is U-shaped too, and MC still crosses AVC at AVC's lowest point.

**Shifting Cost Curves**

**If fixed cost rises** (variable cost unchanged):

- Only **AFC and ATC** change (shift up). **MC and AVC do not change**, because they depend only on variable cost.
- Since MC is unchanged, a change in fixed cost does not change the firm's profit-maximizing output in the short run.

**If variable cost rises** (e.g. higher wages or material prices):

- **MC, AVC and ATC** all shift up. **AFC does not change**.

Figure 8. A fixed-cost increase shifts only ATC (and AFC); a variable-cost increase shifts MC, AVC and ATC.

### Module 56: Long-Run Costs and Economies of Scale

**Returns to Scale (Long-Run Production)**

In the long run all inputs are variable, so the firm can increase **all** inputs together in the cheapest combination.

- **Increasing returns to scale**: increasing all inputs by a given % raises output by a **larger** %.
- **Constant returns to scale**: output rises by the **same** %.
- **Decreasing returns to scale**: output rises by a **smaller** %.

**Short-Run vs Long-Run Average Total Cost**

Suppose Aiden's jade store is so popular it cannot finish all its orders. If he buys more equipment:

- Fixed cost rises immediately (renting or buying the equipment).
- Each worker has more equipment to work with, so **marginal product rises** and the **variable cost of each output level falls**.

More capital can raise worker productivity. In this example, the higher MP allows the same output to be produced with less labor, lowering variable cost at each output.

- In the long run a firm **chooses its fixed cost** (plant size). Each plant size has its own short-run ATC curve.
- The **long-run average total cost curve (LRATC)** shows the lowest possible ATC at each output when fixed cost has been chosen to minimize ATC for that output.
- LRATC is a smooth **"envelope"**: each SRATC curve touches (is tangent to) it at one point and lies above it everywhere else.
- Textbook example: a firm sized for 6 units that produces 6 is on both its SRATC and the LRATC (point C). If it produces only 3 in the short run, it is on its SRATC above the LRATC (point B). If it expects to stay at 3 for a long time, it cuts capital and moves down to the LRATC (point A). The same logic applies at 9 units (Y to X).
- Diminishing marginal returns do **not** explain the LRATC shape, because no input is fixed in the long run.

**Economies and Diseconomies of Scale**

| **Term** | **What happens to LRATC as output rises** | **Typical causes** |
|---|---|---|
| Economies of scale | Falls | Labor and managerial specialization; more efficient large machinery; lower shipping cost per unit; bulk discounts on raw materials; better interest rates; design and advertising costs spread over more units |
| Constant returns to scale | Stays the same | Benefits and problems of size balance out |
| Diseconomies of scale | Rises | Coordination and communication problems across many layers of management; bureaucracy and office politics; workers feel alienated and shirk more easily; more regulation and compliance costs |

- **Minimum efficient scale (MES)**: the **smallest** output at which LRATC is minimized.

Figure 9. LRATC as the envelope of short-run ATC curves.

**MES and Industry Structure**

- **Long flat bottom (wide constant returns)**: small and large firms can both be efficient, so firms of many sizes coexist.
- Large MES relative to total market demand: the market supports only a few firms operating at efficient scale. MES must be compared with market size, not judged as large in isolation.
- Small MES relative to total market demand: the market can support many efficient firms. Industry structure also depends on demand and other barriers to entry.

Figure 10. MES relative to market demand helps explain the number and size of firms.

**Distinguish: Diminishing Marginal Returns vs Returns to Scale**

|  | **Diminishing marginal returns** | **Returns to scale** |
|---|---|---|
| Time period | Short run | Long run |
| Inputs | At least one input held fixed; only the variable input increases | All inputs increase together |
| Explains | Shape of SR curves (MP, MC, AVC, ATC) | Shape of LRATC |

**Sunk Costs**

- A **sunk cost** has already been paid and cannot be recovered. It should be **ignored** when deciding what to do next.
- Example: you spent ＄250 on new brakes and the problem is not fixed. That ＄250 is gone whatever you choose next, so it should not affect the decision.

**Summary of Cost Concepts**

| **Period** | **Measure** | **Definition** | **Formula** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short run | Fixed cost | Cost that does not depend on output | FC |
| Short run | Average fixed cost | Fixed cost per unit | AFC = FC / Q |
| SR and LR | Variable cost | Cost that depends on output | VC |
| SR and LR | Average variable cost | Variable cost per unit | AVC = VC / Q |
| SR and LR | Total cost | Fixed + variable cost | TC = FC (SR) + VC |
| SR and LR | Average total cost | Total cost per unit | ATC = TC / Q |
| SR and LR | Marginal cost | Change in TC from one more unit | MC = ΔTC / ΔQ |
| Long run | LR average total cost | ATC when fixed cost is chosen to minimize ATC at each output | LRATC |

### Module 52: Defining Profit

**Revenue**

- **Total revenue (TR)** = P × Q.
- **Average revenue (AR)** = TR / Q = P. The demand curve shows P at each Q, so **D = AR = P**.
- **Marginal revenue (MR)** = ΔTR / ΔQ: the extra revenue from selling one more unit.

**Explicit vs Implicit Costs**

|  | **Explicit costs** | **Implicit costs** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Out-of-pocket payments to others for their resources | Opportunity cost of using the owner's own resources (no money changes hands) |
| Examples | Rent, wages, materials, electricity bills | Forgone salary, forgone rent, forgone interest on own capital, time |
| Counted by | Accountants and economists | Economists only |

**Accounting, Economic and Normal Profit**

- **Accounting profit** = TR − explicit costs.
- **Economic profit** = TR − (explicit + implicit costs). Economic profit is always ≤ accounting profit.
- **Normal profit** = zero economic profit: TR exactly covers explicit **and** implicit costs. It is the minimum return needed to keep the owner in the business, so economists treat normal profit as a **cost** (part of implicit costs).
- At normal profit, accounting profit equals implicit costs. It is positive when implicit costs are positive and can be zero when implicit costs are zero.

| **Outcome** | **Condition** |
|---|---|
| Economic (abnormal) profit | TR > explicit + implicit costs |
| Normal profit (zero economic profit) | TR = explicit + implicit costs |
| Economic loss | TR < explicit + implicit costs |

**Profit Maximization: MR = MC**

- Firms aim to maximize profit = TR − TC. Rather than compare totals, think **at the margin**:
  - If **MR > MC**: one more unit adds more revenue than cost, so profit rises. **Keep producing.**
  - If **MR < MC**: one more unit adds more cost than revenue, so profit falls. **Produce less.**
- For an interior optimum, choose the output where MC crosses MR from below, then compare producing with shutting down. MR = MC selects output; it does not guarantee positive economic profit. Compare price with ATC at that output to determine profit or loss. With discrete units, choose the quantity giving the highest TR − TC; exact equality may not occur.

### Section 10: Common Exam Traps

- Confusing **diminishing marginal returns** (SR, one input fixed) with **diseconomies of scale** (LR, all inputs variable).
- Saying fixed costs affect MC. They do not: **MC = ΔTVC / ΔQ**.
- Drawing MC through the minimum of AFC. MC crosses only **AVC and ATC** at their minimums.
- Letting ATC and AVC touch or cross. They get closer as Q rises (AFC shrinks) but never meet.
- Counting **sunk costs** in a future decision.
- Forgetting implicit costs (forgone salary, forgone interest) when computing **economic** profit.
- Thinking zero economic profit means the firm is doing badly. It means the owner earns exactly what they could earn elsewhere.

## Section 11 Perfect Competition

Modules 58–60. Use the market price to choose output, then compare that price with average costs to identify profit, loss, or shutdown.

### Short Run Output and Profit

- A perfectly competitive market has many buyers and sellers, identical products, and free entry and exit. Each firm is a price taker: it accepts the market price.
- The market demand curve slopes downward, but one firm faces a horizontal demand curve at the market price. For that firm, P = AR = MR.
- Choose the positive output where rising MC equals P = MR. Check whether producing covers variable costs before deciding to operate.

| **At the chosen output** | **Result** |
|---|---|
| P > ATC | Positive economic profit: (P − ATC) × Q |
| P = ATC | Normal profit: zero economic profit |
| AVC < P < ATC | Operate in the short run to minimize the loss |
| P below minimum AVC | Shut down. Revenue cannot cover variable costs at any positive output. |
| P = minimum AVC | Indifferent between shutdown and the minimum-AVC output; either loses TFC. |

### Why AVC Determines Shutdown

Fixed costs must be paid whether the firm produces or shuts down. Producing is worthwhile when TR exceeds TVC, because the remaining revenue helps pay fixed costs. If TR < TVC at every positive output, operating adds to the loss. Shutdown output is zero and the loss is TFC.

Example: rent is ＄100, AVC is ＄5, and price is ＄3. Producing 10 units gives TR = ＄30 and TC = ＄150, a ＄120 loss. Shutting down loses only the ＄100 rent.

### The Short Run Supply Curve

The rising MC curve at and above minimum AVC gives the firm’s positive-output supply. In a whole-unit table, choose the last unit whose MR is at least MC, provided total revenue covers total variable cost. Exact MR = MC is not required in discrete data.

### Long Run Entry Exit and Efficiency

- Economic profits attract entry. Market supply increases, price falls, and economic profits shrink.
- Economic losses cause exit. Market supply decreases, price rises, and the remaining firms’ losses shrink.
- In standard long-run competitive equilibrium, P = MR = MC = minimum ATC. Firms earn normal profit, including the owner’s opportunity cost. There is no incentive to enter or exit.

### Why Price Reaches Minimum ATC

A price above minimum ATC allows efficient firms to earn economic profit, attracting entry. A price below minimum ATC leaves firms unable to cover all economic costs, causing exit. At the equilibrium price, each firm chooses its best output and just covers ATC. MC meets ATC at ATC’s minimum.

### Industry Costs and Long Run Supply

| **Industry type** | **As the industry expands** | **After demand rises** |
|---|---|---|
| Constant cost | Input prices and firms’ minimum costs remain unchanged. | Output rises; long-run price returns to its original level. |
| Increasing cost | Input prices or firms’ minimum costs rise. | Output rises; long-run price ends higher. |
| Decreasing cost | External economies lower firms’ minimum costs. | Output rises; long-run price ends lower. |

### Efficiency and Innovation

- Allocative efficiency: P = MC. The value of the last unit to consumers equals its marginal cost. If P > MC, output is too low; if P < MC, output is too high in the standard model.
- Productive efficiency: production occurs at minimum ATC. Standard long-run perfect competition achieves both forms of efficiency.
- Innovation may create temporary economic profits. Entry and imitation tend to remove those profits. Creative destruction describes new products and businesses replacing older ones.

### Module 61 Monopoly Characteristics

- One seller supplies the market, so the firm is the industry. Its product has no close substitutes.
- High barriers to entry protect the firm from new competitors. Unlike a competitive firm, it faces the downward-sloping market demand curve.
- A price maker can choose a price–quantity combination on demand. It cannot independently choose both a high price and a large quantity that buyers will not purchase.
- A monopoly may advertise to increase demand. Monopoly status allows economic profit but does not guarantee it.

### Barriers to Entry

| **Barrier** | **How it limits entry** |
|---|---|
| Essential resources | Control of a resource or location that competitors need. |
| Legal protection | Patents, exclusive franchises, or licensing restrictions can limit competitors. |
| Economies of scale | One firm can serve the market at a lower total cost than several firms splitting output. |
| Network effects | A product becomes more valuable as more people use it, making an established network difficult to challenge. |

### Natural Monopoly

A natural monopoly exists when one firm can supply the relevant market output at a lower cost than multiple firms. High fixed costs and economies of scale over the relevant demand range often cause it. Lower production cost does not automatically mean a low selling price; regulation may still be needed.

Slide example: one firm makes 200 units at ＄10 each, for a total cost of ＄2,000. Two firms each make 100 units at ＄15 each, for a combined total cost of ＄3,000. One supplier uses fewer resources for the same 200 units.

### Monopoly Revenue Output and Price

#### Why Marginal Revenue Is Below Price

For a single-price monopolist, selling more usually requires lowering the price on every unit sold. The extra unit adds revenue, but the lower price reduces revenue on the units already being sold. Therefore MR < P at positive output on a downward-sloping demand curve.

From the slides: increasing sales from 3 to 4 units lowers price from ＄142 to ＄132. The fourth unit adds ＄132, but the first three units each lose ＄10 in revenue. MR = ＄132 − ＄30 = ＄102, which is below the new price of ＄132.

#### How to Read the Graph

1. Find the output Qm where MC crosses MR from below.

2. Move vertically up from Qm to demand to find price Pm. Do not use the MR height as price.

3. Read ATC at Qm. Economic profit = (Pm − ATC) × Qm. A positive rectangle means profit; a negative one means loss.

4. If the firm makes a loss, compare TR with TVC. In the short run, operate if revenue covers variable costs; otherwise shut down.

Figure 11. Illustrative curves. Left: Qm = 6, Pm = ＄14, ATC = ＄7, and profit = ＄42. Right: output below the efficient quantity creates deadweight loss.

#### Elasticity and Revenue

On a linear demand curve, MR is positive in the elastic range, zero at unit elasticity, and negative in the inelastic range. With positive MC, a profit-maximizing single-price monopoly chooses the elastic range. In the inelastic range, reducing output raises revenue and lowers variable cost. If MC is zero, the optimum can occur at unit elasticity.

### Common Pricing Mistakes

The highest possible price does not necessarily maximize total profit because few units may sell. Maximizing revenue is also different from maximizing profit: this table’s revenue peaks at 9 units, but profit peaks at 5. A monopoly can make losses if demand is too weak or costs are too high.

### Module 62 Monopoly Welfare and Public Policy

#### Output Price and Deadweight Loss

With the same demand and marginal cost conditions, an unregulated single-price monopoly chooses MR = MC and charges P > MC. It produces less and charges more than the efficient P = MC benchmark. The lost trades between Qm and the efficient output have willingness to pay above marginal cost, so their absence creates deadweight loss.

Consumer surplus is below demand and above the selling price up to Qm. Producer surplus is revenue minus variable cost. Part of lost consumer surplus becomes producer surplus; the deadweight-loss triangle is lost total surplus, not a transfer to the monopolist.

#### Cost Efficiency and Innovation

A monopoly generally does not produce at minimum ATC. X-inefficiency means producing a given output at a higher cost than necessary. Weak competition can reduce pressure to control costs, but monopoly does not always imply higher production costs or no innovation. Natural monopolies can have cost advantages, and innovation incentives depend on the circumstances.

#### Regulating a Natural Monopoly

Figure 12. Natural monopoly with constant MC. Fair return is where demand meets ATC. Socially optimal output is where demand meets MC.

Socially optimal pricing sets P = MC and achieves allocative efficiency. When ATC exceeds MC, the firm makes a loss at this price and may need a subsidy. Fair-return pricing sets P = ATC, covers all economic costs, and gives normal profit. In this natural-monopoly example it produces less than the efficient quantity.

Other policy responses include antitrust action where appropriate and public ownership. Breaking up a natural monopoly can raise production costs by losing economies of scale.

### Module 63 Price Discrimination and Monopoly Taxes

#### Price Discrimination

Price discrimination means charging different prices for the same good or service when the differences are not explained by production costs. It requires market power, a way to distinguish buyers by willingness to pay, and a way to prevent resale.

- Group pricing: charge the less price-sensitive group a higher price and the more elastic group a lower price. Student discounts and coupons can help identify more price-sensitive buyers.
- For separate groups, choose sales so each group’s MR equals the common MC, then read each group’s price from its demand curve.
- A price difference alone does not prove discrimination. Peak-period prices, for example, may also reflect higher costs.

#### Perfect Price Discrimination

A perfectly discriminating seller charges each buyer their maximum willingness to pay for each unit. In the ideal model it sells all units whose willingness to pay is at least MC, so output reaches the efficient D = MC quantity. Consumer surplus becomes zero, and the seller captures the gains from trade as producer surplus. Economic profit still subtracts fixed costs. This removes the standard monopoly deadweight loss but changes who receives the surplus.

#### Taxes on a Monopoly

| **Tax** | **Cost curves** | **Output and price** |
|---|---|---|
| Per-unit tax | Raises MC, AVC, and ATC. | In the standard interior case, output falls and price rises; profit falls. |
| Lump-sum tax | Raises TFC, AFC, and ATC; MC is unchanged. | Output and price stay unchanged if the firm continues operating; profit falls. A sufficiently large avoidable tax can affect operation or exit. |

#### Perfect Competition and Monopoly

| **Feature** | **Perfect competition** | **Single-price monopoly** |
|---|---|---|
| Demand facing one firm | Horizontal | Downward-sloping market demand |
| Revenue | P = AR = MR | P = AR > MR |
| Output rule | MR = MC, subject to shutdown | MR = MC, subject to shutdown |
| Price at optimum | P = MC | P > MC in the standard interior case |
| Long-run economic profit | Zero in equilibrium | Can persist behind entry barriers |
| Efficiency | P = MC and minimum ATC in long-run equilibrium | Generally below efficient output and away from minimum ATC |
