**Quadrilaterals**

**Determinations of a Parallelogram**

There are five unique ways to determine if a quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

- The two opposite sides are equal in length
- One pair of opposite side is equal in length and is parallel.
- Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel.
- Opposite angles are congruent
- Diagonals bisect each other

-Note that the above rules also apply to the identities of a parallelogram

**Special Parallelograms**

**Rectangles**

A rectangle is a parallelogram with four right angles. Thus, it has all the properties of parallelograms. Given a quadrilateral, a rectangle can be determined:

- By having 3 right angles
- By being a parallelogram and having **Congruent Diagonals**

-This also sums up the properties of rectangles.

**Rhombuses**

A rhombus is a parallelogram with four congruent sides. Thus, it has all the properties of parallelograms. Given a quadrilateral, a rhombus can be determined:

- By having four congruent sides
- By being a parallelogram and having **Perpendicular Diagonals**
- By being a parallelogram and having two adjacent sides congruent.

-This also sums up the properties of rhombuses.

**Kite:** A quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent sides congruent and **no opposite sides** **congruent, has perpendicular diagonals.**

**Squares**

A square is a parallelogram with four congruent sides and four right angles. Since a square is both a rhombus and a rectangle, it has all the properties of a rhombus and a rectangle. A square can be determined if:

- A rectangle has two adjacent sides congruent
- A rhombus contains a right angle

In general, to prove a quadrilateral as a square, prove it to have the properties of both a rectangle(equiangular) **AND** a rhombus(equilateral)

**Trapezoids**

A trapezoid is a quadrilateral with **exactly** one pair of parallel sides

The parallel sides are its **bases**, the non parallel sides are its **legs**. The (shortest) distance between the two bases is the **height** of the trapezoid. Two angles that share a base are the **base angles** of a trapezoid.

**Special Trapezoids**

**Isosceles Trapezoids**

A trapezoid with two congruent legs is an isosceles trapezoid. A trapezoid is determined as an isosceles trapezoid

- By having two congruent legs
- By having a pair of congruent base angles
- By having two congruent diagonals. (Proven by drawing heights)

This also sums up the properties of isosceles trapezoids.

**Right Trapezoids:** Trapezoids with a right angle (Self-explanatory)

**Midsegment:** A midsegment in a triangle/trapezoid is a segment connecting the midpoint of two sides/legs

**Triangle Midsegment Theorem:** If a segment joins the midpoints of two sides of a triangle, then the segment is parallel to the third side, and half its length.

**Trapezoid Midsegment Theorem:** If a segment joins the midpoints of two legs of a trapezoid, then the segment is parallel to its bases, and half the sum of its bases.

**Similar Polygons**

Two figures that have the same shape, but not necessarily the same size, are similar.

Two polygons are similar **if and only if:**

- Corresponding angles are congruent.
- Corresponding sides are proportional.

The ratio of the lengths of corresponding sides is the **similarity ratio**

**Similar Triangles**

If ΔABC is similar to ΔA’B’C’, then we write the similarity statement ΔABC ~ ΔA’B’C’. It can be determined by:

- Two corresponding angles are congruent(AA)
- Two sides are proportional and their included angle is congruent(SAS)
- All three sides are proportional(SSS)

4. Right triangles with proportional hypotenuse and leg(HL)

~Q9: Prove ΔABC~ΔFDE.

With the knowledge of similar triangles, we may also derive relationship between segments intercepted by parallel lines.

**Side Splitter Theorem:** If a line is parallel to one side of a triangle and intersects the lines containing the other two sides, then it divides the segments proportionally.

**Corollary of Side Splitter Theorem:** If 2+ parallel lines are on 2 transversals, then segments of the transversals are proportionate.

In addition, if a ray bisects an angle A of ΔABC to meet BC at D, we could use similar triangles/isosceles triangle to prove:

**Angle Bisector Theorem:** AB/AC = BD/CD (Reason: similar triangles, converse of isosceles triangle theorem…)

Note that the theorems above could also be applied by their converse. (For example, if a triangle is split by a ray passing through one of its vertex, and the long leg:long side=short leg:short side, then the ray is an angle bisector)

You may use this equation to determine the areas of other polygons.

Moreover, the areas ratio of similar polygons can be derived as the square of its similarity ratio.
