1-3 Points, Lines and Planes

- Point: a location; no size; represented by a small dot and is named by a capital letter
- Line: a series of points that extends in two opposite directions without end, named as ⃖"""⃗

AB or AB or a lowercase letter

- Collinear points: points that lie on the same line
- Plane: a flat surface that has no thickness; contains infinite lines; extends without end in the directions of all its lines; named by at least three non- collinear points or one greek alphabet
- Coplanar: points or/and lines that are in the same plane
- Postulate (axiom): an accepted statement or fact that can be used to prove theorems, while itself cannot be proved

(Below are some examples, better to understand instead of memorize them!)

- Through any two points there is exactly one line
- If two lines intersect, then they intersect in exactly one point
- If two planes intersect, then they intersect in exactly one line
- Through any three noncollinear points there is exactly one plane

1-4 Segments, Rays, Parallel Lines, and Planes

- Segment: part of a line consisting of 2 endpoints and all points between them.
- Ray: part of a line consisted by 1 endpoint and all the points of the line on one side of the endpoint. (Must start from the endpoint when writing!)
- Opposite rays: two distinct collinear rays with only one common end-point.
- Parallel: not intersecting.
- Skew: non-coplanar, not parallel, and not intersecting.

1-5 Measuring Segments

- Ruler Postulate: The points of a line can be put into one-to-one correspondence with the real numbers so that the distance between any two points is the absolute value of the corresponding numbers.
- Congruent Segments ≌: Segments that are equal in length.
- Segment Addition Postulate: If three points A, B and C are collinear, and B is between A and C, then AB + BC = AC
- Midpoint: a point that divides/bisects the whole segment into two congruent segments.

2-1 Conditional Statements

- A **conditional** statement is also known as an **"if-then"** statement. Conditional: $p \to q$, "if p, then q".
- $p$ is the **hypothesis**; $q$ is the **conclusion**.
- **Truth value:** either true or false (not always true!).
- **Converse:** the flipped-over version of a conditional statement. Converse: $q \to p$, "if q, then p".

- When a conditional and its converse are both true, you can combine them into a true **biconditional** by joining the two parts with the phrase "**if and only if**". Biconditional: $p \leftrightarrow q$, "p if and only if q".

## 5-4 Inverses, Contrapositives, and Indirect Reasoning

- **Negation:** $\sim p$, "not p".
- **Inverse:** if not p, then not q: $\sim p \to \sim q$.
- **Contrapositive:** if not q, then not p: $\sim q \to \sim p$.
- **Equivalent statements** share the same truth value: a conditional and its contrapositive; a converse and its inverse.

**Steps of an indirect proof:**

1. State an assumption (the negation) of what you want to prove.
2. Show that this assumption leads to a contradiction.
3. Conclude that the assumption is false, so what you want to prove must be true.
