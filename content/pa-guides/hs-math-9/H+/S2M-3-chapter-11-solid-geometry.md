## 1.2 Drawing, Nets and Other Models

**TERM: Isometric Drawing**

An isometric drawing is drawn on isometric dot paper, and shows three faces of the object.

Normally, the front, left/right, and top faces would be shown. To draw an isometric drawing, follow the below steps:

TERM: Orthographic Drawing

An orthographic drawing shows the front, side, and top views in separate figures. Edges that could be seen from perspective would be denoted with solid lines, edges that are blocked from perspective would be denoted with dashed lines.

TERM: Foundation Drawing

Foundation drawing always shows the top view of the figure, with numbers marked on each grid, indicating its height.

While drawing isometric, orthographic and foundation drawings, always remember to label the front, right, (top) views.

TERM: Net

A net is a two-dimensional pattern that you can fold to form a 3D figure. A net shows all of the surfaces of a figure in one view. One object can have different nets. Common nets for figures are shown below.

While drawing a net, the question sometimes requires you to label the net with its dimensions. Make sure that every dimension given in the question is labeled clearly either along the edge or connecting to edge with arrow.

## 11.1 Space Figures and Cross Sections

TERM: Polyhedron, Face, Vertex, Edge

A **polyhedron** is a three-dimensional figure whose surfaces are polygons. Each polygon is a **face** of the polyhedron. An **edge** is a segment that is formed by the intersection of two faces. A **vertex** is a point where three or more edges intersect.

Vertex is expressed as a capitalized letter: A, B, C, D

Edge is expressed as two vertices joined together with a line segment above: $\overline{AB}$

Face is expressed as the shape of the polygon, followed by all its vertexes listed out in either clockwise or anticlockwise: $\Delta \text{ ABC }$

FORMULA: Euler’s Formula

The numbers of faces (F), vertices (V), and edges (E) of a polyhedron are related by the formula **F + V = E + 2.**

Example: cube has 6 faces, 8 vertices, 12 edges: 6+8=12+2

In two dimensions, this reduces to **F + V = E + 1**.

Note: need to exclude the region outside the whole net

Example:

F=8, V=22, E=29

F+V=E+1

## 11.2 Surface Areas and Volume of Prisms and Cylinders

TERM: Prism, base, lateral face, lateral area, altitude, height

A prism is a polyhedron with exactly two congruent, parallel faces, called bases. Other faces are lateral faces. The total area of the lateral faces is the lateral area. You name a prism by the shape of its bases. An altitude of a prism is a perpendicular segment that joins the planes of the bases. The height h of the prism is the length of an altitude.

FORMULA:

Lateral Area LA=p*h (p is perimeter of base, h is height)

Surface Area SA = LA+2b (b is base area)

Volume V=B*h (h height of prism, B area of Base)

TERM: Cylinder

A cylinder has two congruent parallel bases. However, the bases of a cylinder are circles. An altitude of a cylinder is a perpendicular segment that joins the planes of the bases. The height h of a cylinder is the length of an altitude.

FORMULA:

LA = 2pi * rh (r radius of base, h height)

SA = LA + 2B

V = pi r2h (r radius, h height)

While finding the volume of a sector of the cylinder, find the corresponding arc first and then count in 2rh for the lateral areas.

## 11.3 Surface Area and Volume of Pyramids and Cones

TERMS: pyramid, base, lateral faces, vertex, altitude, height, slant height

A pyramid is a polyhedron in which the base can be any polygon and the other lateral faces are triangles that meet at a common vertex (called the vertex of the pyramid).

You can name a pyramid by the shape of its base. The altitude of a pyramid is the perpendicular segment from the vertex to the plane of the base. The length of the altitude is the height h of the pyramid. The slant height is the length of the altitude of a lateral face of the pyramid.

FORMULA:

LA = 1/2 pl (p perimeter of base, l slant height)

SA = LA + B (B area of base)

V = 1/3 B h (B area of base, h height)

While solving problems, watch out the difference between lateral edge, slant height, and height. Use Pythagorean theorem to transfer between these three.

TERMS: cone, right cone

A cone is “pointed” like a pyramid, but its base is a circle. In a right cone, the altitude is a perpendicular segment from the to the center of the base. The height h is the length of the altitude. The slant height is the distance from the vertex to a point on the edge of the base.

FORMULA:

LA=pi*r*l (r base radius, l slant height)

SA=LA+B (B base area)

V = 1/3 pi r2 h (r radius, h height)

## 11.4 Sphere

TERMS: sphere, center, radius, diameter, great circle, hemisphere

A sphere is the set of all points in space equidistant from a given point called the center. A radius is a segment that has one endpoint on the center and the other endpoint on the sphere. A diameter is a segment passing through the center with endpoints on the sphere.

When a plane and a sphere intersect in more than one point, the intersection is a circle. If the center of the circle is also the center of the sphere, the circle is called a great circle of the sphere. The circumference of a great circle is the circumference of the sphere. A great circle divides a sphere into two hemispheres.

FORMULA:

SA = 4 pi r2 (SA surface area, r radius)

V = 4/3 pi r3 (V volume, r radius)

## 11.4 Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids

TERMS: Similar solids, similarity ratio

Similar solids have the same shape, with all dimensions proportional. The ratio of corresponding linear dimensions of two similar solids is the similarity ratio.

FORMULA:

V1=k3V (V1 Volume of object 1, V Volume of object, k similarity ratio of object to object 1)

S1=k2S (S1 Volume of object 1, S Volume of object, k similarity ratio of object to object 1)
