**4.4: Newton’s Second Law**

Statement: **The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, and is inversely proportional to the object’s mass. The direction of the force is the same as the direction of acceleration, or** **F = ma, where F stands for** **net** **force, m stands for mass, and a stands for acceleration.**

Note that this section basically explains only this formula; it will be used more extensively later on.

It is highly recommended, if not mandatory, to draw a Full Body Diagram (FBD for short) when doing problems about force, as it will help you discern all the forces and what you need to calculate. The formula, F = ma, will be used extensively since the y-axis forces are usually balanced (i.e., the normal force and gravity cancel each other out), essentially stating that the magnitude of the normal force equates to the magnitude of gravity if no other external factors (This then links to friction as fkf = μkFN).

A useful velocity formula to remember (when acceleration is constant):

Vf2 = vi2 + 2ad

**4.5: Newton’s Third Law**

Statement: **If A exerts force on B, then B exerts and equal force in** **magnitude** **on A.***

*Cannot be cancelled (not the same system).

**Balance Force & Action-Reaction:**

Both have the **same magnitude and opposite direction**, but they have a few differences:

Same System vs. Opposite system

Occurring at different times vs. Occurring at the same time

Not always the same type of force vs. Always the same type of force

Doesn’t change motion vs. May change motion, respectively.

## 4.7 Solving Problems with Newton’s Laws: Free-Body Diagrams

- Newton’s Third Law: Every action has an equal and opposite reaction.
- Equilibrium means net force is zero: Object is either at rest or moves at constant velocity.
- Free-body diagrams are essential: Draw all forces acting on an object before solving.
- Tension problems: Use force balance at junctions; multiple ropes share the load.
- Connected systems with friction: The maximum hanging mass before sliding depends on static friction and the mass on the table.
- Action–reaction examples:
  - In a tug of war, the winning team pushes harder on the ground.
  - When two skaters push apart, the lighter one moves faster.

## 4.8 Problems Involving Friction, Inclines

- Static vs. kinetic friction: Static friction prevents motion; kinetic friction acts during motion.
- Maximum acceleration without slipping: Determined by the coefficient of static friction.
- Pushing vs. pulling: Pulling is often easier because it reduces normal force and friction.
- Inclined planes:
  - Forces split into components parallel and perpendicular to the incline.
  - For an object just resting, μs=tan⁡θμs​=tanθ.
  - Motion on an incline depends on angle, friction, and applied forces.
- Applied forces on inclines:
  - Force parallel to the incline affects acceleration directly.
  - Horizontal applied forces must be resolved into incline components.
- Friction is independent of mass in many simple incline problems (e.g., μsμs​ doesn’t change if mass doubles).

**FBD on an inclined plane:**

The most common variant is a box, or an object, resting on an inclined plane with an n-degree incline. It is recommended to draw the Cartesian plane parallel to the incline. If done like so, the angle between the vector of gravity and the y-axis will form the same angle as the incline. If there is a disparity between the Cartesian plane and a vector, it should be split into two components (shown in blue). There may be variants (i.e., the box is moving up/down the incline), but it can be easily resolved.

**1.4: Measurement & Uncertainty**

**Significant Figures (Digits):**

**Measurement:** The comparison between an **unknown** **quantity** and a **standard**

The last digit of a measurement is known as the **uncertain digit**, where you round at the very end.

**5** **Points to remember for Significant Digits(sf):**

- All nonzero digits & any zeros contained between non-zero digits count:
  - 300042 => 6 sf
- Exact numbers have an infinite number of significant digits:
  - 5 apples
- Leading zeros after the decimal point if the measurement is less than one (measurements can’t be less than zero either) don’t count:
  - 0.00034 => 2 Sf
- Trailing zeros count if there is a decimal point:
  - 3.00 => 3 sf **OR** 0.0003400 => 4 sf
- If there is a decimal point added at the very end of a number with trailing zeros, they count as significant figures:
  - 1900000. <= Period here

**Scientific Notation** should be used if there are multiple trailing zeros (that don’t count as significant figures) or if the number is larger than 100.

**Accuracy & Precision:**

**Accuracy: How close a measurement is to the true value (accepted value)**

**Precision: The repeatability of the measurement using a given instrument**

**Random Error: Caused by precision limitations of measurement device(s)**

**Systematic Error: Reproducible inaccuracies that are consistently in the same direction**

**Least Count: The smallest division marked on an instrument (only one significant figure, round if needed)**

**Instrument Limit of Error (ILE): Half of Least Count**

**Relative Error = (Measured value – expected value) / expected value**

**Percent Error = uncertainty / measured value**

**Note that Uncertainty is always one significant figure, and the measured value should match its decimal point.**

**Propagation & Absolute Errors:**

Absolute uncertainty is always increasing in addition/subtraction, as the uncertainties are simply added together.

For **Multiplication & Division**, consider the uncertainty and measured values as **different** steps:

Calculate the division/multiplication of the measured values first, then use the percent error of the values to find the uncertainty.

For **Exponents**, it is the same as multiplication & division, though you need to multiply the exponent value to the specific percent uncertainty:

X = yazb

X = |a|((Δy)/y) + |b|((Δz)/z)

#### 1–5 Units, Standards, and the SI System

**SI Base Units:**
There are seven fundamental base units in the International System of Units (SI): meter for length, kilogram for mass, second for time, kelvin for temperature, mole for amount of substance, ampere for electric current, and candela for luminous intensity. All other units are derived from these.
