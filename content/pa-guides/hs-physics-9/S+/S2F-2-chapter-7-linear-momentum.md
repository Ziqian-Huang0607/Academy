**7-1: Momentum and Its Relation to Force and Impulse**

Momentum is the product of the object’s mass and its velocity.

p = mv

Δp = mΔv

Momentum is a vector, and its direction is the same as the velocity provided in the formula. The unit is kg*m/s.

The two things to consider when calculating momentum are mass and velocity. A car may have 1/3 of the mass of a specific truck, but it will have the same momentum if it has triple the truck’s velocity.

**7-2: Conservation of Momentum**

When the net external force on a system is zero, the total momentum of the system is conserved. So, when two objects clash together and the net external force on a system is zero, the final momentum of both objects is equal to the initial momentum of said objects:

pi = pf

mAvAi + mBvBi = mAvAf + mBvBf

The Conservation of Momentum is considerably useful for problems that involve recoil, such as rocket propulsion, or the knockback force experienced when firing a gun. For instance, when the fuel burned from a rocket is released, the total momentum remains unchanged, as the fuel travels in one direction, propelling the rocket in the opposite direction, balancing the total momentum.

**7-3: Collision and Impulse**

The product of the force on an object and the time interval over which it acts is called its impulse. From here, we can derive that

I = FΔt

As we all know, F = ma, and plugging this into the first formula provided from the definition, we get

I = m(aΔt) = mΔv (Acceleration is change in velocity over change in time, a=Δv/Δt), which is equivalent to the change in momentum.

**Note: This equation assumes that the force mentioned is constant.**

**7-4: Conservation of Energy and Momentum in Collisions**

There are three kinds of collisions, two of which will be tested on the finals:

Elastic Collisions: Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.

Inelastic Collisions: Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy decreases.

Superelastic/Explosive Collisions: Total kinetic energy increases (Not tested).

**7-5: Elastic Collisions in One Dimension**

In this situation, the objects colliding are considered to be indestructible (permanent deformation is considered to be inelastic), and there are no other forms of energy that are produced (e.g., heat, sound, etc.). At the atomic level, collisions between atoms and/or molecules are considered to be elastic.

There are two characteristics of elastic collisions:

- Conservation of Momentum
- Conservation of Kinetic Energy

Through this, we have two equations:

mAvAi + mBvBi = mAvAf + mBvBf

mAv2Ai + mBv2Bi = mAv2Af + mBv2Bf (The coefficient of 1/2 was simplified for convenience)

After plugging in and simplifying values, we can derive a new equation:

vAi - vBi = - (vAf - vBf)

The relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation.

**7-6: Inelastic Collisions**

This is the type of collision in which the total kinetic energy decreases. Usually, objects made with soft, sticky substances, such as clay, behave in such a manner. The loss in kinetic energy is converted to other forms of energy, such as thermal or sound energy, meaning that the total energy is also conserved.

KEAi + KEBi = KEAf + KEBf + Eother

A collision is considered to be completely inelastic if the two objects within the collision stick together. In such situations, the maximum amount of kinetic energy possible is converted to other forms.

pi = pf

mAvAi + mBvBi = (mA + mB) vf (final velocity the same; the two objects are now connected)
