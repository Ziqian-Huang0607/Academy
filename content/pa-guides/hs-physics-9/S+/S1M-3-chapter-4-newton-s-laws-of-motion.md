**Forces** **(N or kgm/s^2):**

**Force:** A push or pull on an object; an interaction between **two** objects that are:

**System:** The receiver of the force

**Agent:** The object that causes the force to be exerted onto the system.

There are **three** factors:

- Point of Application (**PoA**)
- Magnitude
- Direction

There are also **four** fundamental forces that are gravity, strong, weak, and electromagnetic force.

**Spring Force**:

The force exerted by the spring on an attached object.

**Fsp = -kx** (before elastic limit is surpassed)

**Elastic Limit**: A point beyond which the spring will no longer return to its original shape.

**Normal Force**:

A **contact** force that is always perpendicular to the surface, as long as the molecules between the two objects are compressed against each other. **Note**: If the object is in a circular surface, such as bowl, the normal force’s direction is towards the **center** of the circle.

**Tension**:

The pull exerted by a string, rope, or cable when attached to a body and pulled taut. **Things to Note:** Tension is away from the object & parallel to the string. It has the **same** magnitude along the rope if **mass is ignored**.

**Friction**:

The **contact force** that acts to oppose sliding motion between 2 objects.

**Sliding > Rolling > Fluid** (in terms of force)

Friction depends on only the material of the surface and the normal force between the two objects. It **DOES NOT** rely on the surface area (contact area) between the two objects. The static friction is equal to the applied force.

**Formulas**:

The formulas for static and kinetic friction are extremely similar. However, static friction **depends** on the applied force, and it pushes back with the same amount of **force** until it reaches the **maximum static friction**. The moment the object moves, the friction becomes **kinetic** friction.

**Newton’s First Law**:

Also known as the **law of inertia**, where it states that “**Every object continues in its state of rest, or of uniform velocity in a straight line, as long as no net force acts on it.**”
