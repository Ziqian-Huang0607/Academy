**Basic Idea of Force**

-Force: a push or pull exerted by one object on another (vector)

-Interaction between 2 objects

-System: to whom the force is exerted

-Agent: by whom the force is exerted

-Effects: cause a deformation (a change in dimension/shape) or to change the state of motion (change velocity)

-Unit: Newton (N) 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

-Measurement device: spring scale

-Larger force → spring is stretched longer → larger reading

Three factors of forces:

- Magnitude: the strength of the force vector
- Direction: in which direction the force vector acts
- Point of application: the location the force is exerted

**Different Types of Forces**

Four fundamental forces:

- Strong Nuclear Force: The strongest force, responsible for holding protons and neutrons together in the nucleus of an atom
- Electromagnetic Force: This force acts between charged particles and is responsible for electricity, magnetism, and light
- Weak Nuclear Force: Responsible for processes like beta decay in nuclear reactions
- Gravity: The weakest force, it attracts objects with mass towards each other, governing the motion of planets and galaxies

-These forces govern how objects and particles interact in the universe

Contact forces and Field forces:

- Contact force: an object from the external world touches a system and thereby exerts a force on it
- Field force: exerted without contact

-Examples of Contact Forces: Friction, Drag force (air resistance / fluid resistance) Tension, Normal force, Spring force, Applied force

-Examples of Field Forces: Gravitational force, Electric force, Magnetic force

-Non-fundamental forces: Consequences of fundamental forces

Hooke’s Law: The magnitude of the force is directly proportional to the amount of stretch or compression within the limit of a spring

Formula: Fsp = –kx

Fsp — the force exerted by the spring on the attached object (N)

x — the displacement of the spring end from its equilibrium position (cm)

k — spring constant (N/cm)

“–” — F is opposite to x

Load (N) vs. Extension (cm)

-Extension is proportional to load from the origin to the elastic limit

Elastic limit: the point after which the spring will not return to its original shape

Types of Friction:

- Sliding Friction: the friction that occurs when two solid surfaces slide over each other

-Static Friction (Ffs): Caused by tendency of relative motion. (no relative motion between 2 objects in touch)

-Kinetic friction (Ffk): Caused by the relative motion (relative motion between 2 objects in touch)

- Rolling Friction: the friction that occurs when an object rolls over a surface (smaller than sliding friction)
- Fluid Friction: the friction that occurs when an object moves through a fluid

-Rolling < sliding; Fluid < sliding

Magnitude of Friction:

- Static Friction: the same as the force causing the relative motion.
- Maximum static friction (Ffs max = μs x FN)

-Ffs max — The max static friction

-μs — The coefficient of static friction (depends on how rough the surface is: μs ↑, rougher)

-FN — The normal force between two surfaces

**Equation for kinetic friction**

-Same objects: Ffk ≤ Ffs max

-Ffk = μk x FN

-coefficients of friction can also be bigger than 1, when the surface is sticky

Direction of friction: opposing the relative motion

Air resistance depends on:

- Shape of the object → Streamline: reduce friction
- Size of an object → Surface area ↑ → F_air ↑
- Speed of an object → Speed ↑ → F_air ↑
- Fluid type: viscosity → More viscous → F_air ↑

**Combining Forces**

-Draw vector sum graphically

- Method 1 (connect vectors from tip to tail)

-Place component vectors tip-to-tail

-Draw the resultant vector by connecting tail of first to tip of second

- Method 2 (Parallelogram rule)

-Place component vectors tail-to-tail

-Use the two vectors as adjacent sides and construct a parallelogram

-The diagonal from the common origin is the resultant vector

**Balanced forces**

-Equilibrant and balanced force → two forces are balanced

-Forces are equilibrant when two different forces act on the same object (same point of application), with the same magnitude, opposite direction, and acts on the same line

-Equilibrium: at rest / uniform motion (no acceleration)

## 4.2 Newton’s First Law of Motion

**Newton’s First Law**

-First Law:Every object continues in its state of rest, or of uniform velocity in a straight line, as long as no net force acts on it

-Inertia: Newton’s first law is also called the law of inertia

-Inertia is the tendency of an object to maintain its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line

-Not a force because forces are interactions between two objects

Inertia depends on: Mass, Larger mass → greater inertia

**Inertial Reference Frames (IRF)**

-In accelerating frame of reference, Newton’s First Law does not hold

-Reference frames in which Newton’s first law does hold are called: inertial reference frames (Law of inertia is valid in them)

-Earth’s frame of reference is close to IRF

-A reference frame moving with constant v relative to an IRF is also an IRF

-Reference frames where the law of inertia does not hold are called non-inertial reference frames

## 4.3 Mass

-Newton says: mass is a quantity of matter → Not well defined

-Inertial Mass: mass is equal to the ratio of the net force exerted on an object to its acceleration

-Mass: a measure of inertia of an object

-A measure of the object’s resistance to any type of force

-Gravitational mass: Mass as used in the law of universal gravitation determines the size of the gravitational force between two objects.

The principle of equivalence:

-Newton: inertial mass and gravitational mass are equal in magnitude.

-All experiments conducted so far have supported this principle.

## 4.4 Newton’s Second Law of Motion

-The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, and is inversely proportional to its mass. The direction of the acceleration is in the direction of the net force acting on the object

Formula: ∑F = m x a

-∑ F — vector sum of all forces, net force (“Σ” = “sum of”)

Unit: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

-1 N: the force required to give a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²

Steps to Utilize Newton’s Second Law:

- Identify the system
- Draw FBD, free body diagram (all the forces acting on the object)
- Find the net force, Fnet = F1 + F2 + F3…
- Use Newton’s Second Law, Fnet = m x a to find the acceleration.
- If necessary, use equations to find the velocity or position of the object

**Apparent weight**

-A spring scale measures weight, not mass

-The force exerted by the scale is called the apparent weight

Four elevator scenarios:

-Equilibrium → Fsp = Fg

-Accelerates upward → Fsp = m(g + a) → overweight

-Accelerates downward → Fsp = m(g – a) → lighter

-Free fall → Fsp = 0 → weightlessness

-Weightlessness ≠ zero gravity; means no contact force

## 4.5 Newton’s Third Law of Motion

-Interaction pair

–forces always come in pairs, FA on B =-FB on A

-opposite directions, equal magnitude, different systems and same type

-also called an action-reaction pair of forces

**Newton’s** **Third lLaw**

-Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second exerts an equal force in the opposite direction on the first

-To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction

Action-reaction examples:

- Paddle on water ↔ water on paddle
- Car tire pushes ground backward ↔ ground pushes car forward
- Water sprinkler reaction jets

Compare balanced force and action-reaction:

|  | Balanced-Force | Action-Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Common | Same magnitude, opposite direction | Same magnitude, opposite direction |
| System | Same system (can cancel) | Different system (can’t cancel) |
| Timing | Not necessarily simultaneous | Always simultaneous |
| Force Type | Not necessarily same type | May cause acceleration |
| Effect on Motion | No acceleration | May cause acceleration |

## 4.8 Problems Involving Friction, Inclines

-The maximum acceleration a vehicle can achieve without wheel slippage is limited by static friction:
${a}_{\text{max}}={\mu }_{s}g$.

-When a force is applied at an angle to a horizontal surface:

-Pushing downward increases the normal force: ${F}_{N}=mg+F\sin \theta$, which increases friction and makes motion harder

-Pulling upward decreases the normal force: ${F}_{N}=mg-F\sin \theta$, which reduces friction and makes motion easier

For an object at rest on an inclined plane:

-The coefficient of static friction required to prevent sliding is ${\mu }_{s}=\tan \theta$, where $\theta$is the incline angle

-This value is independent of mass

On an inclined plane with kinetic friction:

-Acceleration down the ramp is given by:

$$a=g(\sin \theta -{\mu }_{k}\cos \theta )$$

-Motion up or down the ramp depends on the direction and magnitude of applied forces, which must overcome both gravity and friction components

For connected objects on inclines (e.g., via a pulley):

-The system’s acceleration depends on the net gravitational component along the inclines minus the total kinetic friction

-Tension in the cord is found by analyzing one object at a time using Newton’s second law

For objects experiencing multiple non-aligned forces (e.g., parachutists):

-Resolve all forces into components

-Net force and acceleration are found using vector addition

In accelerating reference frames (e.g., a car with a pendulum):

-The pendulum angle relates to acceleration by $\theta ={\tan }^{-1}(a/g)$

-At constant velocity (zero acceleration), the pendulum hangs vertically ($\theta ={0}^{∘}$).
