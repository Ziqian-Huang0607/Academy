## 1.4 Measurement and Uncertainty; Significant Figures

-Measurement: comparison between an unknown quantity and a standard

-More significant figures: more precise

-Significant digits: the valid/ reliable digits in a measurement

-Estimated/ uncertain digit: last digit in a measurement

**Rules for Counting Significant Digits**

- All nonzero digits and any zeros contained between nonzero digits count

300042: 6 significant digits

- Exact numbers have an infinite number of significant digits

60 pages: infinite significant digits

- Leading zeros do not count

0.000034: 2 significant digits

- Trailing zeros count if there is a decimal point

0.0002500: 4 significant digits

- Trailing zeros may or may not count if there is no decimal point

190000: 2 significant digits

- In scientific notation, only count the term- the power does not count

1.9* 105 km: 2 significant digits

-Scientific notation: a* 10n, where a is between 1 and 10, and n is an integer

**Calculations with** **significant digits**

-The result never can be more precise than the least- precise measurement

-Use all digits throughout calculations, only round off answers to the appropriate significant digits (calulate the answers first, then round the answer)

-Addition/ subtraction: round the result to have as many decimal places as the measured number with the smallest number of decimal places.

-Multiplication/ division: round the result to have as many significant figures as the measured number with the smallest number of significant figures

**Uncertainty**

-Uncertainty: the variation in measured data

-No measurement made is ever exact

-Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the accepted value

-Precision: the repeatability of the measurements using a given instrument, increases with the number of significant figures

**Error**

-Random errors: statistical fluctuations in either direction in the measured data due to the precision limitations of the measurement device

-Random errors can be reduced by averaging over a large number of observations

-Least count: smallest division that is marked on the instrument

-Instrument Limit of Error (ILE): 1/2 of least count in analogue reading device, least count in digital reading device

-Deviation: repeat measurement several times, find average and then deviation

-Systematic errors: reproducible inaccuracies that are consistently in the same direction. The result of a mis- calibrated device, or a measuring technique which always makes the measured value larger or smaller than the true value

-Example: wrongly calibrated scale; using steel ruler at very low/ high temperature, in low temperature overestimate, in high temperature underestimate; reaction time of experimenter in stopwatches; zero error

-Systematic errors cannot be detected/ reduced by repeating measurements

-For each physics quantity, choose the largest of ILE, deviation, systematic error for uncertainty

-Uncertainty: 1 significant figure, in the same place as measured value’s last significant figure

-(Measured value + uncertainty) unit

-Measured value and uncertainty should be in same power of ten when given in scientific notation

-Absolute error/ uncertainty: size of error and units= x

-Relative/ Fractional Uncertainty: uncertainty/ measured value= x/ x

Keep 1-2 significant figures

-Precision is often reported by using relative/ fractional uncertainty, sometimes absolute value

-Relative error= (measured value- expected value)/ expected value

-Accuracy is often reported by using relative error

-Percentage uncertainty= relative uncertainty* 100%, keep 1-2 significant digits

-Percentage error/ discrepancy= relative error* 100%, keep 1-2 significant digits

**Error vs. Uncertainty**

-Error is the difference between the correct/accepted value, uncertainty is likely range of the true measurement

**Propagation of Error**

-Addition/ Subtraction: absolute uncertainty of the final result= sum of absolute uncertainties

-Formulas: (A A)+ (B B)= (A+B) (A + B)

(A A)- (B B)= (A-B) (A+ B)

-Multiplication/ Division: the percent uncertainty of final result= sum of percent uncertainties

-Formulas: (A A)* (B B)= (A*B) (A/A + B/B)* (A*B)

(A A)/ (B B)= (A/B) ( A/A+ B/B)* （A/B）

-For multiplication by an exact number, multiply the uncertainty by the exact same number

-x= Aya zb

x/ x= |a| (y/y) +|b| (z/z)

-Independent variable: factor changed during the experiment, on x-axis

-Dependent variable: factor that depends on the independent variable, on y-axis

-Line of best fit: draw as close to all data points as possible

## 1.5 Units, Standards, and the SI System

**The SI System**

-SI Base Quantities

Length: meter (m), mass: kilogram (kg), time: second (s), temperature: kelvin (K), amount of a substance: mole (mol), electric current: ampere (A), luminous intensity: candela (cd)

-SI derived units: units defined in terms of these seven base units

Speed: m/s, energy: Joule, electric charge: Coulomb, unified atomic mass

1J= 1kg* m2/s2; 1C= 1A* s, 1u= 1.6605* 10-27 kg

-Metric Prefixes

yotta, zetta, exa, peta, tera, giga, mega, kilo, hecto, deka, deci, centi, milli, micro, nano, pico, femto, atto, zepto, yocto

## 1.6 Converting Units

-Conversion factor: multiplier equal to 1

-Example: 1= 1kg/ 1000g

-to convert units, multiply the original measurement by a suitable conversion factor

## 1.7 Order of Magnitude

**Order of magnitude estimate**

-A rough estimate is made by rounding off all numbers to 1 significant figures and its power of 10. Do the calculation, and again keep 1 significant figure (still in power of 10)

-Your result can be accurate within a factor of 10 and often better

-Order of magnitude: the power of 10
