**Kepler’s First Law: The Law of Ellipses**
Every planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun located at one of the two foci.
Orbits are not perfect circles. The Sun is offset from the geometric center, residing at one focus. The eccentricity quantifies orbital shape: $e=0$yields a circle, while values approaching 1 produce highly elongated ellipses. All bound two-body gravitational systems (moons, exoplanets, binary stars, artificial satellites) follow this law when perturbations are negligible.

**Kepler’** **Second** **Law: The Law of Equal Areas**
A line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time.

**Mathematical Description:**

The areal velocity is constant: This law describes orbital speed variation. A planet travels fastest at perihelion (closest approach to the Sun) and slowest at aphelion (farthest point). The constancy of areal velocity is a direct manifestation of conservation of angular momentum in a central force field. No external torque acts on the planet-Sun system, so the product of tangential velocity and radial distance remains invariant throughout the orbit.

**Kepler’s** **Third Law: The Harmonic Law**

The square of a planet’s orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit.

## 7.2 Using the Law of Universal Gravitation

**The Law of Universal Gravitation: Core Concepts**

\Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation explains how gravity governs the motion of planets, moons, and artificial satellites. The central idea is that gravitational attractions provide the centripetal force required for orbital motion.

**Orbital Motion and Newton's Insight**

Newton imagined a cannon firing projectiles horizontally from a high mountain. At low speeds, projectiles follow parabolic paths and strike the ground. As launch speed increases, the projectile travels farther before falling. At a critical speed, the curvature of the projectile's downward path matches the curvature of Earth's surface: the object continuously falls toward Earth but never reaches it, entering a stable orbit. This demonstrates that orbiting is continuous free-fall, with gravity supplying the necessary centripetal force.

**Speed and period for a Satellite**

A satellite in an orbit that is always the same height above Earth moves in uniform circular motion.

**Geostationary Satellites**
A geostationary satellite remains fixed above one point on Earth. This requires an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation period (one day) and an orbit directly above the equator. Such satellites orbit at a specific altitude where these conditions are satisfied, making them ideal for communications and weather monitoring.

**Weightlessness in Orbit**

Astronauts experience "weightlessness" not because gravity vanishes, but because they and their spacecraft are in free-fall together. Gravitational force still acts on them; their apparent weight is zero because no contact force opposes gravity.

**The Gravitational Field**
Gravity is a field force: any mass creates a gravitational field that exerts force on other masses within it. The field strength $\vec{g}$at a point is defined as force per unit mass (${\vec{F}}_{g}=m\vec{g}$), with units N/kg or m/s². The field points toward the source mass, decreases with distance squared, and depends only on the source mass—not on the object experiencing the field.

**Orbital Speed and Period**
For a satellite in circular orbit, gravitational force equals centripetal force:

$$G\frac{Mm}{{r}^{2}}=m\frac{{v}^{2}}{r}$$

Solving gives orbital speed $v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$and period $T=2\pi \sqrt{\frac{{r}^{3}}{GM}}$. These show that orbital speed decreases with distance, while period increases—satellites farther from Earth move slower but take longer to complete an orbit

**Acceleration Due to Gravity**
At Earth's surface, gravity produces acceleration $g=\frac{G{M}_{E}}{{R}_{E}^{2}}\approx 9.80 {m/s}^{2}$. At greater distances from Earth's center, gravity weakens with the inverse square of distance: $g(r)={g}_{\text{surface}}{\left( \frac{{R}_{E}}{r} \right)}^{2}$. Thus, gravity is weaker at higher altitudes but never truly zero.
