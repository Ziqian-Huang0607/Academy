## 6.2 Circular Motion

**uniform circular motion:** movement of an object or particle trajectory at a constant speed around a circle with a fixed radius

When an obj is doing a UCM it is:

- has centripetal acceleration: pointing towards the center of the circle
- changing direction, so it is accelerating
- velocity tangent to the circle, perpendicular to the radius
- speed (magnitude) stays the same, only direction changes

a centripetal = v2/r = 4π2r / T2

Fcentripetal = m x acentripetal = m x v2/r = m x 4π2r / T2

**Centripetal force** is:

- not a real thing, it’s an already existing force renamed
- causes an obj to change direction (only direction, not speed)

**Question types:**

      - find ac or Fc
- just sub in the formulas
      - horizontal circular motion (car turning, merry-go-round)
- friction provides centripetal force

Fc ≤ √µsgr

      - vertical circle – minimum speed at the top (roller coaster, lasso)
- at minimum speed, Fn = 0 or Ft = 0
- only gravity provides centripetal force

Fc = mg

vmin= √gr

      - vertical circle – tension/ normal force at bottom
- Ft = mg + Fc

## 6.3 Relative Velocity

**Definition**

Relative velocity describes the relationship between motion measurements taken by different observers. In classical mechanics, velocity is not an intrinsic property of an object; rather, it is a relational quantity defined exclusively with respect to a chosen frame of reference. The absence of a universal rest frame means that all velocity measurements inherently answer a specific question: how an object’s position changes over time from the perspective of a particular observer. Changing the observer changes the measured velocity, a principle that serves as a foundational introduction to the broader concept of relativity in physics.

**Reference Frames**

A reference frame consists of a coordinate system attached to an observer, accompanied by a timekeeping mechanism. While frames fixed to the Earth’s surface or a laboratory are conventionally treated as stationary, this designation is purely practical. The Earth itself undergoes rotational and orbital motion, demonstrating that “stationary” is a relative term. Selecting a moving reference frame often simplifies complex motion analysis. For instance, analyzing pedestrian movement inside a train becomes straightforward when the frame is attached to the train car. In fluid dynamics, adopting a frame that moves with a current or wind stream transforms ground-relative trajectories into simpler, stationary-medium problems. In orbital mechanics, shifting between Earth-centered, heliocentric, or spacecraft-centered frames isolates specific dynamical behaviors. Explicit specification of the reference frame is essential, as ambiguity in frame designation is a primary source of analytical error.
