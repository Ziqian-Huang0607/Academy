## 6.1 Driving Forces Within the Atmosphere

- Wind direction is named after the direction from which

wind blows

-   21-point compass rose used for direction
- Arrows that point the direction the wind comes from;

small barbs indicate wind speed (drawn on left hand)

- Wind pressure: weight of pressure per unit area
- Four major forces: Gravity Force, Centrifugal Force,

Pressure Gradient Force, Friction Force, Coriolis Force

1. Gravity Force:

- Directed downwards, perpendicular to the horizon
- Fg=mg

2. Centrifugal Force:

- Caused by Earth’s rotation (to the right)
- Influences the speed and direction of Earth’s wind (curved)
- F=mv2/ r

3. Pressure Gradient Force (PGF):

- creating force
- isobar: isoline connecting points of equal pressure; spacing indicates gradient and wind strength/speed

- wind direction is due to PGF only
- high pressure system creates anti-cyclone (outward)

- low pressure system creates cyclone (inward)

4. Friction Force:

- Occurs when Earth’s surface or objects on it cause resistance to airflow & reduce wind speed
- Opposite force of motion
- Drags on wind as it moves across Earth’s surface
- Decreases with height above surface

5. Coriolis Force:

- Deflecting force, acts only on objects already in motion
- An effect of Earth’s rotation
- Only affects large-scale motions
- Northern Hemisphere: deflects right; Southern Hemisphere: deflects left
- Proportional to sine of angles (equator=0, poles=maximum)

- Higher latitude: lower linear velocity; Lower latitude: higher linear velocity
- F(centrifugal)= mv2/ r, Fg=mg, F=G(m1+m2)/r2

- Geostrophic winds: as wind gains speed, deflection increases until Coriolis = PGF; at this point, winds blow parallel to isobars

## 6.2 Applications of Coriolis Effect

- Global Atmospheric Circulation

- Hadley Cell: convection cell, tropical atmospheric circulation, abundant sunlight causes high temperature & low pressure, warm air rises and diverges to move to higher latitudes, air cools down and descends to 30°N/S
- At latitudes of 30°N/S, stable subtropical high-pressure center, very dry regions, the location of most deserts (e.g.: Sahara, Atacama, Gobi, Rub ‘ al Khali)

- At midlatitudes of 60°N/S, unstable low-pressure system, was the source of most mid-latitude storms
- Polar Cell: high pressure, frigid deserts (Arctic and Antarctic), very dry despite general snow cover, rare & gentle precipitation
- Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ): wet & cloudy, calm wind region, with most warm moist air rising, condensing into clouds to produce heavy rainfall, convergence of trade winds
- Doldrums: located at the ITCZ, a calm, windless region
- Earth’s tilt results in subsolar point changes, causing north/south shift of the ITCZ
- Horse Latitudes: around 30°-35°N/S, with sinking dry air, little rain & sunny region, little to no wind, resulting in deserts

## 6.3 Atmospheric Patterns of Motion

-   3 levels: primary circulation —>general worldwide circulation

secondary circulation —>migratory high-pressure and low-pressure systems tertiary circulation —>local winds and temporal weather patterns

- meridional flows: winds moving N or S along meridians of longitude
- zonal flows: winds moving E or W along parallels of latitude
- warmer, less-dense air along the equator rises —>creates low pressure at the surface
- colder, more-dense air at poles sinks —>creates high pressure at the surface
- primary winds flows between the high- and low-pressure areas of primary circulation
- econdary circulation forms within primary pressure areas, seasonally migrate to produce changing weather patterns
-   4 primary pressure areas cover each hemisphere: 2 areas stimulated by thermal factors: equatorial low —>around the ITCZ, warm air associated with low pressure, polar highs —>at N and S poles, cold air associated with high pressure; 2 areas formed by dynamic factors: subtropical highs (H), subtropical lows (L)
- equatorial lows form ITCZ
- monsoons: seasonally shifting wind systems
- The Asian Monsoon Pattern: driven by the unequal heating between the Asian landmass and the Indian Ocean, influenced by the shifting migration of ITCZ

- in summer, ITCZ moves northward, warm belt winds move northward —>lands become warmer, wind blows towards lands, clockwise

- Human influences on Asian Monsoon: air pollution, the warming of atmospheric and ocean temperatures
- Drivers of the Monsoon Systems: solar heating of the land during the spring season that helps to establish a land-sea temperature difference, the N/S oscillation of the ITCZ, El Niño and La Niña

## 6.4 Ocean Currents

- driving force is the frictional drag of the winds
- ther factors: Coriolis force, density differences caused by temperature and salinity, configuration of continents and ocean floor, astronomical forces that cause tides
- gyres: the oceanic circulation systems, generally offset toward the western side of ocean basin
- surface currents moves clockwise about high-pressure cells in N hemisphere, moves counter-clockwise in S hemisphere
- Equatorial currents: trade winds drive the ocean surface waters westward along the equator, remain near the equator due to weakness of Coriolis force (0 at equator)
- western intensification: water piled up against eastern shores of continents (avg. h=15 cm)

- upwelling current: where surface water is swept away from coast, either by surface divergence or offshore winds
- downwelling current: deep currents that flow vertically and along the ocean floor, travel the full extent of ocean basins, redistribute heat energy and salinity over the globe
- thermohaline circulation (THC): density differences produced by differences in temp. and salinity
- surface circulation: winds driven, parallel to surface of ocean (horizontal)
- deep circulation: driven by differences in density caused by differences in temp. or salinity, perpendicular to surface (vertical)

## 6.5 Natural Oscillations in Global Circulation

- El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO): forces the greatest interannual variability of temp. and precipitation on a global scale

- cold Peru Current (the Humboldt Current) flows northward off South America’s west coast, joining the westward movement of the South Equatorial Current near the equator
- La Niña: when surface waters in the central and eastern Pacific cool to below normal temperature
- No correlation in strength or weakness between the 2 phases

- Global Effects Related to ENSO: El Niño —>droughts, La Niña —>wetter conditions (e.g. hurricanes)
- Both occur every 2-7 years; El Niño lasts for 9-12 months (up to 18), La Niño lasts for 1-3 years
