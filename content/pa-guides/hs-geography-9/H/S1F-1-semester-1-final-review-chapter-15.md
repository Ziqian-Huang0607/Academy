## Chapter 15: River Systems

— Hydrology: Science of water and its global circulation, distribution, and properties.

  - Focuses on water at and below earth surface.

— Fluvial: Processes related to streams and rivers.

— River: Refers to mainstream of river system.

— Stream: General term for water flowing in channel. Unrelated to size.

— Discharge: Streamflow volume past a point during a given period of time.

— Rivers:

  - Shape landscape by removing weathering, mass movement, and erosion products.
  - Provide water supplies for agriculture, industry, etc.
  - Receive, dilute, and transport wastes.
  - Provide form of transportation.

— Gradient: slope of the river (rise over run)

  - rise = vertical change, height (can be read directly from graph)
  - run = horizontal change, distance (use ruler to measure length then use scale to convert units)

**Drainage Basins and Drainage Patterns**

— Drainage Basins = Watersheds = Portions of landscape where streams receive water.

  - This is where all the precipitation drains down into a river. Think of it like a bit like a funnel that catches all the water and drains it into a single hole.
  - Small drainage basins gather their runoff and sediment into local streams that merge together to form bigger streams with a larger volume.

**Drainage Divides:**

— Overland flow takes place when the “funnel” takes water down into the hole. It is when the water moves downslope to the stream. It takes two forms:

  - Sheetflow: A thin film spread over ground surface. Directed by interfluve.
  - Interfluve: High ground that separates two valleys and directs sheetflow.
  - Rills: Smaller grooves in landscape that is formed by downslope movement of water. May turn into gullies and then stream channels.
    - Act as drainage divides that define catchment (water receiving) area of drainage basin.
    - Drainage Divides: Determine which drainage basin water flows into.
    - Continental Divides: Seperate drainage basins that empty into different bodies of water surrounding a continent.

**Drainage Basins as Open Systems:**

— Input: Precipitation, minerals, and rocks.

— Output: Water, sediment is dispersed to lakes, larger streams, or seas and oceans.

— Redistribution of energy and matter occurs when stream adjusts landscape.

— Change in any part of drainage basin affects entire system.

**International Drainage Basins:**

— Danube River: Polluted river with economic value.

**Internal Drainage:**

— This is when stream drainage doesn’t reach the ocean and leaves the drainage basin through evaporation or subsurface ground flow instead.

— Eg. Humboldt River, Dead Sea, Aral Sea, Caspian Sea, Utah’s Great Salt Lake, etc.

**Drainage Patterns:**

— Drainage Density: Total length of all stream channels in basin divided by area of basin.

  - Primary feature that reflects the landscape’s regional geology and topography.

— Drainage Pattern: Arrangement of channels in an area. Determined by:

  - Regional steepness and relief
  - Rock resistance, climate, and hydrology
  - Structure of rocks underneath

— Dendritic Drainage: Tree-like pattern. Joints are insignificant. Erosion can happen in all directions.

  - Carries around water and sediment most efficiently because length is minimized.

— Trellis Drainage: Main streams are parallel to each other between ridges.

  - Formed by folded rock that vary in resistance towards erosion.
  - Tributaries are perpendicular to the main stream and are short.

— Radial Drainage: When streams flow off a central peak of a dome and flow off in all directions. Eg. Volcanic mountain

— Parallel Drainage: When rivers run side-by-side in the same elevation.

* Slope is steep but uniform

— Angular Drainage: Stream that flows in rocky valley and has very irregular slope.

— Rectangular Drainage: Stream cuts along faults or joints to form tributaries

* Grid Shape

* Main stream and tributaries always bends at right angles

— Annular Drainage: Streams flow down an elevated dome region into river surrounding it. The main stream is circular.

— Deranged Drainage: No clear geometry or true stream valley.

— Due to the evolvement of landscape and erosion of streamflow, sometimes drainage patterns may seem discordant to their surrounding landform.

  - Superposed Stream: Flows over newer layer of strata and then erodes down to flow in the path of the old, underlying strata.

— Centrifugal: water flows from center outwards (radial drainage pattern)

— Centripetal: water flows towards middle (flow into a lake)

**Basic Fluvial Concepts**

**Gradient**

— Drop in elevation per unit distance (m/km, ft/mi).

— Slope of the river (rise over run)

  - rise = vertical change, height (can be read directly from graph)
  - run = horizontal change, distance (use ruler to measure length then use scale to convert units)

— Exact same thing as channel slope.

— In a river, headwaters have a steeper slope, and downstream has a more gradual slope.

— Gradient affects velocity of the flow of material.

**Base Level**

— The lowest level that a stream can erode its valley.

— Ultimate Base Level: Sea Level.

— Local/Temporary Base Level: The lower limit of a regional stream erosion.

  - Rivers and lakes are natural local base levels.
  - Reservoirs are human-caused local base levels.
  - In a case of internal drainage, valleys and other low points are local base levels.

**Stream Discharge**

— Potential energy becomes kinetic energy as water from base levels above flow down.

  - Rate of conversion decides ability of stream to do geomorphic work.
  - Discharge: Stream’s volume of flow per unit of time.
  - Q = wdv, Q = discharge, w = channel width, d = channel depth, v = stream velocity.
  - Unit: Cubic meters/feet per second.

**Changes in Discharge with Distance Downstream**

— In humid regions, discharge usually increases in a downstream direction.

  - Potentially different if stream originates in humid region but flows through arid regions. High POTET rates in arid regions cause discharge to decrease further downstream.
  - As discharge increases, velocity also increases. This is because velocity decreases due to the increase of friction, which is higher in mountains streams full of rocks and boulders.
  - Turbulent flow: Stream energy is expended. Forms where the contact with stream bed and banks are high or where the channel is rough.
  - Wide, lowland rivers with little friction have higher velocity and more energy to move sediment.

**Changes in Discharge Over Time**

— Perennial Streams: Flows all year. Fed by melting snow, rainfall, groundwater, etc.

— Ephemeral Streams: Only flow after precipitation events. Not connected to groundwater.

— Intermittent Streams: Flows for a period of time every year. May have some connection to groundwater.

— Hydrograph: Graph of stream discharge over time.

* A steep slope has shorter lag time since water has less time to infiltrate into the soil, and surface runoff would become higher.

* Afforestation (bare land or forested): no vegetation no interception = low infiltration

* Rock/Soil Type (impermeable or permeable): impermeable = shorter lag time

* Land use (urbanization): high surface runoff

— Annual Hydrograph: Hydrograph that shows discharge of entire year.

— Storm Hydrograph: Reflects changes in discharge caused by precipitation that led to floods. Overland flow + throughflow = storm flow.

— Base flow: Low discharge maintained by local groundwater during dry seasons.

— Peak flow: High discharge that happens during precipitation.

  - Affected by nature of watershed surface.

**Measuring Discharge:**

— Done through measuring velocity of different subsections and combining results with width and depth to compute subsection discharge, which is added together and totaled.

**Fluvial Processes and Landforms**

— Erosion: Process of water dislodging, dissolving, or removing weathered material.

— Deposition: Laying down of materials relocated through weathering.

— Running water is a very significant piece of erosion.

**Stream Channel Processes:**

— Hydrolic Action: Erosion caused by only water done through loosening and lifting rocks.

  - Maximum erosion is in upstream or middle section due to larger sediments and turbulent flow.
  - Abrasion: When erosion happens downstream, the movement of the sediment mechanically erodes the stream bed.
  - Attrition: Rocks and pebbles bang against each other, wearing them down to become small, rounded pebbles.
  - Corrosion: Chemicals erodes away rock in slightly acidic water near factories or etc.

— Fluvial processes as such cause erosion downward, laterally, and lengthen.

  - Channel incision: Deepening of channel.

— Headward Erosion: Lengthening of streams upstream.

  - Occurs when flow entering a main channel is powerful enough to downcut.
  - Occurs when groundwater seeps out at the head of the channel and weakens channel’s upstream endpoint.
  - Stream piracy: Eroding flow of one river breaks through drainage divide and becomes a part of another stream. Result of headward erosion.

— Fluvial erosion at source is V-shaped incision. Erosion at middle or lower course is lateral and U-shaped.

— Thal-weg: Connects lowest points in valley and highest flow velocity.

**Sediment Load:**

— Sediment transport: Sediment flows downstream when there is energy and sediment.

— Sediment Load: Material carried by stream.

  - Supply decided by topographic relief, nature of rock and soil, climate, vegetation, and human activity.
  - More discharge, more sediment.

— Three types of sediment load are moved through solution, suspension, saltation, and traction:

  - Dissolved load: Dissolved chemical compounds that move as solutions.
  - Suspended load: Fine pieces of rock that are not deposited until there is no movement. Affected by turbulence.
  - Bed load: Course material moved by traction (rolling or dragging) or saltation (bouncing and jumping).
    - Saltation: Too large to be suspended but isn’t restricted to bouncing and hopping.
    - Traction: Dragged, pushed, rolled along streambed.
    - Under high stream velocity, bed load is swept up to be a part of suspended load.

— Competence: Stream’s ability to move particles, stream velocity, and energy available for moving materials.

* Flood flows bring stream energy that makes stream competence high, causing sediment transport. This makes the channel erode, which is known as degradation. Aggradation is the opposite process, where the slowing of sediment flow causes building up of deposition.

— Capacity: Maximum sediment that can be transported. Function of discharge.

**Channel Patterns:**

— Multiple thread occurs with abundant sediment or lower reaches of large river systems.

— Single thread channels are either straight or meandering.

  - Straight: Headwater zones with high gradient.
  - Meandering: Zones with fine sediment and low gradient.

— Sinuosity is the ratio between the distance between two points measured along a stream as it curves and the shortest, straight-line distance between the same two points.

* =AB/CD where AB is length of actual river and CD is displacement of river.

* If the number is equal to one, then the river is straight. If the number is from 1 to 1.5 then the river is sinuos. If then number is greater than 1.5, then the river is meandering.

**Multiple Thread Channels:**

— Braided stream: Cause by reduced discharge, commonly found in glacial environments and wide, shallow channels with changing discharge.

— Anabranching/Anastomosing Channel Pattern: Multiple large channels spread across a floodplain.

* “ana” means again and “stomosis” means mouth/opening

* Avulsion: usually occurs at the lowest reaches of large river systems, they form diversions to form new channels

* formed by aggradation and flood

* Avulsions may rejoin main channel downstream.

**Single Thread Channels:**

— Straight channels are usually found in mountainous areas or bedrock controlled channels.

  - Hight gradient, low sinuosity.
  - Sinuosity: Ratio between curved distance of river and shortest distance of river.
  - Velocity is highest at surface and centre, and decreases at the sides and bottom due to a frictional drag on water flow.

— Meandering channels form due to gradual slope and is a sign of propensity to find a path of least effort.

  - Velocity is highest on outer side of curve, and shifts to centre when the curve straightens.
  - Outer side = fast water velocity = greatest erosive action (scouring) = undercut bank.
  - Inner side = Slow water velocity = aggradation = point bar.
  - Point bar: Accumulation of sediment on inside of meander bend.
  - Leads to lateral erosion = causes meander scars left from previous river channel residual deposits.
  - If meandering streams erode into each other, they form a cutoff, which makes the stream straighter.
  - Oxbow lake: Part that is cut off and isolated from rest of river. Either gets filled up with sediment or floods back into the original river
  - Includes finer sediments

— Braided channels have multiple threads , frequent lateral shifts, and no levees.

* Mobile bars are covered during high flow, and emerge during low flow.

* Bars are poorly vegetated, and are formed by deposition.

* Channels form by a sudden decrease in gradient, for example in alluvial fans and river deltas, and where channel depth and velocity dramatically decrease.

* Has high bedload of sand or gravel.

* Meandering streams can sometimes become locally braided in reaction to a sudden influx of sediment from tributaries.

**Graded Streams:**

— Longitudinal profile: Changes in river gradient from headwater to mouth.

  - River naturally has steeper slope at headwaters, so the gradient is usually concave.

— Natural systems lean towards equilibrium, so the channel is adjusted so that the flow can move the supplied sediment.

— Graded stream: Where stream velocity is just enough to transport sediment load due to adjustments of channel slope.

  - Any variation will be smoothed out over time.
  - Dynamic equilibrium between gradient and sediment load.

— Discordant drainage pattern: follows initial path irrespective of changes in topography

— Concordant drainage patter: dependent on slope and topography

**Tectonic Uplift:**

— Tectonic uplift may change the stream’s elevation in relation to its base level, increasing the stream gradient and causing erosion.

— Entrenched meanders: Are deeply incised into landscape due to degradation of channel.

— Antecedent stream: Downcuts at the same rate as upliftings.

**Nickpoints:**

— Point of interruption where gradient changes abruptly. Relatively temporary.

— Caused by stream flowing through resistant rocks, faults, or surface deformation.

— In a waterfall, the potential energy falls and becomes kinetic energy, smoothing out the gradient.

— High velocity of falling water gradually undercuts waterfall, leading to collapse, so the nick point migrates upstream and is gradually eliminated.

— Cuesta: Ridge with steep slope on one side.

Depositional Landforms:

— Alluvium: General term for unconsolidated materials deposited by running waters.

— Fluvial deposition: Creation of depositional landforms due to deposition of alluvium.

Floodplains:

— Floodplain: Flat, low area adjacent to channel and subject to recurrent flooding.

  - Flooded and receives alluvial deposits that cover underlying rock.
  - Natural leeves: Low ridges of coarse sediment.
  - Discharge increases > river is flooded > loses stream competence and therefore capacity > drops coarse part of sediment load, building foundation for leeves > drops fine part of sediment.
  - If a lot of floods happens the leeves grow in height until the river channel is perched above the floodplain.

— Riparian Marshes: Wetlands formed in poorly drained fine sediment deposited by over back flow.

— Yazoo Streams/tributaries: Parallel to main river but are blocked from each other by natural leeves.

— Bar-and-swale topography: Landscape of alternating high and low ground.

Stream Terraces:

— Alluvial Terraces: Topographic steps above river that are caused by entrenchment of river into its own floodplain.

  - Are paired at similar elevations on sides of valley.
  - More than one pair of terraces = more than one episode of rejuvenation.
  - Represent a floodplain eroded by a stream that is down cutting and has experienced change in gradient.
  - Not matching in elevation: Entrenchments were continuous.

Alluvial Fans: Cone/fan shaped deposits of fluvial sediments in arid/semi arid climates.

  - Occur at mouth of canyon where an ephemeral stream channel exits the mountains to a flat valley.
  - Caused when flowing water loses velocity and drops sediment on mountain bases. When water flows over the base again, a braided drainage pattern is formed.
  - Bajada: A gentle slope formed by the merging of many alluvial fans.
  - Coarse materials are inside, with finer materials flowing to the valley floor.
    - When water evaporates, salt may be left in the desert to form a playa, which is a dry lakebed. This may become an ephemeral lake if there is water.
  - Alluvial fans can be a source of groundwater.
