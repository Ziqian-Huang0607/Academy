**HOW CAN YOU DETERMINE YOUR LOCATION**

Essentially, there are three ways to find our locations: by calculating time difference and thereby finding the longitude, by finding the angle between the horizon and Polaris, and by finding the solar declination and thus calculating the solar height and the latitude.

**Time and** **Longitude**

All time will be relative to GMT (Greenwich Mean Time), which would be 0 degrees latitude, and from then on, each hour of time difference would be 15 degrees east or west. Remember!!! If comparing two points, the more eastern point’s time is always ahead of the western point (for example, China is east of India, and so Beijing is around 2.5 hours ahead of New Delhi. When it is 9am in Beijing, it’s 6:30am in New Delhi).

IDL – International Date Line:180 degrees east or west. (if you cross the IDL from west to east, you gain one day; if you cross it from E to W, you lose one day)

**Polaris**

By drawing a tangent as your horizon at your location on Earth’s ellipsoid, the angle between your horizon and the north pole will be your latitude at the north pole, you will have to look up to see the star, and it will be 90 degrees. At the equator, the star is on your horizon, so it will be 0.

*ONLY IN THE NORTHERN HEMISPHERE

**Solar Declination and Solar Height**

Solar Declination: Latitude on Earth where the Sun is directly overhead (subsolar point) on a given day.

Sub Solar point: the location on Earth where the sun is directly above your head (no shadow at all), can only be between 23.5N and 23.5S

Solar Height: the angle between the horizon and the sun

Analemma: the figure eight that displays the solar declination on different dates

**Equation**: if you and the subsolar point are in the same hemisphere, **Latitude=(90-H)+Declination**; if you are in opposite hemispheres, **Latitude=(90-H)-Declination** (H= Solar Height)

HOW DO I GRAPH THIS: 1. Find your latitude based on your calculation results from formulas 2. Use the latitude you calculated to graph your position on Earth 3. Draw a line from the center of the Earth to your location and extend the line 4. Find the subsolar point based on the given date and the analemma, draw a point for it, draw a sunray by connecting the Earth’s center to this subsolar point 5. Draw the second sunshine ray on your location, make sure its parallel to the first ray 6. Label the solar height angle, the declination angle, the latitude angle

**Hydrosphere**

**Origins of Water** **&** **Distribution of Water**

The most common beliefs of how water came onto Earth’s surface is that **comets** with ice dust tails collided with Earth, that there are water sources in the mantle and during volcanic eruptions, the gasses become water vapor (supported by the finding of a water gem in Brazil) – also known as **outgassing**, and that there are reserves of water under **ocean crusts.**

**Eustasy:** refers to the change of global sea levels because of the change of volume in the oceans (for ex. If glaciers melt, then sea levels increase; during ice age, everything became ice and global sea level dropped)

Most water on Earth today are stored in Oceans (Pacific>Atlantic>Indian>Arctic), most freshwater is locked in glaciers (surface) and groundwater (subsurface), while very little water resides in lakes and rivers (less than 1%).

The seven greatest lakes on Earth (Lake Baikal, The Great Lakes, Lake Tanganyika) store 50% of all water from lakes

When it rains a lot, a city usually faces more runoff because it has less exposed soil for water to infiltrate

**Transpiration**: Water released by leaves through stomata

**Infiltration**: When precipitation goes in the soil

**Canopy Interception**: When precipitation is temporarily caught and stored by leaves

**Percolation**: When water infiltrates the soil and flows downwards into the saturated zone (usually because of gravity or big pores)

**Drippage**: When water drops form plants to the soil

**Stem Flow**: When water flows via the stem into the soil

**Throughfall**: When water falls into the soil between plants

**Throughflow**: When water infiltrates the soil but instead of percolating, it flows horizontally in the unsaturated zone (its easier to flow in a saturated zone than in an unsaturated zone)

**Runoff:** When water doesn’t enter the soil either because no ground is exposed or because all soil is saturated and flows via the slope into another source of water

**Base Flow**: a portion of a stream’s flow that comes from the seepage of groundwater (when precip infiltrates but flows into streams)

**Zone of saturation**: the groundwater zone; all pores are filled with water. The water table separates the saturated zone from the unsaturated zone

The **soil-moisture zone:** place underground that contains the volume of sub-surface water stored in the soil that is accessible to plant roots.

**Groundwater (Aquifers &Wells)**

How to identify the different wells? The water surface of an ordinary well is the water table of the unconfined aquifer. Since it’s unconfined, there’s not a lot of pressure build up and so the water in the well does not rush up. The water surface of an artesian well is the potentiometric surface (or the water table of the confined aquifer), since artesian well drills in the confined aquifer, the pressure build up causes the water in the well to rise a lot as well. A gushing artesian well is shorter than a normal artesian well, and so the water gushes out at the ground surface. For a perched ordinary well, the water surface of the water within the well is also the water table of the local source (the water table of the perched aquifer).

**Label** **in the above graph:** gushing artesian well, artesian well, perched artesian well, ordinary well, impermeable layer, confined aquifer, unconfined aquifer, recharge area, water table, potentiometric surface, unsaturated zone (zone of aeration)

During dry season, the water table decreases because less precipitation = less infiltration

Over withdrawal water table goes down, when too much groundwater is extracted, the soil can crumble causing the ground surface to be uneven

Tectonic movements fractures and faults can create new pathways for water to move, causing not enough water to be in the unconfined aquifers or causing an increase of groundwater flow

**Geyser vs Artesian Well**: artesian well produces a steady flow of water from a confined aquifer, whereas a geyser erupts intermittently with hot water and steam due to magma heating.

**Dry Wells** can occur if well isn’t drilled deep enough or if too much water is extracted from an unconfined aquifer, causing the water table to decrease

**Springs** form where the perched water table intersects with the surface

**Types of Groundwater**

**Gravitational Water**: when water enters large pores that can’t hold them, and so the water flows down with gravity. Not available to plants because of how fast they move downwards

**Capillary Water**: When water enters small pores, but when they drop down, they become gravitational water. The only type of water available to plants.

**Hygroscopic Water**: when water strongly adheres to the soil particles and so cannot be extracted.

**Field Capacity**: The largest amount of water small pores can hold; when capacity is reached, water flows down because of pressure or becomes surface runoff.

**Wilting Point**: When all capillary water from the unsaturated zone is consumed and only hygroscopic water is left. As plants cannot absorb hygroscopic water, they will die (also the reason people invented irrigation).

Out of all soil types, clay is the densest and contains the most hygroscopic water, while sand has big pore size and so contains the most gravitational water**. Loam**, a combination of all, is the best soil type for planting because its pore size is perfect for capillary water.

**Base Flow of a River**

**Base flow** is part of stream flow (water that first infiltrates then later end up in a stream), is completely composed of groundwater, and occurs when a stream intersects with the water table, also called drought flow (because even during a drought when there is no rain, the base flow is what keeps the channel flowing)

**Influent stream**: During a dry climate, when the water table of groundwater is lower than that of river; when it suddenly rains, it is easier for water to enter the stream through direct rainfall than it is to infiltrate and percolate all the way to the saturated zone in the soil, and so when the river has more water, the river water flows into groundwater (happens only during storms when water table of river increases suddenly) results in an ephemeral stream (when the river is almost dry all the time, unless it suddenly storms) or an intermittent stream (in most months its dry, but there are a few months with little water)/ intermittent Nile; ephemeral river in Death Valley

**Effluent stream**: During a humid climate, when water table in groundwater is higher than in river channel because evaporation happens quicker in rivers than in soil, and so the water from the soil flows into the river results in a perennial stream (flowing all year long and discharge is very high) ex. Amazon, Mississippi

**Alluvial Fan vs Ephemeral Streams**: An alluvial fan forms when an ephemeral river suddenly slows as it leaves a mountain (as slope decreases), depositing sediment in a fan-shaped pattern on the plain (as the water loses its kinetic energy). Ex. Death Valley in CA

**Water Budget**

**A water budget** is a way of describing how water enters, moves through, and leaves a particular system — like a watershed, lake, or even the entire Earth.

**P = (POTET−** **Deficit) + Surplus ± Storage**

**Deficit**: POTET>ACTET: when the need of moisture is greater than the amount of rainfall and there is not enough soil moisture left in storage (for ex. When evapotranspiration rates are very very high or in a desert)

**Soil Storage Usage**: PRECIP<ACTET: The demand is higher than the amount of rainfall, but moisture storage from surpluses is available.

**Soil Recharge:** PRECIP>POTET: Abundant rainfall and the demand is already met, and so the zone of aeration starts to fill up and store water

**Surplus:** PRECIP>POTET: Since zone of aeration is also saturated, the precipitation and extra moisture become surface runoff

In humid subtropical regions (ex. Kingsport): recharge and surplus occur in the winter, abundant rainfall throughout the year, but still deficit period in the summer because of how hot it is and how the evaporation rates increase too fast

Mediterranean regions (ex. California): recharge and surplus in the winter, but in the summer the precipitation rates become minimal, and it becomes very dry and large deficit

Arid regions (ex. Arizona): Minimal rainfall throughout the whole year, very very large deficit in the summer
