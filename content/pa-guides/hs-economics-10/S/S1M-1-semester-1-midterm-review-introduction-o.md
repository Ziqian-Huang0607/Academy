**Scarcity, Choice & Opportunity Cost**

**Key Definitions**

- Economics: Study of how **limited** resources are allocated to satisfy **unlimited** human wants and needs.
- Business: How organizations make resource, operation, marketing & personnel decisions to create value.
- Finance: How people/institutions manage money: saving, borrowing, investing & risktaking.
- Scarcity: Core economic problem: finite resources vs unlimited wants. Scarcity forces people/firms/governments to make choices.
- Opportunity Cost: The nextbest alternative you give up when making a choice.

**Economics as a Social Science**

-Economics is social science: studies human behavior & society; uses scientific method (observehypothesistestconclude).

-Difference vs natural science: human behavior is unpredictable; many questions involve value judgements, no absolute “correct” answer.

**Realworld example: Global Wheat Market**

- Scarcity: Limited farmable land; climate change reduces usable land; population growth raises demand for wheat.
- Choices: Farmers choose wheat vs corn/soybeans; governments choose domestic subsidies vs wheat imports.
- Opportunity cost: More wheat means less other crops on same land; money for wheat subsidies cannot be spent on healthcare or government policies

**How economists approach the world**

|  | **Microeconomics** | **Macroeconomics** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Individual households, firms, single markets | Whole economy, aggregate phenomena |
| Examples | Wheat price, smartphone demand, car production cost | National unemployment, GDP growth, inflation |

**Positive vs Normative Statements**

**-Positive (P)**: Describes “what is”. Can be proven true/false by realworld evidence (facts).

**-Normative (N)**: Describes “what should be”. Contains value judgements; cannot be tested by data; often uses words “should / ought / need”

**Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)**

PPC: Economic model (graph) showing maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce given fixed resources & technology。

**Points on the PPC diagram**

- **Points ON the PPC**: Efficient production. All resources are fully used; tradeoffs exist.
- **Points INSIDE the PPC**: Possible but **inefficient**. Resources are unused or unemployed.
- **Points OUTSIDE the PPC**: **Unattainable** with current resources and technology.

**PPC & Opportunity Cost**

- Movement **along the PPC**: Reallocate resources between two goods → there is always opportunity cost.
- **Slope of PPC = opportunity cost** of one good in terms of the other.

**Two shapes of PPC**

- **Straightline PPC**: Constant opportunity cost. Resources are perfectly interchangeable between goods.
- **Bowshaped (curved outwards) PPC**: **Increasing opportunity cost**. As you produce more of one good, you use resources less suited for it; each extra unit costs more of the other good.

**Shifting the PPC (Economic Growth)**

- **Outward shift of whole PPC (Potential Growth)**: Causes: more resources; better technology; improved labor skills. Economy can produce more of both goods.

Example: better fertilizers for wheat → PPC shifts outward.

- Movement **from inside point onto PPC (Actual Growth)**: Cause: Put unemployed/idle resources back to work. The maximum potential output itself does NOT grow.
- **Inward PPC shift**: Loss/destruction of resources (drought, war destroying farmland for wheat).

**Demand & Supply**

**Markets: Product vs Factor Markets**

- **Market**: Any arrangement for buyers & sellers to complete economic transactions.
- **Product market**: Trade finished goods/services (wheat market, phone market). Buyers=households; Sellers=firms.
- **Factor market**: Trade factors of production (labor, capital). Buyers=firms; Sellers=households.

**Demand**

- **Quantity demanded (Qd)**: Quantity buyers are willing AND able to buy at a price.
- **Law of Demand (ceteris paribus)**: As price ↑ → Qd ↓; price ↓ → Qd ↑. Inverse relationship → demand curve slopes **downward**.

Market demand = horizontal sum of all individual consumers’ demand curves.

**Movementalong vs Shift of demand curve**

- Movement along demand curve is caused by **only change in the good’s own price** - Shift of demand curve is caused by nonprice demand determinants

**Demand shifters (nonprice factors)**

- Income
  - Normal good: income ↑ → demand shifts **right**
  - Inferior good: income ↑ → demand shifts **left** (cheap bus tickets, lowquality clothing)
- Prices of related goods
  - Substitutes: Price of good A ↑ → demand for good B ↑ (apples & oranges; coffee & bubble tea)
  - Complements: Price of good A ↑ → demand for good B ↓ (skates & hockey sticks; coffee & pastries)
- Consumer tastes/preferences
- Expectations (expected future income; expected future prices)
- Number of buyers in market

**Supply**

- **Quantity supplied (Qs)**: Quantity sellers are willing AND able to sell at given price.
- **Law of Supply**: Price ↑ → Qs ↑; price ↓ → Qs ↓. Direct relationship → supply curve slopes **upward**.

Market supply = horizontal sum of all individual sellers’ supply curves.

**Movementalong vs Shift of demand curve**

- Movement along supply curve is caused by **only change in the good’s own price** - Shift of supply curve is caused by nonprice demand determinants

**Supply shifters (nonprice factors)**

- Input / resource costs (wages, raw materials): input cost down → supply shifts right
- Technology improvements → shift right
- Sellers’ price expectations (expect higher future price: hold stock back → supply today shifts left)
- Number of sellers in market
- Government policies
  - Tax on producers: raises cost → supply shifts **left**
  - Subsidy for producers: lowers cost → supply shifts **right**

**Market Equilibrium, Surplus & Shortage**

- **Equilibrium**: Where Qs = Qd. Intersection point of S & D curves.
- At equilibrium: **no surplus, no shortage**, market price stable.
- **Surplus (Excess supply)**: Qs > Qd (market price higher than equilibrium). Sellers cut prices; price falls until equilibrium restored.
- **Shortage (Excess demand)**: Qd > Qs (market price lower than equilibrium). Sellers raise prices; price rises back to equilibrium.

**Welfare Economics**

**Core Definitions**

- **Willingness To Pay (WTP)**：Maximum price a buyer is ready to pay for a good. Height of demand curve = WTP of marginal buyer (the buyer who exits market if price rises higher).
- **Cost (for sellers)**：Opportunity cost of production; height of supply curve = cost of marginal seller (seller exits market if price falls lower).

**Consumer Surplus (CS)**

- Formula: CS = WTP - P
- Diagram: **Area underneath demand curve, above market price**, from quantity=0 up to traded Q
- Meaning: Net benefit / gain for buyers from participating in market.

When price falls: CS rises; two sources of gain:

- Existing buyers pay less for units they already bought
- New buyers enter the market and make purchases

**Producer Surplus (PS)**

- Formula: PS = P - production cost
- Diagram: **Area above supply curve, below market price**, from quantity=0 up to traded Q
- Meaning: Net benefit / gain for sellers from participating in market.

When price rises: PS rises; two sources of gain:

- Existing sellers get higher price for existing output
- New sellers enter market and sell goods

**Total Surplus & Allocative Efficiency**

- Total Surplus = CS + PS
- Total surplus = total gains from trade for whole society.
- **Allocative Efficiency**: At freemarket equilibrium (S=D), total surplus is **maximized**.
- Goods go to buyers who value them most highly.
- Goods are produced by sellers with lowest production cost.

**Elasticity**

Elasticity: Numerical measure of **responsiveness** of quantity to change in price.

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED): How responsive quantity demanded Qd is to price change.

PED= (%change in Qd) / (%change in price); ignore negative sign, since law of demand makes P & Qd move opposite directions

**Two Calculation Methods**

- **Point method**: uses original value as base.

-Limitation: answer changes depending whether you move from point A→B or B→A.

- **Midpoint Method**：uses average/midpoint of two points as base; same result regardless direction.
- %change in Qd= [(Q2-Q1) ÷ (Q1+Q2)/2] x 100%
- %change in P= [(P2-P1) ÷ (P1+P2)/2] x 100%

| **Value** | **Classification** | **Curve shape** |
|---|---|---|
| **PED>1** | **Elastic** | **Flatter demand curve;** **Qd** **changes more than price** |
| **0<PED<1** | **Inelastic** | **Steeper demand curve;** **Qd** **changes less than price** |

**Determinants making demand MORE elastic**

- Many close substitutes available
- Good is luxury (not necessity)
- Narrow market definition
- Longer time horizon for consumers to adjust

**Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)**: How responsive quantity supplied Qs is to price change.

PES= (%change in Qs) / (%change in price)

| **Value** | **Classification** | **Curve shape** |
|---|---|---|
| **PES>1** | **Elastic** | **Flatter supply curve; Qs responds strongly to price** |
| **0<PES<1** | **Inelastic** | **Steeper supply curve; Qs responds weakly to price** |

**Determinants of PES**

- Supply is more elastic in longrun: producers can expand capacity, hire more workers, convert land.
- Supply tends to get less elastic as output approaches full capacity limits.

**Wheat example:**

- Shortrun PES inelastic: crops are already planted; farmers cannot quickly raise output.
- Longrun PES more elastic: farmers can plant more wheat, convert new farmland.

**Government policies**

**Guiding question:** **When markets are unable to satisfy important economic objectives, to what extent does government intervention help?**

- Price ceiling: helps buyers who can still buy; creates a shortage and deadweight loss; problems grow in the long run
- Price floor: helps sellers who can still sell; creates a surplus and deadweight loss that someone must pay for
- Indirect tax: raises revenue and reduces consumption; creates deadweight loss unless it corrects an externality
- Corrective tax, regulation, advertising: can move a failing market toward the social optimum, if the government can measure the externality
- **Perunit Subsidy**

**Subsidy:** a payment from the government to an individual or firm for the purpose of increasing the purchase or supply of a good

**Reasons governments give agricultural subsidies**

1. Raise producer income for volatile farming sectors

2. Lower consumer prices for essential food necessities

3. Boost domestic production volume

4. Make domestic goods cheaper for export

Key Prices & Quantity after subsidy:

- Pb (buyer / consumer price): price consumers pay → lower than original equilibrium price
- Ps (seller / producer price): price sellers receive Ps=Pb + subsidy → higher than original
- equilibrium price
- Qt: new traded quantity, larger than original equilibrium \(Q_1\)
- Vertical gap between original supply and shifted supply = perunit subsidy value.

**Impacts on every stakeholder:**

- Consumers: Better off
- Pay lower price, consume higher quantity.
- Consumer surplus (CS) increases.
- Producers (farmers): Better off
- Receive higher effective price, sell more output.
- Producer surplus (PS) increases; total revenue rises.
- Government: Worse off
- Government total cost = subsidy per-unit x Qt (rectangle area in diagram).
- Opportunity cost: tax money cannot be spent on healthcare, education, infrastructure.
- Whole society: Deadweight Loss (DWL)
- Deadweight loss: welfare loss to society. Formula: DWL=triangle on the graph
- Causes overproduction: units produced between Q1 and Qt cost more to make than consumers value them. Allocative efficiency is lost.
- **Price control** **–** **Ceiling**

Price ceiling: A legal maximum on the price of a good or service, e.g. rent control

Goal: Make essential goods/services affordable for lowincome households.

-Binding constraint happens when the price ceiling is set below the current equilibrium (If price ceiling is set above the equilibrium, then nothing happens)

-Binding constraint causes:

1. A shortage of supplies, where the demand increases and supply decreases. In the long run, shortage will be larger

2. Rationing, where firms pick buyers they want to sell to, which is likely based on seller’s personal bias, or a long line might occur. As a result, the market will be inefficient and goods don’t go to the buyers who value them the most.

- **Price control** **–** **Floor**

Price Floor: A legal minimum on the price of a good or service, e.g. minimum wage

Goal: Guarantee sellers / suppliers a minimum acceptable income.

-This is the opposite with price ceiling and it is set above the current equilibrium

-Causes a surplus, supply increase, demand decrease

- **Tax**

**Indirect (perunit) Tax**

Perunit tax: fixed dollar amount for every unit sold; shifts curve vertically by tax size

- Tax placed on sellers → **supply curve shifts upward / left by full tax amount**
- Tax placed on buyers → demand curve shifts down.

**Outcome is identical regardless of who physically pays the tax to government.**

**Reasons of imposing taxes:**

- Collect revenue for public services such as schools, roads, and health care.
- Discourage consumption: raise the price of goods the government want people to buy less of (e.g. cigarettes, alcohol, fuel, sugary drinks…)
- Correcting externalities: a tax can make buyers and sellers pay for costs they impose on others
- Pc = price buyers pay (higher than original equilibrium)
- Pp = price sellers receive (lower than original equilibrium)
- Vertical wedge: Pc-Pp = tax per unit
- Qt = new traded quantity; Qt < Q1
- Government tax revenue = tax per unit x Qt (rectangle area)
- DWL triangle: between Qt and original Q1; lost gains from trade.

**5.** **Market Failure** **and** **Externalities**

**Market Failure:** free competitive market fails to reach allocative efficiency; total social surplus is NOT maximized. Causes include externalities, public goods, market power.

**Key definitions:**

MPB Marginal Private Benefit: benefit to the buyer = demand curve

MPC Marginal Private Cost: cost to the seller = supply curve

MSB Marginal Social Benefit: total benefit for whole society (including thirdparties)

MSC Marginal Social Cost: total cost for whole society (including thirdparties)

Free market equilibrium happens at MPB=MPC

Socially optimal quantity is where MSB=MSC

**Externality**: spillover cost / benefit falling on **third parties** (people who are neither buyer nor seller in transaction). Two categories: negative externality (spillover cost); positive externality (spillover benefit). Can come from production OR consumption.

Externalities are a type of market failure because free markets only consider **private costs and private benefits** (MPB and MPC). Buyers and sellers ignore spillover effects on third parties.

**Negative Externality**

1. Negative production externality (factory pollution): MSC > MPC. Vertical gap = external cost. Freemarket quantity Qm > socially optimal → **overproduction**.

- Units between Qopt and Qm: MSC>MSB, creates DWL triangle.

2. Negative consumption externality (cigarette second‑hand smoke):

MSB < MPB. Market quantity too high, DWL exists.

**Government responses for negative externality** **(trying to make MPB/MPC closer towards MSB/MSC)**:

- Corrective tax: set tax = external cost. Shifts supply up to match MSC. Eliminates DWL, quantity moves to social optimum.
- Regulation: emission rules, bans. Directly limit quantity.
- Negativeadvertising / warning labels: shift private demand (MPB) down towards MSB.
