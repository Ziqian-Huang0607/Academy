# S1M-1 Chapter 1 Elements of Pitch

> From the Grade 10 Resource Department's Semester 1 Midterm Music AP review guide (PA). Writers: Sophia Lu 10-7. Range: Chapter 1-6. Student-made reference material: where it differs from class, follow class.

Pitch and notation

Pitch describes how **high or low** a sound is.

**Middle C is C4**; the lowest C on a standard piano is C1.

A staff has five lines and four spaces. Ledger lines extend it. A clef fixes the pitches represented by the lines and spaces. The **grand staff** joins a treble staff and bass staff with a brace. **Read the clef before naming a note**, then check the key signature and any accidental.

Half steps whole steps and accidentals

A half step is the distance to the nearest piano key, white or black. A whole step equals two half steps.

- Sharp (#): raises the written note by one half step. Flat (b): lowers it by one half step.
- **Double sharp (x)**: raises it by two half steps. **Double flat (bb)**: lowers it by two half steps.
- Natural: cancels an earlier alteration.

An accidental normally applies to that staff position for the rest of the measure.

**Enharmonic equivalents** sound alike in equal temperament but have different spellings, such as F# and Gb. Their harmonic meaning can differ. B# sounds like C, and Cb sounds like B.

Building a major scale

The ascending major-scale pattern is W W H W W W H. Worked example: D major. Write D E F G A B C D first. Apply the major pattern to obtain D E F# G A B C# D. The half steps are between degrees 3–4 and 7–1; all other adjacent degrees are whole steps.

**Major key signatures and related keys**

Sharps appear in the order **F C G D A E B**. Flats appear in the reverse order **B E A D G C F**. Preserve this order when writing a key signature.

- For a s**harp major key signature**, go up a **half step from the last sharp** to find the **tonic**.
- For a **flat major key signature**, the **next-to-last flat names the tonic**. Memorize **F major** as the **one-flat exception** and **C major as the no-accidental key.**

Relative major and minor keys **share a key signature**. The **relative minor tonic is a minor third below the major tonic**, or degree 6 of the major scale. Parallel keys share a tonic but have different modes and usually different key signatures: C major and C minor are parallel; C major and A minor are relative.

Three forms of the minor scale

Natural minor lowers degrees 3, 6, and 7 of the parallel major scale. Its ascending step pattern is W H W W H W W. A natural minor is A B C D E F G A.

**Harmonic minor raises degree 7 of natural minor.** A harmonic minor is A B C D E F G# A. The raised seventh creates a leading tone a half step below the tonic. The interval from F to G# is an augmented second, not a minor third, because the letters are F and G.

**Traditional melodic minor raises degrees 6 and 7 of natural minor when ascending and restores them when descending.** A ascending melodic minor is A B C D E F# G# A; descending it is A G F E D C B A. Minor-key music may vary degrees 6 and 7 according to melodic and harmonic needs rather than rigidly following a direction rule.

Scale degree names

**1 tonic; 2 supertonic; 3 mediant; 4 subdominant; 5 dominant; 6 submediant.** **Degree 7 is the leading tone when a half step below tonic** and the subtonic when a whole step below tonic. Thus **G# is A minor’s leading tone, whereas G is its subtonic.**

Transposition procedure

To transpose, **preserve interval relationships while changing the starting pitch or key.** Identify the original key, label scale degrees, write the new key signature, and reproduce the same degrees and alterations in the new key.

Worked example: C D E G in C major uses degrees 1 2 3 5. In D major it becomes D E F# A. Transposing each letter without checking the new scale can produce the wrong quality.

**Naming and constructing intervals**

An interval is the distance between two pitches. **A harmonic interval sounds simultaneously; a melodic interval sounds successively.** Every complete interval name gives a number and a quality.

Step 1: **Count letters inclusively for the number.** C–E is a third; C–F is a fourth. Accidentals never change the numerical name.

Step 2: Determine the **quality** from the actual distance or from a major scale built on the lower note.

- **Perfect family:** unisons, fourths, fifths, octaves and their compounds.

**Major family**: seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths.

- Major made one half step smaller becomes minor; minor made one half step smaller becomes diminished. Major made one half step larger becomes augmented.
- Perfect made one half step smaller becomes diminished; perfect made one half step larger becomes augmented. There is no standard minor fifth or major fourth.

Interval inversion and consonance

Invert a simple interval by moving the lower pitch up an octave or the upper pitch down an octave. The numbers add to 9: 2 ↔ 7, 3 ↔ 6, 4 ↔ 5, and 1 ↔ 8. **Qualities change major ↔ minor and augmented ↔ diminished; perfect stays perfect.** M3 inverts to m6, and a diminished 5th inverts to an augmented 4th.

In the tonal style studied here, **major and minor thirds and sixths, perfect fifths and octaves, and unisons are consonant**. **Seconds, sevenths, and augmented or diminished intervals are dissonant.** A perfect fourth is treated as dissonant when measured above the bass; between upper parts its treatment depends on context.

**Common errors:** counting only the spaces between letters; treating enharmonic spellings as the same interval name; forgetting raised degree 7 in harmonic minor; confusing relative keys with parallel keys.
