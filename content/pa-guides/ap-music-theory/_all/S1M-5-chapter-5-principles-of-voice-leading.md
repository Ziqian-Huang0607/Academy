# S1M-5 Chapter 5 Principles of Voice Leading

> From the Grade 10 Resource Department's Semester 1 Midterm Music AP review guide (PA). Writers: Sophia Lu 10-7. Range: Chapter 1-6. Student-made reference material: where it differs from class, follow class.

Writing a clear melodic line

Use a short, singable line with a clear shape. **Keep most motion conjunct, or stepwise**. A **single focal point** is the highest note and helps organize the contour. Repeating the highest note many times can weaken that sense of direction.

- **Rhythm**: use simple durations, mostly equal to or longer than a beat. Put the final note on a strong beat.
- **Harmony**: in these early exercises, every melody note should belong to the chord harmonizing it. Nonchord tones are a later topic.
- **Contour**: prefer clear stepwise motion and purposeful changes of direction. Avoid a line that wanders without a focal point.
- **Leaps**: avoid augmented intervals, sevenths, and intervals larger than a perfect octave. A **diminished** interval may be used if **immediately followed by a step in the opposite direction**.
- For a leap larger than a perfect fourth, **usually approach and leave in the direction opposite** to the leap. Consecutive smaller leaps in the same direction should outline a triad.

Tendency tones

Scale degree **7**, when it is the leading tone a half step below tonic, **strongly tends to rise to 1**. A descending stepwise 1–7–6–5 line is an exception described in the reading. Scale degree 4 often moves down to 3, although less consistently than 7 rises to 1.

Full and reduced scores

A full score gives all or most parts separate staves. A reduced score places the parts on fewer staves at concert pitch. For SATB, soprano and alto normally share treble clef, tenor and bass share bass clef.

On each shared staff, the upper part has stems up and the lower part stems down. Therefore soprano and tenor stems point up; alto and bass stems point down. Preserve these directions even when the voices cross, so each line remains identifiable.

**Voicing a single triad**

Voicing distributes chord members among the voices and registers. The same chord can sound very different when tightly packed, widely spread, or concentrated in a low register.

First identify the required chord and bass position, then place its members in an arrangement that is readable and singable.

**Four-part writing of a triad uses four voices but only three distinct chord members, so at least one member must be doubled.** For initial complete root-position triads, doubling the root is a practical starting point; the exact choice of doubling depends on context. Keep all three members unless a later exercise permits an omission.

Close structure and open structure

The reading defines close structure as less than an octave between soprano and tenor, and open structure as an octave or more between them. This describes the span of the three upper voices together, not the distance from soprano to bass.

Voice crossing and spacing

Avoid letting any part move above the soprano or below the bass in initial exercises. Alto and tenor may cross briefly in some music for a reason, but avoiding crossing is the clearest approach in beginning work.

Keep adjacent upper parts within an octave: soprano–alto no more than P8, and alto–tenor no more than P8. Tenor–bass can exceed an octave. The upper-voice spacing rule controls the gap between neighboring parts, not the total soprano-to-tenor span.

**Do not confuse crossing with wide spacing.** Crossing changes the vertical order of voices. Excessive spacing leaves their order intact but puts adjacent upper parts too far apart.

Five relationships between two voices

- Static: neither voice moves.
- Oblique: one voice moves while the other holds its pitch.
- Contrary: both voices move in opposite directions.
- Similar: both move in the same direction by different intervals.
- Parallel: both move in the same direction by the same interval, maintaining the interval between them.

**Parallel fifths octaves and unisons**

Avoid parallel perfect fifths and octaves. Parallel unisons also weaken the independence of the parts. The issue is how the same pair of voices moves from one event to the next.

The corresponding restriction also covers consecutive fifths or octaves reached by contrary motion in the style discussed here. Check actual interval classes rather than assuming opposite directions automatically make the progression acceptable. Merely holding an octave doubling of an entire line is a different orchestration situation from independent four-part exercise writing.

Parallel thirds and sixths are commonly acceptable. Parallel fourths between upper voices can be acceptable, even though fourths are inversions of fifths. Apply the rule to the actual pair of voices and to the fourth’s relationship to the bass.

Unequal fifths

Unequal fifths alternate a perfect fifth and a diminished fifth in the same pair of voices. For these exercises, the reading permits unequal fifths except a diminished fifth moving to a perfect fifth between the bass and another voice. Thus the direction and the participation of the bass matter.

Direct or hidden fifths and octaves

A direct fifth or octave occurs when the outer voices move in similar motion into P5 or P8 and the soprano leaps. It does not require the previous interval to be perfect. Check soprano and bass together, the final interval, and whether the soprano moves by leap or step.

Review

Check in this order: key and accidentals; chord spelling; bass and inversion; voice identity and notation; crossing and adjacent upper-voice spacing; each melodic line; tendency tones; all six pairs for parallel perfect intervals; unequal fifths involving bass; outer voices for direct fifths or octaves. Correct one error, then recheck nearby connections because moving one voice can affect several pairs.
