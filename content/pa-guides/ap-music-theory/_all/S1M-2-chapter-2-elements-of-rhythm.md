# S1M-2 Chapter 2 Elements of Rhythm

> From the Grade 10 Resource Department's Semester 1 Midterm Music AP review guide (PA). Writers: Sophia Lu 10-7. Range: Chapter 1-6. Student-made reference material: where it differs from class, follow class.

Duration symbols and rests

Rhythm organizes musical events in time. A note value specifies relative duration; a rest specifies silence of the corresponding duration. **Duration is not the same as speed**: a quarter note has no fixed length in seconds until the tempo is known.

Each ordinary value divides into two of the next smaller value. One breve equals two whole notes; one whole equals two halves; one half equals two quarters; one quarter equals two eighths; one eighth equals two sixteenths. The sequence continues to thirty-second notes and beyond.

When the quarter note is the beat: whole = 4 beats; half = 2; quarter = 1; eighth = 1/2; sixteenth = 1/4. These numbers change if a different note value represents the beat.

Dots ties and beams

A dot **adds half** the original value. A second dot adds **half the first dot’s value**; a third adds **half the second dot’s value**. A single dotted note lasts 1.5 times its undotted value, and a double-dotted note lasts 1.75 times its undotted value.

- Dotted half = half + quarter = 3 quarter-note beats.
- Dotted quarter = quarter + eighth = 1.5 quarter-note beats.

A **tie** joins notes of the same pitch into one sustained sound whose duration is their sum.

For one voice on a staff, stems generally point down for notes on or above the middle line and up for notes below it. For two independent voices sharing a staff, voice identity determines stem direction: upper voice up, lower voice down.

Beat and tempo

The beat is the regular pulse; tempo is its rate. A metronome marking identifies both the beat unit and the number of those units per minute. Quarter = 72 means 72 quarter notes per minute, so each quarter lasts 60/72 seconds.

Common tempo terms, broadly slow to fast: Grave, Largo, Lento, Adagio, Andante, Moderato, Allegretto, Allegro, Vivace, Presto.

**Meter and metric accents**

Meter is the recurring organization of beats. **Duple meter has two beats, triple has three, and quadruple has four.** A measure, or bar, contains one notated grouping and ends at a bar line. The first beat normally receives the strongest metric accent.

Typical patterns: duple = strong–weak; triple = strong–weak–weak; quadruple = strong–weak–secondary strong–weak.

Simple and compound beat division

Simple beats divide into two equal parts; compound beats divide into three. Beat type concerns division within a beat, while meter type concerns how many beats occur in a measure. Combining them gives simple duple, simple triple, simple quadruple, compound duple, compound triple, and compound quadruple.

- 2/4: simple duple; 2 quarter-note beats; each beat divides into 2 eighths.
- 3/4: simple triple; 3 quarter-note beats; each beat divides into 2 eighths.
- 4/4: simple quadruple; 4 quarter-note beats; each beat divides into 2 eighths.
- 6/8: compound duple; 2 dotted-quarter beats; each divides into 3 eighths.
- 9/8: compound triple; 3 dotted-quarter beats; each divides into 3 eighths.
- 12/8: compound quadruple; 4 dotted-quarter beats; each divides into 3 eighths.

In simple signatures, the upper number normally gives the beats per measure and the lower number the note value for a beat. In compound signatures with upper numbers 6, 9 or 12, divide the upper number by 3 for the usual number of beats. The denominator names the division of the beat, not the full dotted beat.
