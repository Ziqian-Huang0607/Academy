# S1M-3 Chapter 3 Triads and Seventh Chords

> From the Grade 10 Resource Department's Semester 1 Midterm Music AP review guide (PA). Writers: Sophia Lu 10-7. Range: Chapter 1-6. Student-made reference material: where it differs from class, follow class.

Tertian harmony and triad members

Tertian chords are built by **stacking thirds**. A triad has three distinct chord members: root, third and fifth. The root is the note from which the chord is constructed; it is **not necessarily the lowest sounding note**. The bass is the lowest actual pitch in the voicing.

To identify a triad, ignore octave doublings and rearrange its letter names into a stack of thirds. E G C rearranges to C E G, so its root is C. Determine the qualities from the root and the two stacked thirds.

Five common seventh chord types

A seventh chord adds another third above a triad’s fifth. It has root, third, fifth and seventh. **Name the seventh by measuring it from the root, not from the chord’s fifth.**

- Major seventh, M7: major triad + major seventh. C E G B.
- Major-minor seventh, Mm7: major triad + minor seventh. C E G Bb.
- Minor seventh, m7: minor triad + minor seventh. C Eb G Bb.
- Half-diminished seventh, ø7: diminished triad + minor seventh. C Eb Gb Bb.
- Diminished seventh, °7: diminished triad + diminished seventh. C Eb Gb Bbb.

Chord inversions and bass position symbols

Root position means the **root is in the bass**. First inversion places the third in the bass; second inversion places the fifth there. A seventh chord also has third inversion, with the seventh in the bass. The soprano note and the distance between upper notes do not determine inversion.

Triads: root position uses no added bass-position figure, with full figured bass **5/3**; first inversion uses **6**, short for **6/3;** second inversion uses **6/4**.

Seventh chords: root position uses 7; first inversion uses 6/5; second inversion uses 4/3; third inversion uses 4/2 or 2. These slash forms represent stacked figures, not fractions.
