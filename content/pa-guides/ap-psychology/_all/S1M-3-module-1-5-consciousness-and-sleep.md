## 3.1 Consciousness and Dual Processing

Consciousness is being aware of yourself and your surroundings; it has multiple states — awake, asleep, dreaming. Cognitive neuroscience studies how brain activity connects to mental processes.

Thinking, knowing, remembering, and communicating happen on two levels at the same time: the conscious track is slow, deliberate, and step-by-step (like long division), while the unconscious track is fast, automatic, and effortless (like catching a ball).

- **Blindsight.** The eyes work but the part of the brain that makes you "aware" of seeing is damaged — a person responds to visual things without being consciously aware of them. Lawrence Weiskrantz walked a blindsight patient down a cluttered hallway; told it was empty, the patient had no idea obstacles existed — yet weaved around every one.
- **Parallel processing.** The brain handles many parts of a stimulus at once (recognizing a friend's face = color + shape + movement together).

- **Sequential processing.** Handling one part at a time, step by step — used for new or difficult problems.

## 3.2 Sleep and the Circadian Rhythm

Sleep is a natural, repeating loss of consciousness — NOT the same as a coma, general anesthesia (drugs that make you unconscious for surgery), or hibernation.

- **Circadian rhythm.** Your internal biological clock on a roughly 24-hour cycle ("about a day" in Latin), controlling sleep, body temperature, alertness, and hunger.
- **The master clock.** The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN): light → SCN decreases melatonin (the "sleepy hormone") → you wake up.

- **Desynchronization.** Internal clock vs. outside world out of sync (jet lag).

## 3.3 The Stages of Sleep

Brain wave signatures follow a sequence: beta (alert) → alpha (relaxed) → theta (Stage 1) → delta (Stage 3 deep) → REM (rapid, irregular).

![Brain wave signatures: beta, alpha, theta, delta, and REM.](/media/pa-guides/ap-psychology/s1m-fig-06.png)

*Figure 6. Brain wave signatures: beta, alpha, theta, delta, and REM.*

| **Stage** | **Brain activity** | **Key facts** |
|---|---|---|
| Awake (relaxed) | Alpha waves — slow, big waves | Drowsy, calm |
| Awake (alert) | Beta waves — fast, small waves | Thinking, focused |
| NREM-1 | Theta waves | Hallucinations (seeing/hearing things that aren't there); hypnagogic sensations = hallucinations right as you fall asleep (the falling feeling) |
| NREM-2 | Sleep spindles (short bursts of fast brain activity) | Spindles help process and store memories |
| NREM-3 (deep) | Delta waves — large, slow waves | Hardest to wake; the body restores itself |
| REM | Rapid, irregular waves (like Stage 1) BUT heart rate rises, breathing fast & irregular | Dreams happen here; paradoxical sleep (brain active, body paralyzed); REM rebound = extra REM after deprivation |

Across a night of sleep, deep sleep dominates early and REM periods grow longer toward morning.

## 3.4 Why We Sleep

Six reasons: (1) protects — kept ancestors safe from nighttime dangers; (2) restores — repairs body and brain; (3) consolidates memory — files away what you learned; (4) feeds creative thinking — the brain connects ideas; (5) supports growth — growth hormone is released; (6) conserves energy.

Sleep deprivation causes: fatigue, irritability, poor concentration, weak memory, depression, obesity, joint inflammation, slowed performance, and a weakened immune system.

## 3.5 Sleep Disorders

- **Insomnia (in- = not; somnus = sleep).** Trouble falling or staying asleep.
- **Narcolepsy (narco = sleep).** Sudden, uncontrollable sleep attacks during the day.

- **Sleep apnea (apnea = "no breathing").** Repeatedly stopping breathing during sleep.

## 3.6 Why Do We Dream? (Four Theories)

- **1. Information processing.** Dreams sort and file memories.
- **2. Physiological function.** Dreams exercise brain circuits.

- **3. Neural activation.** Random brain firing turned into stories.
- **4. Cognitive development.** Dreaming helps the brain develop.
