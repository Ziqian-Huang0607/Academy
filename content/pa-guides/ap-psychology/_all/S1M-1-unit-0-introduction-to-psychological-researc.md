## 1.1 The Scientific Attitude

Critical thinking in psychology rests on three habits working together: curiosity, skepticism, and humility. Together they let you examine assumptions, appraise sources, discern hidden biases, and weigh evidence against conclusions. Three biases constantly get in the way: hindsight bias (the "I knew it all along" effect), overconfidence in our own judgments, and the tendency to perceive order in genuinely random events.

## 1.2 Key Concepts

- **Falsifiability.** A scientific claim must be testable. The classic non-falsifiable example: the existence of God.
- **Operational definition.** A precise, measurable definition of a research procedure or concept.

- **Hypothesis vs. theory.** A hypothesis is a testable prediction; a theory is an organized explanation. A hypothesis is often implied by a theory.

## 1.3 Non-Experimental Methods

- **Case study.** In-depth analysis of one individual or group.
- **Naturalistic observation.** Observing behavior without interfering.

- **Survey.** Self-report via questions.
- **Correlational study.** Measuring the association between two variables.

**Exam note.** Know the three survey biases: wording effect, social desirability bias, and self-report bias (people report their own behavior inaccurately).

- **Random sampling.** Everyone in the population has an EQUAL CHANCE of being selected.

**Exam note.** On the exam, write "equal chance" — the phrase "random" alone does not earn the point.

Population refers to ALL those studied; the sample is the SOME who represent the population.

## 1.4 Correlation

A correlation is a linear association between two variables, summarized by the correlation coefficient r, which ranges from −1.00 to +1.00. Correlational data are displayed in scatterplots — the only graph type you need to know for the exam. Two related pitfalls: an illusory correlation is perceiving a relationship that is not actually there (similar to confirmation bias), and regression toward the mean describes how extreme scores drift toward the average on retest.

**Exam note.** r is NOT the slope. The sign of r only tells you the direction of the relationship.

**Exam note.** Illusory correlation, lurking variables, and "correlation ≠ causation" are three distinct concepts — do not blur them together.

## 1.5 Experimentation

An experiment compares an experimental group against a control group. Random assignment equalizes pre-existing differences between the groups. In a single-blind study participants are blind to the treatment; in a double-blind study participants AND researchers are blind. Key vocabulary: the placebo effect (improvement from expectation alone), confounding variables (factors that may influence the dependent variable), and experimenter bias (researcher expectations skewing results). The independent variable is what the researcher manipulates; the dependent variable is what is measured.

| **Validity** | **Reliability** |
|---|---|
| Does the experiment measure what it is supposed to measure? | Can the results be consistently replicated? |

**Exam note.** Reliability cannot be inferred from the name alone — memorize the definition: consistently replicated.

## 1.6 Other Research Methods and Ethics

- **Developmental designs.** Twin studies (identical vs. fraternal twins), cross-sectional, and longitudinal studies.
- **Data types.** Quantitative vs. qualitative (categorical) research.

- **Ethics.** Informed consent — informed ASSENT for minors — debriefing, protection from greater-than-usual harm, and confidentiality.
- **Guidelines.** Issued by the APA and BPS; animal studies are governed by common sense.

## 1.7 Statistics

Descriptive statistics measure and describe characteristics (in AP Psychology, histograms are basically bar charts). Inferential statistics ask whether results can generalize to the larger population. Better studies have representative rather than biased samples, bigger rather than smaller samples, more estimates rather than fewer, and may pool findings in a meta-analysis.

| **Mode** | **Median** | **Mean** |
|---|---|---|
| Most frequent value. Bimodal = two peaks (textbook-tested, not covered in class). | Middle value. | Average — pulled toward the tail in skewed data. |

Measures of variation include the range and the standard deviation (skip the IQR).

**Exam note.** Define standard deviation as the AVERAGE distance of all data points from the mean.

Skew: look at the tail. A tail trailing right = right (positive) skew, and the mean is HIGHEST in positive skew — the reverse for negative skew.

## 1.8 Statistical Significance

A p-value tells you how likely the result occurred by chance, assuming there is no difference in the population.

**Exam note.** In AP Psychology, p < 0.05 AND p = 0.05 both mean "no difference" (no significant effect).

Statistical significance is not the same as practical significance; the effect size tells you the strength of the relationship between two variables.
