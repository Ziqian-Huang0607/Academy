**13-1 -- 13-3 Atomic Theory,** **Temperature, Thermal Equilibrium**

- Matter is made up of atoms
- John Dalton ideated the existence of atoms 200 years ago
- Atomic and molecular masses are measured in atomic mass units
- Unified atomic mass unit (u)
- 1u = 1.6605*10-27 kg= 1/12 mass of a C-12 atom
- **Brownian motion**: shows atoms of any substance are in random motion
- In 1827 Robert Brown noticed:
- the twisted path of pollen grains in water
- smoke particles moving in the air

→ they were being pushed by the air and water molecules

→ the atoms of any substance are always in random motion

- In 1905, Albert Einstein calculated the approximate size and mass of atoms and molecules
- The diameter of a typical atom is about 10-10 m (1 angstrom)
- Intermolecular forces: forces of attraction or repulsion which act between neighboring

particles: atoms, molecules, or ions

- Both attraction and repulsion forces exist, the overall force depends on which is stronger. (electron-electron repulsion, nucleus-nucleus repulsion, electron-nucleus attraction)

**3 phases of matter**

| Crystalline solid | Strong attractive force | Atoms only move slightly, oscillating about relatively fixed positions, often in an array called a crystal lattice |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid | Weaker attractive force | Atoms/molecules move more rapidly, can change positions, free to pass over one another |
| Gas | Weak forces | Atoms move at high speeds in every direction, filling any container, occasionally colliding with one another |

**Temperature**

- Temperature: a measurement of how hot or cold something is, depends on the average kinetic energy of the particles in the object.

| Unit | Freezing point of water | Boiling point of water |
|---|---|---|
| Celsius | 0ºC | 100ºC` |
| Fahrenheit | 32ºF | 212ºF |
| Kelvin (SI unit of temperature) | 273.15 K | 373.15 K |

- 0K (-273.15 ºC) is the absolute zero temperature, the lowest possible temperature

**Conversions**

- TK = TC + 273.15
- TF = 9/5 TC + 32
- TC = (TF - 32) * 5/9
- Many properties of matter change with temperature: volume, color, and electrical resistance

**Thermal equilibrium:**

- If two bodies of different temperatures are placed close together, thermal energy will be transferred from the one with higher temperature to the one with lower temperature. The objects will eventually reach the same temperature.

**Zeroth law of Thermodynamics**:

- If 2 systems are in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other. (1931 Ralph Fowler)

Different types of thermometers:

- Liquid in glass (use “change in volume” to measure temperature)
- Coiled bimetallic strip (change in length)
- Thermistor thermometer (resistance)

Liquid in glass thermometers:

| Alcohol | expands 6 times greater than mercury | Boils easily | Used in cold places |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury | Good conductor | Freezes at cold places | Used to measure higher temperatures |

**Bimetallic strip**:

- Binds together two metals whose rates of expansion are different
- As strip is heated, strip curves
- the metal in the inside of the curve has the lower expansion rate

**13-4 Thermal Expansion**

**Thermal expansion:** Temperature of an object increases, volume increases

- when heated, particles move more → more vibrations → take up more space (particles themselves do not expand)
- does not change shape, change is proportional

**Linear expansion**:

- change in lengths of almost all solids is directly proportional to change in temperature, as long as the change in temperature is not too large
- Formula for linear expansion:

$$L={L}_{0}(1+\alpha \Delta T)$$

- L: Length after thermal expansion
- L0: Original length
- α: Coefficient of linear expansion
- Formula for volume thermal expansion:

$$V={V}_{0}(1+\beta \Delta T)$$

- V: Volume after thermal expansion
- V0: Original volume
- β: Coefficient of volume expansion
- Both formulas are accurate only if ‌ΔL & ‌ΔV are small compared to L0 and V0.‌

‌

- For solids that are **isotropic** (having the same properties in all directions): β ≈ 3α

-Water: anomalous thermal expansion between 0ºC and 4ºC

- 0 → 4ºC: volume decreases
- above 4ºC: volume increases
- water has the greatest density at 4ºC

**13-6 The Gas Laws and Absolute Temperature**

- **Pressure**: force per unit area

$$P=\frac{F}{A}$$

- Atmospheric pressure (PA):
- 1atm = 1.013 * 105 N/m2, **the average pressure of the atmosphere at sea level**
- 1 pascal (Pa): 1Pa=1N/m2
- 1 bar = 1*105 N/m2 → pressure caused by 10 meters of water on a 1 m2 area
- Gauge pressure (PG):
- the pressure above and beyond atmospheric pressure
- Absolute pressure (P):
- Atmospheric pressure + gauge pressure

**Gas Laws**

- Conditions for gas laws to be accurate (gas is close to ideal gas)
- Gas is not too dense
- Pressure is not too high
- Gas is not close to the liquefaction/boiling point (temperature not too low)

|  |  | Law holds under condition | Graph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boyle’s Law | PV = constant | mass and temperature are constant | Hyperbola (also known as isothermal curve) |
| Charles’s Law | V/T = constant | mass and pressure are constant | Linear |
| Gay-Lussac’s Law | P/T = constant | mass and volume are constant | Linear |

**(use temperature in Kelvin for calculations)**

- General gas law:

$$\frac{PV}{T}=\text{ Constant }$$

**13-7 -- 13-9 The Ideal Gas Law**

- One **mole** is defined as:
- the amount of substance that contains as many atoms or molecules as there are in 12 grams of carbon-12
- The number of molecules in a mole: Avogadro’s constant: NA = 6.02 * 1023
- **Molar mass**: number of grams per mole of a substance
- calculated by summing the atomic masses of atoms in a molecule

$$n=\frac{m}{M}$$

- n: number of moles (mol)
- m: mass (g)
- M: molar mass (g/mol)
- **Ideal Gas Law**

$$PV=\text{ nRT }$$

- Use SI unit
- R = universal gas constant (same for all gases) = **8.314J/mol*K**
- Ideal gas:
- A gas in which the molecules do not exert forces on each other except when

colliding

- Ideal gases obey the ideal gas law at all P, T, V’s
- real gases only obey it within a certain range

-At pressures about or less than 1atm (low pressure), and when T is not close

to the liquefaction point

- Ideal gas law in terms of molecules

$$PV=\text{ nRT }=N\frac{R}{{N}_{A}}T=\text{ NkT }$$

- N: number of molecules/atoms, N=n*NA
- k: Boltzmann’s constant, **k=1.38*10-23** **J/K**
- **STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure):**
- T = 273K (0 ℃)
- P = 1atm = 1.013*105 Pa

→Ideal gas at STP has volume 22.4 L/mol

- Molar volume (Vm): volume occupied by one mole of a substance at a given T and P

$${V}_{m}=\frac{M}{\rho }$$

- $\rho$: mass density (kg/m3)

**13-10 Kinetic Theory**

- Kinetic theory: the analysis of matter in terms of atoms in continuous random motion
- Postulates:
- Large amounts of molecules with mass m move in random directions with a variety of speeds
- Molecules are far apart from each other on average (average distance >> diameter)
- Potential energy (negligible) from attraction is small compared to kinetic energy
- Collisions are perfectly elastic, collisions have short time duration, their potential energy is negligible
- Internal energy of an ideal gas = total KE of gas molecules
- rms: root mean square

$${v}_{\text{ rms }}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} {{v}_{i}}^{2}}{n}}$$

- square root of the mean of the squares
- Pressure a gas exerts on the container:

$$P=\frac{1}{3} \frac{Nm{{v}_{\text{ rms }}}^{2}}{V}$$

- Average kinetic energy of molecules:

$$\overline{KE}=\frac{3}{2}kT$$

- Root mean square of velocities:

$${v}_{\text{ rms }}=\sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}}$$

## Chapter 14: Heat

## 14.1 Heat as Energy Transfer

**Heat**: the transfer of thermal energy from one object to another because of a temperature difference.

Scalar; Symbol: Q; SI unit: joule (J)

**Special units**:

**calorie (cal)**: the amount of heat necessary to raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1 ºC

**Calorie (Cal; kilocalorie)**: the heat needed to raise 1 kg of water by 1 ºC. 1 Cal = 1000 cal

**Btu (British thermal unit)**: the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 lb of water by 1 ºF. 1 Btu = 0.252 Cal = 1055 J

**Mechanical equivalent of heat**: 1 cal = 4.186 J; 1 kcal = 4.186 kJ (Joule's experiment: a given amount of work done was always equivalent to a particular amount of heat input — heat, like work, represents a transfer of energy)

**Sign convention**: Q > 0: the object absorbs energy; Q < 0: the object loses energy

## 14.2 Internal Energy

**Internal energy**: the total energy of all molecules in an object, both kinetic and potential. (Sometimes thermal energy is used to mean the same.) Symbol: **U**

Difference from temperature, heat and internal energy:

**Temperature** is defined as a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles of matter

**Heat** is the transfer of thermal energy from one object to another because of a temperature difference

Direction of heat flow depends on temperature, not on how much internal energy each object has.

**Internal energy of an ideal gas**: the internal energy of n moles of an ideal monatomic (one atom per molecule) gas equals the average kinetic energy per molecule times the total number of molecules, N (total KE of gas molecules).

Average kinetic energy of molecules: $\overline{KE}=\frac{3}{2}kT$

$$U=N\overline{KE}=\frac{3}{2}\text{ NkT }=\frac{3}{2}\text{ nRT }$$

The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature and the number of moles of gas.

Ideal gas with more than one atom per molecule: internal energy also includes rotational and vibrational energy of the molecules, so U is greater than a monatomic gas at the same T

Real gases: depends mainly on T, also somewhat on P and V (due to atomic potential energy)

Internal energy of liquids and solids is quite complicated — includes electrical potential energy associated with the forces (or "chemical" bonds) between atoms and molecules

## 14.3 Specific Heat

**Specific heat (capacity), c**: the ability to absorb heat — the amount of energy required (gained/lost) to increase/decrease the temperature of 1 kg of a material by 1 ºC.

Scalar; Symbol: c

SI unit: J/kg·ºC, J/kg·K; common unit: kcal/kg·ºC, kcal/kg·K

Example: ${c}_{\text{ water }}$ = **4186 J/kg·ºC**

The value of c depends on T and slightly on P. For temperature changes that are not too great, c can often be considered constant.

Important values (J/kg·ºC): Water — ice (-5 ºC): 2100; liquid (15 ºC): 4186. Aluminum: 900; Copper: 390; Iron or steel: 450; Glass: 840; Lead: 130; Mercury: 140; Silver: 230.

**Specific heats for gases** depend very much on how the process of changing its temperature is carried out:

${c}_{p}$: specific heat of gas kept at constant pressure

${c}_{v}$: specific heat of gas kept at constant volume

${c}_{p}$ is always greater than ${c}_{v}$ (because additional energy is used to increase the average distance / increase the potential energy between atoms/molecules); for liquids and solids this distinction is usually negligible

**Heat transfer (temperature change):** Q = mcΔT = mc(${T}_{f}$ − ${T}_{i}$)

Q: amount of heat transfer (J); m: mass (kg); c: specific heat capacity (J/kg·ºC); ΔT: temperature change (ºC or K)

## 14.4 Calorimetry

**System**: any object or set of objects that we wish to consider

**Environment / surroundings**: everything else in the universe

**Closed system**: one for which no mass enters or leaves (but energy may be exchanged with the environment)

**Open system**: mass may enter or leave, as may energy

**Isolated system**: a closed system in which no energy in any form passes across its boundary

**Calorimetry**: the quantitative measurement of heat. **Calorimeter**: a device used to measure changes in thermal energy (used to determine the specific heats of materials). A calorimeter is carefully insulated so that no heat is exchanged with the environment.

**Work principle: Conservation of thermal energy ("Method of mixtures")**: when different parts of an isolated system are at different temperatures, heat will flow from the part at higher temperature to the part at lower temperature within the system.

Heat lost = Heat gained (energy out of one part = energy into another part)

The total heat transferred into or out of the isolated system is zero: ΣQ = 0, i.e. ${m}_{A}{c}_{A}{\Delta T}_{A}$ + ${m}_{B}{c}_{B}{\Delta T}_{B}$ = 0

**Final temperature**: ${T}_{f}$ = (${m}_{A}{c}_{A}{T}_{A}$ + ${m}_{B}{c}_{B}{T}_{B}$) / (${m}_{A}{c}_{A}$ + ${m}_{B}{c}_{B}$); check: ${T}_{i}(\text{ cold })$ < ${T}_{f}$ < ${T}_{i}(\text{ hot })$

**Bomb calorimeter**: used to measure the thermal energy released when a substance burns (e.g., to determine the Calorie content of food by burning the food). ${Q}_{\text{ sample }}$ = ${Q}_{\text{ water }}$ + ${Q}_{\text{ cup }}$ + ${Q}_{\text{ bomb }}$ = (${m}_{\text{ water }}{c}_{\text{ water }}$ + ${m}_{\text{ cup }}{c}_{\text{ cup }}$ + ${m}_{\text{ bomb }}{c}_{\text{ bomb }}$)ΔT

## 14.5 Latent Heat

**Phase change**: the physical change of matter from one phase (solid, liquid, or gas) to another.

**Common phase changes**:

**Melting (fusion)**: solid → liquid; **Freezing**: liquid → solid

**Vaporization**: liquid → gas. Two kinds:

**Evaporation**: a phase change from liquid phase to gas phase that occurs at temperatures below the boiling point (a surface phenomenon)

**Boiling**: a rapid phase change from the liquid phase to gas phase that occurs at the boiling point (occurs throughout the liquid, from the bottom)

**Condensation (liquefaction)**: gas → liquid

**Sublimation**: solid → gas (e.g., dry ice, iodine)

**Deposition**: gas → solid (e.g., frost)

**Three important temperature points**:

**Melting point (**${T}_{m}$**)**: the temperature at which a substance changes from the solid phase to the liquid phase (e.g., ice: 0 ºC)

**Freezing point (**${T}_{f}$**)**: the temperature at which a substance changes from the liquid phase to the solid phase. A substance’s freezing point and melting point are the same

**Boiling point (**${T}_{b}$**)**: the temperature at which a substance changes from the liquid phase to the gas phase (e.g., water: 100 ºC)

**Conditions for a phase change to take place**: heat change (heat added or removed) AND the substance must be at a particular temperature.

*However, vaporization (specifically, evaporation) can happen at any temperature within the liquid T range

**Characteristics of phase change**:

During the phase change: the kinetic energy of the particles does not increase (but potential energy changes; PE increases when melting or boiling and decreases when freezing or condensing); there is no change in temperature

After a phase change: a change in thermal energy will result in a change in temperature

**Latent heat**: the amount of energy associated with a phase change.

**(Specific) Latent heat of fusion,** ${L}_{F}$: the heat required to change 1 kg of a substance from the solid phase into the liquid phase.

SI unit: J/kg (kJ/kg; cal/kg); e.g., ${L}_{F}$ = 3.33×10^5^ J/kg

Heat required to melt a solid: Q = m${L}_{F}$

Heat of fusion equals the heat of solidification; heat released to freeze a liquid: Q = −${mL}_{F}$ (negative sign: heat is transferred from the sample to the external world)

**(Specific) Latent heat of vaporization,** ${L}_{V}$: the heat required to change 1 kg of a substance from the liquid phase to the gas phase.

SI unit: J/kg; e.g., ${L}_{V}$ (water) = 2.26×10^6^ J/kg

Heat required to vaporize a liquid: Q = m${L}_{V}$; heat released to condense a vapor: Q = −m${L}_{V}$

**Kinetic theory of latent heats**: for a given substance, heat of vaporization is generally much greater than heat of fusion (${L}_{V}$ >> ${L}_{F}$).

Latent heat of fusion does not act to increase the average kinetic energy (temperature) of the molecules, but is used to overcome the potential energy associated with the forces between molecules (work must be done against attractive forces)

Vaporization is a more violent reorganization of molecules than melting (average distance between molecules greatly increases); for gas, intermolecular potential energy is greater than in liquid

**Evaporation**: the latent heat to change a liquid to a gas is needed not only at the boiling point — water can change from liquid to gas even at room temperature.

The heat of vaporization of water increases slightly with a decrease in temperature (${L}_{V}$(20ºC) = 2450 kJ/kg; ${L}_{V}$ (100ºC) = 2260 kJ/kg): at higher temperature, the average kinetic energy of liquid molecules is higher, so they require less energy to become gases

When water evaporates, the remaining liquid cools: the energy required comes from the water itself, so its internal energy and therefore its temperature must decrease

**Phase-change diagram (heating curve)** — segments for water (temperature vs. heat):

Solid warms: Q = m${c}_{\text{ ice }}$ΔT; slope is related to the specific heat of the substance (steeper slope → smaller specific heat capacity)

Melting at constant T: Q = m${L}_{F}$

Liquid warms: Q = m${c}_{\text{ water }}$ΔT

Vaporization at constant T: Q = m${L}_{V}$

Gas warms: Q = m${c}_{\text{ steam }}$ΔT

**Conservation of energy for isolated systems**: heat lost = heat gained; equilibrium → same final temperature.

If no phase changes occur: Q(gain) = cm(${T}_{f}$ − ${T}_{i}$); Q(lost) = cm(${T}_{i}$ − ${T}_{f}$)

If phase changes occur: determine the final state first — Q(phase change) = mL must also be considered

## 14.6 Conduction

**Conduction**: heat transfer that occurs when two materials of different temperatures are in direct contact; by adjacent molecular collisions inside a material, kinetic energy of thermal motion is transferred from faster molecules to slower molecules.

Conduction involves molecules (and/or electrons) moving only over small distances and colliding; in metals, collisions of free electrons are mainly responsible for conduction

Direction of heat flow: from hot to cold

**Conduction equation**: Q/Δt = kA(${T}_{1}$ − ${T}_{2}$)/L

Q/Δt: heat transfer rate (J/s or W)

k: **thermal conductivity** (J/s·m·K) — a measure of a material’s ability to conduct heat

A: cross-sectional area (m²); L: the distance between the two ends (m)

**Thermal conductor vs. thermal insulator**: substances for which k is large conduct heat rapidly (good thermal conductors, e.g., silver 420, copper 380, aluminum 200); poor conductors with low k are good thermal insulators (e.g., wood 0.1, fiberglass 0.048, wool 0.040, goose down 0.025, polyurethane 0.024, air 0.023 J/s·m·Cº)

**Applications of heat insulators**:

Fiberglass batt: insulation of walls and ceilings to prevent heat transfer between the inside of the building and the outside environment

Air as a thermal insulator: "double-pane windows" — an air gap reduces heat loss more than simply increasing the glass thickness, since the thermal conductivity of air is much less than that of glass

Clothes keep us warm by trapping air so it cannot move readily — it is the trapped air that insulates, not the cloth

**Vacuum flask**: a vessel with double walls, the space between which is evacuated, to further enhance thermal insulation

## 14.7 Convection

**Convection**: the process whereby heat flows by the bulk movement of molecules from one place to another via fluid flow. Convection involves the movement of large numbers of molecules over large distances.

Convection currents: heated fluid at the bottom rises because of its reduced density and is replaced by cooler fluid from above

**Types of convection**:

**Forced convection**: e.g., a forced-air furnace, in which air is heated and then blown by a fan into a room

**Natural convection**: heat transferred to a fluid raises its temperature and reduces its density, giving rise to buoyant forces that lift the fluid (due to density difference) and transport the absorbed heat to another location

## 14.8 Radiation

**Radiation**: heat transfer that occurs when microwaves, infrared radiation, visible light, or another form of electromagnetic radiation is emitted or absorbed. EM rays do not require a medium.

Any object not only emits energy by radiation but also absorbs energy radiated by other objects.

**Stefan–Boltzmann equation (radiation equation)**: Q/t = εσAT⁴

The rate at which an object radiates energy is proportional to the fourth power of the Kelvin temperature, T

σ (sigma) = **Stefan–Boltzmann constant** = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/m²·K⁴

ε (epsilon) = **emissivity**, a factor between 0 and 1, characteristic of the surface

A: surface area (m²)

Net rate of heat transfer between an object and its surroundings: Q/t = εσA(T₁⁴ − T₂⁴)
